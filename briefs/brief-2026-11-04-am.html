World escalation

Overnight drone war hits Ukraine's grid again, Komyshuvakha toll rises to 37, and Brussels talks open today

Russia launched 310 drones at Ukraine overnight into Wednesday, November 4, according to Ukraine's air force, which said it downed or suppressed 272 of them, with hits recorded at 19 locations and debris falling at 15 others. Regional officials reported energy facilities struck in at least six regions — Ternopil, Kirovohrad, Rivne, Volyn, Odesa and Zhytomyr — and President Volodymyr Zelensky said damage stretched across nine regions in all, including power generation facilities and substations, with emergency and unscheduled outages continuing in many cities and communities. In Odesa, a shopping centre and residential homes were hit, though no one was injured there, according to regional head Oleh Kiper; in Rivne, a residential building was partially destroyed and a woman was injured; in Volyn, a large part of the region lost power before settlements were reconnected by morning. Kyiv and surrounding regions reported power and water outages, and an industrial facility in Dnipro was hit, injuring two men.

The other side's account of the night looks very different, as usual. Russia's defence ministry said it shot down 709 Ukrainian drones overnight over 14 Russian regions, occupied Crimea and the Sea of Azov and the Black Sea — a figure Ukraine has not confirmed and that cannot be independently checked. What is confirmed from the Russian side is that Ukrainian drones hit a large Ozon logistics complex in Adygeysk, southern Russia, setting it on fire and forcing evacuations, with no injuries reported, according to the head of the Republic of Adygea, Murat Kumpilov. Russian officials also said eight people, including four children, were injured when Ukrainian strikes hit apartment buildings and cars on the border, and that a cargo ship carrying supplies for Ukraine's military was hit in the Black Sea. Russia's ministry said its own strikes hit power substations in the Kyiv and Zhytomyr regions, framing the campaign as continued targeting of energy infrastructure.

The human toll from Monday's strike is still rising, and that is the grimmest number in this brief. Reuters reported Wednesday that the death toll from the bombing of the aid distribution point in Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region, has risen to 37, with dozens still missing — regional official Ivan Fedorov put the missing at 59 — as rescuers continue searching the rubble of the former school. Zelensky says Russian forces first dropped aerial bombs on the school as people arrived to collect aid, then struck the shelter with more bombs. Russia's defence ministry, by contrast, maintains the attack targeted Ukrainian military units around Komyshuvakha and two other settlements, killing up to 25 servicemen — a claim the BBC previously reported it found no credible evidence for. Treat the 37 figure as the latest official count, not a final one, while the rubble is still being cleared.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the practical meaning is another winter morning of outages: the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry has accused Russia of crimes against humanity over its energy strikes, saying they are deliberate and part of a state policy of depriving civilians of essential energy — a charge Moscow rejects in its framing of the strikes as military targeting. The diplomatic clock, meanwhile, is ticking in Brussels today. The European Commission is hosting representatives from Ukraine, the United States, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, NATO and the World Bank to discuss a "prosperity plan" and security guarantees for Ukraine, building on earlier talks in Miami, with a separate NATO session on security guarantees due Thursday. Zelensky has said he wants US guarantees modelled on NATO's Article 5 and backed by a congressional vote, and has rejected a repeat of the 1994 Budapest Memorandum assurances. All of this leads to the US-brokered trilateral talks with Russia in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11, which Moscow has confirmed it will attend, with Kyiv seeking an end to energy strikes and a Black Sea ceasefire among its priorities.

In short: 310 Russian drones overnight, 272 claimed downed, power and water hit across Ukraine, the Komyshuvakha toll now 37 with dozens missing, Ukrainian drones burning an Ozon warehouse inside Russia — and today in Brussels, the allies try to agree what security guarantees are actually on offer before Abu Dhabi.

Turkey set to ratify a Saudi defence pact as Houthis claim a Riyadh airport strike and Iran says a deal is still far off

Turkey's parliament was set on Wednesday to ratify a defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan that includes a collective-defence clause, Reuters reports — committing NATO's second-largest army to Riyadh's defence as the kingdom faces attacks by Yemen's Iran-backed Houthis. The Mecca Joint Defence Agreement, signed by the three countries in August, mirrors NATO's Article 5 in treating an attack on one member as an attack on all, and plans deeper defence-industry cooperation and military coordination. Ratification alone does not send Turkish forces to Saudi Arabia: deploying personnel would need a separate parliamentary mandate, which a ruling-party lawmaker told Reuters Turkey would also submit. Defence Minister Yasar Guler has said he did not see Turkish troops engaging in combat or offensive operations against the Houthis, and officials told Reuters last month that Turkey's contribution was expected to be limited to equipment, intelligence sharing and weapons systems — though analysts told Reuters that Turkish troops and advisers could later deploy to install and operate systems sent by Ankara once a deployment mandate passes.

The pact's timing matters because the Houthi campaign has not paused. On Wednesday the Houthis said they had targeted King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, Reuters reports via Devdiscourse, with no confirmation from Saudi authorities, and the group again urged international airlines to refrain from flying over Saudi airspace. That claim sits in the same unverified category as Tuesday's rival claims — the coalition's claimed drone interception over the Mecca region, which the Houthis called a "slanderous and recycled lie," and their claimed strikes on Abha airport and Khamis Mushait air base — and it should be read that way until Riyadh confirms or denies it. What is firmer is the diplomatic backdrop: a senior Iranian official told Reuters on Tuesday that Tehran and Washington remain "far from a deal" to end their war, and Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei said the US response to Iran's seven conditions — including waiving oil sanctions, ending the naval blockade and releasing frozen funds — was "not convincing," adding that the Strait of Hormuz would only reopen if Washington fully and explicitly met them. President Donald Trump, in a Fox News interview on Tuesday, said he was ready and "locked and loaded if we need to go, but there may be no reason for it," and declined to rule out a resumption of US combat operations after the US elections.

For ordinary people in the region, the stakes are flights, fuel and shipping: Riyadh airport has partially reopened after earlier closures, Jeddah and Dammam still face delays and cancellations, and the US Mission has tightened its own travel rules for staff. A ratified pact does not end the Houthi threat, and it does not put Turkish troops on Saudi soil tomorrow — but it does give Riyadh a formal mutual-defence commitment from a major military power, which is exactly the kind of deterrent signal the pact's authors say they intend, and exactly the kind of escalation Tehran and the Houthis will read as a hardening of the Saudi position.

In short: Turkey ratifies a collective-defence pact with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan — formal obligation, no troops without a second vote — while the Houthis claim a new Riyadh airport strike Riyadh has not confirmed, and Iran says the US answer on Hormuz is still not good enough.

Sources: Reuters on Turkey's parliament set to ratify the defence pact, Reuters via Devdiscourse on the Houthis' Riyadh airport claim, Reuters via BusinessWorld on Iran being "far from a deal" and Trump's "locked and loaded" warning

Major court cases

Israel's government formation formally opens: Herzog receives certified results, says no party clearly won, and starts with the smallest parties

President Isaac Herzog received the certified results of Israel's October 27 election on Wednesday, November 4, and opened the consultations that will decide who gets the first mandate to form a government — and, in his opening remarks, said no party clearly won and urged leaders to accept that not every campaign promise can be kept. The count, certified by Central Elections Committee chair Justice Noam Sohlberg, gives Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats, Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc 48, and the Arab-led parties a record 19, in a 120-seat Knesset where 61 are needed for a majority. No candidate had been given the mandate by Wednesday evening Israel time, and Herzog has seven days from the formal announcement to assign it; the nominee then gets 28 days, extendable by 14, to form a government.

The two camps' positions are already staked out, and they do not add up to 61 on their own. Netanyahu's allies Religious Zionism and Shas have said they will recommend Netanyahu, while the five opposition leaders who met in Herzliya on Tuesday agreed all their parties will recommend Eisenkot. The Arab-led parties' 19 seats are the arithmetic key — and the political problem: a Joint List source told Fox News Digital the alliance was likely to recommend Eisenkot, while Yisrael Beiteinu leader Avigdor Liberman opposes a government dependent on Arab parties, and Netanyahu has called for a broad coalition without Arab lists after accusing Arab groupings of having "stolen" the vote. That fraud claim took a hit Wednesday: the Central Elections Committee said its review found no evidence of irregularities that compromised the election's integrity or changed its results, undercutting the allegation, according to an Associated Press report carried Wednesday. Eisenkot has said Netanyahu's camp makes the claim "because they lost."

Herzog is hearing factions in ascending order of size, a departure from custom — Religious Zionism first on Wednesday evening, followed by Shas and Yisrael Beiteinu, with the two largest parties, Likud and Eisenkot's Yashar, last on Thursday evening — and his office has not excluded a further round. For ordinary Israelis, the practical effect is continuity with uncertainty: Netanyahu remains in office while talks go on, the new Knesset is sworn in November 10, and the possible paths — an Eisenkot government with Arab support, a Netanyahu–Eisenkot unity arrangement, or failure and new elections — are all still open, with none yet chosen.

In short: certified results confirm 53–48–19 with no majority, the election committee rejects the fraud claim, Herzog opens consultations smallest-first, and the mandate decision — most likely Netanyahu or Eisenkot — is due within seven days.
