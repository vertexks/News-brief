Evening brief — Sunday, November 8, 2026

Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant loses all external power — then gets it back the same day

Europe's largest nuclear power plant spent most of Sunday running on emergency diesel generators after losing its last external power line in the early hours, the UN's nuclear watchdog said — the 27th time the plant has lost all off-site power since the war began. By evening, Ukraine's state nuclear operator Energoatom said the line had been switched back on at 17:41, reconnecting the plant to the grid.

The outage began at 02:36 on Sunday, when the Ferosplavna-1 line — a 330 kV link and the plant's only remaining grid connection — disconnected. An automatic reconnection attempt failed, leaving the plant's own systems supplied solely by backup diesel generators. The International Atomic Energy Agency confirmed the outage and said the cause and location of the disconnection were not yet known. The Russian-installed plant management said automatic protection systems had shut the line down after damage was detected, and that radiation levels on site and in the surrounding zone were normal.

All six of the plant's reactors are shut down, but the fuel in them still needs continuous cooling — and cooling needs electricity. The IAEA said the plant had enough diesel to run a limited number of emergency generators for about two weeks. Energoatom warned that operating in blackout mode "creates a direct risk of failure of life-support systems and nuclear fuel cooling in the event that diesel generators break down or exhaust their fuel reserves, which could lead to a radiation accident." IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi called for the grid connection to be restored and diesel stocks increased. The plant's other main line, Dniprovska, has been unavailable since at least October 23, and in a November 6 update the IAEA said work was still needed to reconnect it — so the site was down to a single line before Sunday's failure, and it still is.

The blackout came on top of another night of Russian strikes on Ukraine's grid. DTEK, the country's largest private energy company, said the overnight attack left more than 600,000 people without power in the Dnipropetrovsk region, and national grid operator Ukrenergo imposed emergency power cuts in Kharkiv, Sumy and Poltava. Russia's defence ministry said on Sunday it had also hit a power substation in the Zhytomyr region and three cargo vessels in the Black Sea that it claimed were supporting Ukraine's military — claims Kyiv has not confirmed. Zelenskyy has accused Moscow of intensifying the energy campaign to pressure Kyiv ahead of trilateral talks with Russia and the United States in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday and Wednesday, November 10–11. Moscow says it is striking military-industrial, energy and transport targets.

For ordinary Ukrainians the stakes are immediate: temperatures in Kyiv are near freezing, emergency and scheduled outages are running across most regions, and Kyiv's water supply was only just restored after its worst outage of the war. For the wider region, the Zaporizhzhia blackout is the sharper warning — a plant with no grid connection and a two-week diesel clock, in a war zone, for the 27th time.

In short: Zaporizhzhia lost its last power line early Sunday and ran on diesel generators for most of the day before power was restored in the evening — but with its backup line still down, the plant remains one failure from another blackout, while Russian strikes left 600,000+ people without power in Dnipropetrovsk alone, two days before the Abu Dhabi talks.

Trump says new Iran strikes "highly unlikely" if talks progress — but names 38 bridges as targets

President Trump told CNN on Sunday that he is "highly unlikely" to order fresh attacks on Iran as long as talks to end the conflict keep progressing — the clearest signal yet that the post-midterm escalation he had hinted at may not come. "It depends on whether or not they sign a very strong agreement," he said in a phone interview with CNN's Jim Sciutto. "The answer is, highly unlikely it's going to be necessary."

The reassurance came with a threat attached. Trump said that if strikes do resume, the US has already identified potential infrastructure targets, including 38 bridges "that we can knock down in literally one hour." He also said Iran was "withering away" financially and militarily, and that US forces in the region are prepared to strike at any moment. "I would, if necessary," he said.

Tehran's response on Sunday was to press its own case. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the midterm results should push the White House to accept Iran's two-day proposal to end the war — under which Washington would lift its blockade of Iranian ports, agree a ceasefire on all fronts and unfreeze Iranian assets, and Iran would reopen the Strait of Hormuz within two days. Araghchi has spoken to 12 foreign ministers in 48 hours, and Iran submitted a nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatar on Thursday. The seven conditions Tehran has set — sanctions relief, frozen assets, war reparations and an end to the blockade among them — still stand, and Iran insists the strait stays closed until they are met.

There was no sign of a new large-scale US operation on Sunday, and no new strikes were reported. Saturday's mystery explosions on Qeshm Island and along Iran's southern coast remain unattributed: Iranian state television suggested they were related to Iranian military activity in the waterway rather than US strikes, and neither side has confirmed any missile strikes on Iranian territory. Both sides did keep hitting shipping over the weekend — Iran's Revolutionary Guards said two tankers caught fire after hitting mines on an "unauthorised route" and a third tanker exploded, while the US said its air force disabled a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman. Reuters could not confirm any of the reported strikes. Qatar, which is mediating, denied on Saturday that talks had collapsed and said "significant progress" had been made.

The two sides are still far apart on sequencing: Washington insists nuclear issues must feature in the first talks, while Tehran says it will not negotiate on its atomic programme until its core conditions are addressed. The next watch point is Monday, November 9 — the new moon, a dark night favourable for military operations — and the US carrier USS Theodore Roosevelt is not expected in the region before mid-November.

For the world economy, the shipping war is the part that bites: UK maritime monitors said at least 11 ships were attacked in and around the strait over the preceding week, with three sinking, and flows through Hormuz are running at under 60% of capacity. Brent settled around $98 on Friday after a roughly 12% weekly drop.

In short: Trump publicly took a major new Iran campaign off the table — for now, and conditional on a deal — while naming 38 bridges the US could hit if talks fail; Iran is pushing its two-day reopening proposal, Qatar says mediation is alive, and Monday's new moon is the next moment to watch.

Houthi/Saudi — updates

Arab states condemn Riyadh attacks; Saudi schools reopen

Syria condemned the Houthi attacks on Riyadh's King Abdullah Financial District and an Aramco facility on Sunday, pledging "full support for Saudi Arabia" — four days after Syria and Saudi Arabia signed a bilateral defence pact. Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Egypt issued parallel condemnations. The Saudi-led coalition said it had destroyed two ballistic missile launchers in Saada used in the Sunday attack on Riyadh, and Saudi schools returned to in-person classes on Sunday after weeks of remote learning because of the airstrikes. The Houthis said the coalition carried out 105 airstrikes in the last 24 hours, concentrated around Sanaa — that figure is single-source and unverified. (Byte EU · SRN News · Demócrata)

Krakow — updates

Prosecutors preparing murder charges; suspect still critical

Prosecutors are preparing six murder charges against Michał O., 24, over the six people killed in Krakow's Main Market Square on Saturday, with the figure able to rise to nine if the deaths of his parents and sister at the family home are added. The suspect underwent several operations overnight and remains in critical condition, so he has not yet been formally detained or questioned because of his medical condition. Prosecutors formally opened a terrorism investigation on Sunday. Poland is in national mourning today and Monday; the next test comes Wednesday, Independence Day, when the annual march traditionally brings the radical right onto the streets. (EuropeSays · Le Monde)

Cybersecurity

Norway's aid agency breached — all 20,614 portal users exposed

Norway's Agency for Development Cooperation (Norad) disclosed that attackers may have accessed the names and email addresses of all 20,614 registered users of its Grants Portal, which serves 12,511 aid organisations worldwide. The attackers got in on October 30 by abusing 27 compromised user accounts, and also stole grant-application information from at least three organisations. Norad says it cannot rule out that the attackers saw the names of applicants operating in conflict zones and authoritarian states — the most serious part of this breach. The portal was shut down immediately; Norway's Data Protection Authority, National Security Authority and police have been notified. Why it matters: this is a registry of aid workers, some in dangerous places, and the attacker is still unknown. (Norad · The Register)

Microsoft Paint has an unauthenticated RCE — update via the Store, not Windows Update

A newly disclosed unauthenticated remote code execution flaw in Windows Paint (CVE-2026-83844) is fixed via a Microsoft Store update, not a standard Windows Update — so it is easy to miss. (PwnSpectrum)

KEV status: no new CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities additions on Sunday. The catalog stands at 1,769 entries; the SonicWall SSLVPN flaw (CVE-2026-19868) federal deadline is November 20. Japan approved a ¥10 billion (about $63 million) emergency cybersecurity package this week. (TheCyberMind weekly brief)

Courts

Eisenkot formally gets the mandate; Ra'am will back him

Gadi Eisenkot formally received the mandate to form Israel's next government from President Isaac Herzog at a ceremony at the President's Residence on Sunday at 5 p.m. He has 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension, to build a coalition needing 61 seats. Haaretz reported that the Arab party Ra'am will support an Eisenkot-led government, while the Joint List will not vote for it but will not seek to topple it — likely abstaining. The Knesset is sworn in on Tuesday, November 10. (New Kerala · Indian Economic Observer)

No fresh developments: Russell Brand's sentencing is Monday, November 9; FTC v. Amazon, the Netanyahu trial and the Murdaugh retrial (next hearing November 13) are all quiet.
