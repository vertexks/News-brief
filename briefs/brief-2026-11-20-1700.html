Nvidia craters 14% as the SEC opens an accounting probe — and the AI selloff spreads

Nvidia's stock fell 14% on Friday after Reuters reported that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission is probing the company's accounting practices, capping a week in which the chipmaker lost nearly 23% of its value and more than 47% from its early-October peak. It was the seventh straight weekly decline — a stretch not seen since July 2008 — and the seventh straight losing session, over which Nvidia has shed roughly $1.16 trillion in market value. Shares touched a 52-week low of $126.64 during the session, and the company's market cap now sits at about $3.03 trillion, below Google parent Alphabet's $3.24 trillion — making Nvidia the fourth-largest U.S. company, behind Alphabet for the first time since April 2024.

According to MarketWatch, the SEC probe was reported by Reuters on Friday afternoon and centers on potential accounting issues, with renewed scrutiny of Nvidia's Singapore revenue reporting. The same day, the New York Times reported that Alice Huang — the former executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed with whom Jensen Huang previously admitted an extramarital affair, and who U.S. officials suspect was a Chinese intelligence asset — has ties through "family and associates" to China's defense industry. The board's investigation into whether Huang compromised national security or revealed corporate secrets is the backdrop to all of it: Huang resigned as CEO on November 12, CFO Colette Kress is interim chief, the board says it does not intend to reinstate him, and the search firm Heidrick & Struggles has approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas about the permanent job, Bloomberg reported. President Donald Trump weighed in at a Friday news conference, saying he would "like to see Jensen make a big apology and go back" to Nvidia, adding: "We have to protect our brilliant people. Jensen is one of them."

The two sides of the market argument are now starkly split. On one side, Nvidia's business itself just delivered blockbuster numbers — $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue on Tuesday, up 103% year over year, with $130 billion in fourth-quarter guidance, both above expectations — and some analysts think the selling is overdone: Seaport Research's Jay Goldberg told MarketWatch he expects things to stabilize next week, though "there is no one in a hurry to buy it until we know who the next CEO is." On the other side, fund managers who held Nvidia as a "safety stock" are rotating out, Goldberg said, and Catalyst Funds' David Miller called the stock "vulnerable" to more selling, saying the length of the losing streak "tells us very little about where the bottom is" and that he would want "greater clarity on permanent leadership and the investigations, along with continued execution on customer demand and product deliveries, before becoming confident in a sustained recovery." MarketWatch also notes the stock was already under pressure before Huang's exit, with investors questioning whether AI demand can sustain current spending.

The damage spread well beyond Nvidia on Friday. Anthropic, which debuted on the Nasdaq only the day before, fell 8.5% to close at $88.91 — after touching $83.05 intraday, just five cents above its $83 IPO price — giving back most of its 17% first-day gain. The Nasdaq tumbled 5.3% on Friday and 10% for the week, its biggest five-day percentage decline since March 2020 and its worst points decline on record, while AMD fell 11% to $433.50. For ordinary investors, the stakes are direct: Nvidia made up 6.14% of the largest S&P 500 index fund as of November 12, so the slide is hitting retirement accounts and index portfolios, not just traders — and the same AI-valuation doubts dragging Nvidia down are now washing over the whole sector, including a stock that raised a record $81 billion barely 24 hours earlier.

In short: a reported SEC accounting probe turned Nvidia's bad week into a rout — down 14% Friday, down 23% on the week, at a 52-week low and below Alphabet in market value — while Trump publicly urged Huang to apologize and return, the CEO search drags on, and the AI selloff pulled Anthropic and the Nasdaq down with it. Watch for Huang's transcribed interview before three House committees on December 1, any formal SEC statement, and whether a permanent CEO is named.

Poland asks NATO for reinforcements after Russian missiles cross its border — and NATO's chief calls it a "grave violation"

Poland will formally ask NATO for more troops and surveillance aircraft for the alliance's eastern flank after two Russian missiles and a jet-powered drone crossed into its airspace during Friday's massive attack on Ukraine, Defence Minister Władysław Kosiniak-Kamysz said. NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte called the incursion a "grave violation" of Polish airspace, said he had spoken with Prime Minister Donald Tusk, and is expected to travel to Poland this weekend. Warsaw also plans to seek an extraordinary meeting of NATO member states.

According to the Associated Press, one missile — identified as a Russian Kh-101 cruise missile — came down at Nowy Lipowiec, about 50 km from the Ukrainian border and roughly 35 km from where European troops were conducting exercises; a second likely missile fell at Rudnik nad Sanem, and the probable jet-powered drone at Mołodycz. Poland's military said 11 objects threatened its airspace and three crossed, all travelling faster than 800 km/h. The Kh-101 spent about three minutes over Poland. Polish F-16s and Swedish Gripen fighters were airborne, Patriot batteries were activated around Rzeszów-Jasionka airport and Świdnik, and two NATO AWACS aircraft were operating over Poland — but nobody fired: a Swedish pilot could not get visual identification in poor weather, and the firing chain could not positively identify the fast-moving tracks in the time available. Rzeszów airport suspended operations and rail traffic was halted in the Subcarpathian region for about an hour. Nobody in Poland was hurt and no infrastructure was destroyed, and Moscow has not commented.

The two sides are reading the incident in opposite ways. Tusk called it an "intentional provocation," saying "we know that what landed on Polish soil came from Russia" and that it "does not look like an accident" — and pointed to the timing: the incursions happened while thousands of troops from 11 European countries were taking part in the KOALICJA 26.2 exercise in southeastern Poland, rehearsing a possible postwar stabilization mission in Ukraine. Russia has said nothing about the Polish incident, but on Friday Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov said even a quasi-NATO membership for Ukraine would be unacceptable, and Putin has said European troops deployed to Ukraine after a peace deal would be legitimate targets. European leaders condemned the incursion — Britain's Prime Minister Andy Burnham called it "utterly unacceptable," European Council President António Costa said Russia "continues to spread (its) threats" — while the UK's chief of defence intelligence, Lt. Gen. Matthew Jones, said he was confident Russia "does not actively seek war with NATO," but that his greatest fear "is the risk of a significant miscalculation," warning there have been "too many recent examples of reckless Russian behavior where, if the incident had been slightly different, the results could have been extremely serious."

For people in southeastern Poland, the war is no longer something across the border: alarms sounded in border towns after 6:30 a.m., an airport shut down, trains stopped, and explosive-ordnance teams were deployed to villages where missile fragments fell in fields and forests. It is the second Russian missile to come down in Biłgoraj County in under four months, and the episode forced NATO pilots to make a shoot-or-hold decision in about three minutes, in bad weather, against objects they could not positively identify — exactly the kind of split-second judgment where a mistake could kill civilians or trigger a wider war.

In short: Poland is treating Friday's incursion as a deliberate Russian probe timed to NATO exercises, and is asking the alliance for more forces and surveillance — while NATO's chief calls it a grave violation and heads to Warsaw, and allied commanders warn the real danger is miscalculation, not intent. Watch for Rutte's visit this weekend, whether NATO agrees to reinforce Operation Eastern Sentry, and whether Poland invokes Article 4 consultations.

Judge refuses to dismiss Alex Murdaugh's murder indictments — retrial stays on for April 2027

A South Carolina judge on Friday refused to dismiss the original murder indictments against Alex Murdaugh, clearing the way for his retrial in the 2021 killings of his wife and son. Judge Debra McCaslin issued an 11-page order denying Murdaugh's motion to dismiss the murder and weapons charges, after hearing nearly two hours of arguments at a pretrial hearing in Lexington on November 13. The retrial is now set to begin the week of April 5, 2027, in Sumter County, before McCaslin.

Murdaugh's defense argued that the lead state investigator, South Carolina Law Enforcement Division agent David Owen, gave false or misleading testimony to a Colleton County grand jury in 2022 in order to secure the indictments — specifically about human blood spatter on a T-shirt Murdaugh was wearing the night of the killings, and about shotgun ammunition patterns matching one of the missing murder weapons. Prosecutors countered that the remaining evidence established probable cause regardless, and said they were prepared to seek a fresh set of indictments as soon as next month if the defense had succeeded. McCaslin sided with the state: she wrote that Murdaugh "has not established intentional prosecutorial or investigative misconduct that compromised the integrity of the grand jury proceedings," and that "the remaining unchallenged evidence overwhelmingly established probable cause." She added that dismissal of an indictment over alleged grand-jury misconduct is "an extraordinary remedy reserved for exceptional circumstances."

The ruling lands in a case that has already been through one full trial and a reversal. Murdaugh was convicted of both murders in 2023 and sentenced to life, but the South Carolina Supreme Court overturned the convictions earlier this year, citing "shocking jury interference" by the court clerk in the Colleton County trial. The defense's strategy at the retrial has been to attack the investigation itself — including claims that officers mishandled the murder weapons — while prosecutors have fought to keep the original indictments and the evidence behind them intact. The next milestone is January 20, 2027, the deadline for both sides to agree on a juror questionnaire to be mailed to potential Sumter County jurors.

For the families, the ruling means the case finally has a firm path forward after months of pretrial fights over DNA testing, ballistics data and the grand jury record — but also that the retrial, now more than five years after Maggie and Paul Murdaugh were killed at the family's Moselle property, is still nearly five months away.

In short: Judge McCaslin found the alleged grand-jury errors did not amount to bad-faith misconduct and did not undercut probable cause, so the original murder indictments stand and the April 5, 2027 retrial in Sumter County goes ahead. Watch for the January 20 juror-questionnaire deadline and any further pretrial rulings on evidence.

Ukraine: death toll from the overnight barrage rose to nine; one Kyiv bridge reopened

The death toll from Friday's overnight Russian barrage rose to nine, Ukrainian officials said — seven killed in Kyiv and two in Lviv, with at least 28 injured — and the Podilsko-Voskresenskyi bridge in Kyiv reopened to traffic around 3 p.m. local time, while damage assessments continue on the Darnytskyi bridge and the Pivdennyi bridge remains partially restricted. Strikes on power plants, substations and transmission lines forced Ukraine's three operational nuclear plants to reduce generation, bringing emergency outages in 11 regions. UNICEF said at least 1,144 children were killed or injured in Ukraine between January and October 2026, the highest verified number in three years. Ukraine also said it has deployed an experimental laser air-defense system in Kyiv, and its Unmanned Systems Forces said they have hit 40 Russian oil facilities and defence-industry targets over the past 50 days, including the Dorogobuzh chemical plant in Russia's Smolensk region, struck on November 10 and again on November 20.

Saudi defences shot down two more Houthi missiles aimed at Jazan on Friday evening

The Saudi-led coalition said its air defences intercepted and destroyed two ballistic missiles launched by Yemen's Houthis toward the southern Jazan province on Friday evening, with debris falling in the al-Shuqaiq area, where the Houthis had earlier said they would target a power station. The evening intercept came hours after a morning wave — one ballistic missile and two drones toward the south — that was already reported this morning. No injuries or damage were reported from either wave. Jazan is home to a 400,000-barrel-per-day Aramco refinery on the Red Sea coast.

*Sources: Devdiscourse (Anadolu), Global Arab Network*

Iran/US: Senate rejected a war-powers measure, and Trump says he is "not rushing" a decision

The U.S. Senate voted 48 to 51 on Thursday against a House-adopted resolution to remove American forces from hostilities with Iran, CBS News reported — the latest failed bid to limit the president's war powers. Trump told Israel's Channel 12 he is "not rushing to make any decisions on Iran" and will "see what happens after the new year starts." The Pentagon's deputy comptroller told lawmakers the war has now cost the U.S. military more than $48 billion, up from $47.1 billion in mid-October. No Iranian fire on a Gulf host and no U.S. strike on Iran has been reported since November 18 — the exchange is paused, not settled, and a major U.S. or joint U.S.–Israeli air campaign before early December is now seen as unlikely, though the option stays open.

Apple is reportedly building its own autonomous AI agent — from job listings, not an announcement

Apple has posted job listings this week for engineers to build an autonomous AI agent that can browse the web, use apps, run commands, write and fix code, and control devices on a user's behalf, Bloomberg reported on November 19. One listing, for a "Senior Engineering Manager, Agentic AI – Proactive," says Apple is building agents that work the way people do — moving between a browser, a document, a terminal, an editor and a service — with permission models and sandboxing as design priorities. The project is in its early stages and could launch as early as late 2027, though Apple has not confirmed a timeline. This is a report based on hiring, not an Apple announcement — treat it as an early signal, not a product. The same week, Apple seeded second public betas of iOS 27.3 and macOS 27.3, which make it harder for third-party agents to get control of a Mac.
