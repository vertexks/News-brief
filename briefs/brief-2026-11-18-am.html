Morning Brief — Wednesday, November 18, 2026

Kyiv is running on four hours of power a day as Russia's grid campaign grinds on

Russia launched another 141 long-range drones at Ukraine overnight, and the country's energy minister says Moscow has now hit Ukrainian energy facilities more than 1,000 times since September. In Kyiv, the power crisis has become the war's civilian front line: many homes get electricity for only around four hours a day, some have gone 37 hours straight without it, and the mayor is now in a public fight with the president over who failed to prepare for winter.

Ukraine's Air Force said it intercepted 126 of the 141 drones launched overnight, including 59 jet-powered ones, with impacts recorded at seven locations. Local authorities reported five people killed and 15 wounded across the country over the past day — four in Donetsk region, one in Kharkiv, six injured in Dnipro including a 15-year-old boy, and three more in Sumy. Energy infrastructure was damaged in Kyiv, Mykolaiv and Rivne regions; a strike in Rivne cut power to 80 settlements. President Zelenskyy said the overnight attacks damaged power generation facilities and substations in nine regions, leaving emergency outages in many cities. Russia's Defence Ministry confirmed it hit two power stations in Kyiv and Zhytomyr regions.

The Guardian reports from Kyiv that the outages are now shaping daily life and politics. Residents in the capital are being urged to use public drinking-water fountains, and emergency centres offering hot tea and charging points have been set up in the worst-hit areas. Maksym Bakhmatov, head of Kyiv's Desnianskyi district — home to 600,000 people — blamed the crisis first on Putin and second on Mayor Vitali Klitschko's management, saying the city built no backup generation and has "almost a collapse." Zelenskyy publicly blamed Klitschko last week for failing to prepare for the heating season; Klitschko shot back that the central government was trying to "get rid of Klitschko by any means necessary" and was "in fact destroying Kyiv." About 500 people protested outside City Hall on October 17 demanding the mayor's resignation over winter readiness and shelter shortages.

What each side says: Ukraine and its allies describe a deliberate campaign against civilian energy — the Guardian notes it previously reported on intercepted Russian plans to make Kyiv uninhabitable by cutting electricity, heating, water and even sewage, and the US-based Institute for the Study of War said in a November 7 report that the intercepted plan confirms the systematic nature of the attacks. Russia presents the strikes as hitting energy infrastructure that supports Ukraine's war effort and has not acknowledged targeting civilians. Meanwhile, Ukrainian strikes overnight hit Russian energy infrastructure too, leaving parts of occupied Crimea on outage schedules, according to Russia's Energy Ministry.

For ordinary people, this is now a winter-survival problem, not just a battlefield one. Kyiv is heading into freezing temperatures with a grid that is being hit nightly; Kharkiv's mayor told the Guardian his city faces a "catastrophic" funding shortage for heating and is trying to build decentralised "energy islands" — small fuel-powered generators and modular boilers — but called it "very costly and time-consuming." In Kyiv, Bakhmatov said his district of 600,000 has no workable evacuation plan: "We need help with the generators, with food, with power stations, and so on. Just help us."

In short: Russia's nightly drone campaign is steadily degrading Ukraine's grid — over 1,000 energy strikes since September — and Kyiv is now rationing power to hours a day while its leaders argue over who is to blame. Watch whether the grid holds as temperatures drop, and whether the political fight in Kyiv escalates.

The US-Iran exchange widens: Qeshm strike answered by drones over Bahrain and Kuwait, tankers still burning

The US and Iran traded fire again on Tuesday, and the exchange kept spreading overnight: after American jets hit an Iranian missile site on Qeshm Island, Iran sent drones at US forces in Bahrain and — for the first time in about two and a half months — Kuwait. In the Strait of Hormuz, more tankers caught fire, one crew member is missing, and a third US aircraft carrier has arrived in the Gulf.

A US military official told Reuters and AFP that American fighter jets used precision munitions to strike an Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps missile launch site on Qeshm Island, describing it as self-defence in response to Iranian aggression against US personnel and assets. The strike came hours after the IRGC said its drones carried out a "precision operation" against a US-operated radar at Sheikh Isa Air Base in Bahrain — the third Iranian attack on Bahrain in five days. Bahrain said it intercepted the drones with no casualties or damage. Overnight into Wednesday, Bahrain's defence force said it intercepted a further round of Iranian aerial attacks, and Kuwait's military said its air defences were responding to hostile drone attacks around 1:39 a.m. Gulf time. Iran's army then said its drones had targeted Patriot air defence systems and drone and missile launch platforms at Ali Al-Salem Air Base in Kuwait. Kuwait has not named the origin of the drones and reported no casualties or damage.

At sea, the IRGC navy claimed two "violating" vessels — a supertanker carrying more than 2 million barrels of crude and an LNG carrier — exploded and caught fire after hitting naval mines, and warned ships using the southern route faced "a wall of iron and fire" of mines. Independent trackers reported a third vessel hit, and Iran's army separately reported a fourth tanker damaged east of the strait. None of the mine claims has been independently verified, and the US military says it has cleared the main routes. What is confirmed: the Panama-flagged LPG carrier Tara Gas was struck twice off Oman, suffering an engine-room fire; India's shipping minister said 24 crew were accounted for but one Indian sailor is missing. The Panama-flagged On Phoenix was also hit by an unknown projectile off Oman on Monday. Region Alert, citing ADNOC, reported an ADNOC Logistics vessel was attacked on November 17 with 11 crew injured and one missing at sea. The USS Theodore Roosevelt arrived in the region on Monday, joining the USS George H.W. Bush and USS George Washington — three US carrier strike groups near Iran.

What each side says: Washington describes its strikes along Iran's southern coast as limited and defensive, and CNN noted the Qeshm strike resembled others US officials have also called defensive rather than a significant escalation. Tehran says its drone attacks answer repeated US drone violations of Iranian airspace, and its foreign ministry has rejected as "rubbish" a CNN report — citing seven US officials — that Iran is developing plans to target senior Trump administration officials in Washington with a dirty bomb or chemical weapon; the White House says there are no credible threats to the homeland. Trump has said further strikes on Iran are "quite unlikely" and the military part of the war is "effectively over." Diplomacy is still running in parallel: Qatar's foreign ministry says mediation continues after a meeting with the Iranian side on Tuesday, and Iran's foreign minister met Oman's foreign minister and Sultan Haitham in Muscat to discuss maritime corridors and preventing escalation.

For ordinary people, the costs are stacking up in the strait and at the pump. Brent crude was around $92.76 a barrel on Wednesday morning per Reuters — up slightly on the day but well below April's wartime peak of $126.41 — while shipping through the strait, which carried about a fifth of the world's traded oil and gas in peacetime, remains partly blocked and heavily escorted. Seafarers are paying the sharpest price: one crew member missing from the Tara Gas, another reported missing from an ADNOC vessel, and crews evacuating burning ships. Iraq has resumed pumping oil to Turkey by pipeline at 120,000 barrels a day, rising to 500,000, to avoid Hormuz altogether.

In short: the tit-for-tat is no longer confined to one country — US strikes on Qeshm drew Iranian drone attacks on both Bahrain and Kuwait, tankers keep burning in the strait, and three US carriers are now in the region. Watch whether Kuwait confirms and attributes the overnight drone attack, whether US Central Command details the Qeshm strike, and whether Thursday's Trump-Xi-Putin trilateral produces any pressure to step back.

Coalition hits 60 Houthi targets as the Houthis close on Aden — and Yemen's government brands them terrorists

The Saudi-led coalition carried out a "precision operation" against 60 Houthi military targets on Tuesday, as the Houthis press their advance toward Aden and Yemen's internationally recognised government formally designated the group a terrorist organisation.

Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki said on Wednesday morning that the strikes hit Sanaa, Hodeidah, Amran and Saada governorates and destroyed tunnels the Houthis used as workshops and storage for ballistic missiles. The operation came during the Houthis' advance toward Aden, the government's temporary capital, after government-aligned forces withdrew from Dhubab airport and territory around the Bab al-Mandeb strait to defend the city. The Houthis are now reported to be about 60 kilometres from Aden. The coalition also said it intercepted two Houthi cruise missiles launched toward Riyadh on Wednesday; the Houthis' military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group targeted oil pumping stations near Riyadh and a radar installation in southern Saudi Arabia, which Saudi authorities have not confirmed. Alerts sounded in Jizan, Najran and Khamis Mushait on Wednesday before the danger passed.

In Taiz, fighting has resumed in Tur al-Bahah in Lahij province, and Al Jazeera's correspondent there reports "fierce battles" between government forces and the Houthis. Local authorities accuse the Houthis of shelling civilians and residential neighbourhoods; villages in Jabal Sabr are described as under complete siege, with daily sniper attacks targeting civilians, primarily children and women. Taiz authorities also accuse the Houthis of blocking medicine shipments coming from Aden and confiscating medical supplies, and have called on the United Nations to intervene, saying the blockade endangers "the lives of thousands of patients." Separately, Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council chairman issued Law 1/2026, published by the SABA news agency on November 18, designating the Houthis a terrorist organisation, with criminal provisions and restrictions on financing.

What each side says: the coalition frames its strikes as precision attacks on Houthi missile capability, and the Yemeni army claims hundreds of operations against Houthi positions. The Houthis present their attacks as strikes on Saudi military, civilian and oil infrastructure, and say the Bab al-Mandeb shipping lanes are "safer now" in their hands. The advance has also revived Yemen's southern separatists: STC leader Aidarous al-Zubaidi has launched a mobilisation campaign to defend Aden, Lahij and al-Dhalea, and one STC representative told Al Jazeera that "in January, the most effective anti-Houthi force in Yemen was taken apart by decisions in Riyadh, not by the Houthis" — a reference to the Saudi-backed campaign that weakened the separatists earlier this year. Analysts are split on whether the STC can hold Aden, with one expert warning Houthi capabilities exceed those of the government and the STC combined.

For ordinary people, the squeeze is worst in Taiz, where a siege is cutting off medicine as well as movement, and in the south, where the International Organization for Migration counts 348,000 people displaced since August 1 as the front line moves toward Aden. If the Houthis take the Bab al-Mandeb coast in force, shipping through the strait — normally about 12% of global trade — faces a new threat, and Saudi Arabia's overland oil alternative, the East-West Pipeline to Yanbu, has already been hit by drones before.

In short: the coalition is hitting back hard — 60 targets in a day — but the Houthis are still advancing on Aden, Yemen's government has now formally labelled them terrorists, and Taiz is under siege with medicine blocked. Watch whether the Houthis push the last 60 kilometres to Aden and whether the STC's mobilisation holds the southern front.

Nvidia's first earnings without Huang were a blowout — and the stock still can't shake the scandal

Nvidia's first quarterly report since Jensen Huang resigned blew past Wall Street's expectations, and the board used the earnings call to rule out his return and announce a $150 billion share buyback. The shares jumped about 5% after hours and were up roughly 3% before Wednesday's open — but they remain about 35% below their October record, and the market's reaction was notably cooler than the numbers.

Revenue for the quarter ended October 25 was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and well above the roughly $109.1 billion analysts expected, with adjusted earnings of $2.68 a share against forecasts of about $2.47. Data centre revenue hit $107.6 billion, up 110% year over year, and net income reached $65.3 billion. Guidance for the current quarter was $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, ahead of the roughly $125.1 billion consensus, and interim CEO Colette Kress raised the fiscal 2028 revenue growth outlook to about 75% from 70%. Kress said the new Vera Rubin platform generated "nearly $20 billion" in its first quarter of production shipments — the fastest product ramp in Nvidia's history, according to the company. The board also approved an additional $150 billion in share repurchases, bringing total remaining buyback capacity to $227.3 billion, after returning $28.1 billion to shareholders in the quarter.

The leadership question dominated the call as much as the numbers. Board chair Harvey Jones opened by saying the board "unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO," and declined to take questions about the internal investigation. Jones said Huang resigned after disclosing personal conduct "inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards and cultural norms." The board has hired Heidrick & Struggles to find a permanent CEO and created a board committee on national security and export control oversight. Huang said on Tuesday he would be "honoured" to return if the board judged it in shareholders' interest, and said "I own my mistakes completely." He is due to appear before congressional committees on December 1, followed by Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter on December 2, according to AP. Meanwhile Washington tightened the screws further: the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on Monday requiring data centre operators in Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand to get a US government licence before exporting advanced AI chips — aimed at stopping Nvidia chips being rented to Chinese companies through overseas data centres. Nvidia earned $2.4 billion from licensed chip sales to China in the quarter and has now ceased those shipments.

What each side says: bulls point to the numbers — demand held up without Huang, guidance went up, and one analyst told CNN the results show "an orderly CEO transition does not necessarily have to dilute the company's value." The sceptics note the stock is still down about 17% for the year and roughly $2 trillion of market value has been erased since the October 6 record of $243.37, and as one fund manager told Reuters, "the story here is very complicated, and it's hard not to factor that in as an additional risk." Gross margin is also set to narrow to 71–72% in the current quarter from 74.8%, as the newest products ramp.

For ordinary people, this is the bellwether for the whole AI trade: Nvidia's stock had its sixth straight weekly decline heading into the report, its longest losing streak, and the Nasdaq fell into correction on Monday. A clean beat that only buys a 3–5% bounce tells investors the market is pricing the unresolved scandal, not just the chips. The next test comes tonight, when Anthropic prices its IPO ahead of Thursday's Nasdaq debut.

In short: Nvidia proved the business still works without Huang — $115.8 billion in revenue, a raised outlook and a $150 billion buyback — but the board has closed the door on his return, Washington is tightening chip curbs, and the stock is still roughly a third below its peak. Watch Wednesday's regular session for whether the bounce holds, and Huang's congressional appearance on December 1.

Trump lands in Shenzhen for a first-ever trilateral with Xi and Putin

Donald Trump met Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. in Manila on Wednesday, announced a new Philippines trade deal, and then flew to Shenzhen, where China's Xi Jinping greeted him on the tarmac — a rare honour Xi did not extend to Vladimir Putin when he arrived earlier in the day. On Thursday, the three leaders hold the first-ever US-China-Russia trilateral, with nuclear arms, Ukraine and Iran on the agenda.

Trump's Asia trip opened on Tuesday with the first Quad meeting in two years — Trump, India's Narendra Modi, Japan's Sanae Takaichi and Australia's Anthony Albanese — which produced a joint statement expressing "grave concerns" about economic coercion, including through arbitrary export restrictions, without naming China. The statement also flagged joint maritime patrol-aircraft exercises next year and an AI accelerator with more than $85 million in funding. Trump then met Marcos at Malacañang Palace on Wednesday, had lunch, and announced a new trade deal — though he had also said last year that an agreement had been reached, and no details were given beyond the White House saying Manila will buy more energy and farm products. The US goods deficit with the Philippines has risen from $4.9 billion in 2024 to $12.8 billion in the first nine months of this year, according to the Census Bureau. Trump also signed a trade deal with Thailand on Tuesday under which Bangkok will eliminate tariffs on US exports. In Shenzhen, Xi greeted Trump at the foot of Air Force One's stairs with a handshake that became a hug, as dancers waved flowers and flags — reciprocating a gesture Trump made during Xi's state visit to Washington in September.

What each side says: Trump says he plans to use Thursday's trilateral to talk about limiting nuclear weapons and ending the war in Ukraine, saying "I think it's very close to getting it done," and he has teased a private meeting with Putin and a separate sit-down with North Korean leader Kim Jong Un. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said on Wednesday that Trump was "serious" about meeting Kim, but that no meeting was currently scheduled for Thursday. Analysts are not expecting substantive breakthroughs despite the unprecedented nature of the meeting, and Trump's own aides had told reporters before the trip that Ukraine would not be on the agenda. China, for its part, has accused the US of "economic coercion" over its latest restrictions on Chinese access to advanced chips, and has effectively shut off exports of key critical minerals to Japan for nearly a year as relations between Asia's two largest economies have nosedived.

For ordinary people, the backdrop is a trade truce that expires in January and a chip war that is already reshaping supply chains — Washington's new rules on renting advanced AI chips through overseas data centres landed on Monday, just as Trump arrived in Asia. At home, a run of technical outages at Microsoft, the Federal Aviation Administration, JPMorgan Chase and the NSA's communications network overshadowed the trip; Rubio stepped in to reassure Americans, saying there was "zero" evidence the outages are connected or tied to rival nations, hackers or criminals, and that they stemmed from systems being fixed after vulnerabilities were found. In Manila, demonstrators gathered for a fifth day of protests that included burning Trump effigies and calls to end the Middle East war.

In short: Trump arrives in Shenzhen with a Philippines trade deal in hand and a tarmac welcome from Xi, but Thursday's trilateral — the first time the leaders of the US, China and Russia have met three-way — is where the substance is supposed to happen, on nuclear arms, Ukraine and Iran. Watch Thursday at 2:30 p.m. local time, and whether anything concrete comes out of it.

Java4Shell: governments now urging urgent patching

"Java flaw leaves systems open to hackers, say US, South Korea, China's Tencent" — Reuters

The Java4Shell flaw (CVE-2026-114938) reported last night is now drawing urgent government warnings. Reuters reports US and South Korean authorities and China's Tencent have urged companies to patch the critical flaw in Java's Base64 decoder, which can allow unauthenticated remote code execution on systems with AVX-512 VBMI processors. South Korea's presidential office held an emergency meeting on Tuesday, and its science ministry and internet security agency said on Wednesday that public institutions and private enterprises must urgently install the updates. Tencent Cloud warned customers the flaw — dubbed "Java4Shell" — could let unauthenticated attackers steal sensitive information or take control of affected servers. CISA has urged immediate patching, internet-facing systems first; Oracle shipped fixed builds on November 17. No known exploitation as of last night.

Source: Reuters via KWSN

Microsoft's agent-framework package was hijacked on PyPI to steal cloud credentials

"Attackers Compromise Microsoft's Agent Framework PyPI Package to Steal Cloud Credentials" — The Hacker News

Two malicious versions of Microsoft's agent-framework Python package (1.26.1 and 1.26.2) were published to PyPI 33 minutes apart on Tuesday evening, carrying a credential stealer that runs every time Python starts — even if the package is never imported — by dropping a .pth startup file. The stealer targets Azure and Azure DevOps credentials, environment variables with names containing TOKEN, SECRET, PASSWORD or KEY, credential files (.azure, .npmrc, .netrc, .git-credentials, Docker config, Hugging Face tokens), and in the second version, GitHub Actions OIDC tokens. Both versions were removed within about 45 minutes, and the last safe version is 1.26.0. Wiz, which reported it to Microsoft, says the attacker appears to have used a compromised client certificate and private key for Microsoft's internal release-signing pipeline; SafeDep found PyPI now lists a second maintainer, "vibiret," an account created on the day of the attack. The package gets about 190,000 downloads a month, so any CI pipeline that installed it in that window should pin agent-framework==1.26.0, rebuild, and rotate every secret it could have touched.
