Ukraine: only 4 of 36 Russian ballistic missiles were intercepted — and Kyiv says North Korean missiles hit a key power plant

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said on Saturday that Ukraine's air defences shot down only four of the 36 ballistic missiles Russia fired at Kyiv and the surrounding region overnight — and that preliminary examinations at the impact sites indicate Russia used North Korean-made KN-23 missiles to hit one of the key energy facilities damaged in the attack. It is the sharpest public admission yet of how thin Ukraine's missile shield has become, days before trilateral talks open in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday.

The details Zelenskyy gave after a coordination call on the damage paint a stark picture. Ukraine's energy facilities were hit 18 times in the past 24 hours, according to First Deputy Prime Minister and Energy Minister Denys Shmyhal. Zelenskyy said there is still "a significant shortage of interceptors for Patriot systems, and the United States is in the best position to help address it," and that Kyiv is working "at every level" to speed up deliveries. He also ordered the interior minister and Kyiv's city administration to prepare backup public transport routes and extra transport for the capital — noting that, at present, such contingency plans are "practically nonexistent" at the municipal level. G7-format meetings are being prepared, Zelenskyy said, with new European contributions expected to the PURL initiative that funds US weapons purchases for Ukraine, plus new energy support packages.

The two sides describe the same night in opposite terms. Zelenskyy's argument is that the escalation is deliberate pressure ahead of the talks: "By intensifying its attacks on Ukraine and our infrastructure at this very moment, Russia is trying to additionally lay the groundwork for putting pressure on Ukraine's position and that of its partners," he said, adding that "Russia has a very particular view of diplomacy — as a subtype of aggression." He also used the KN-23 finding to argue that global sanctions "need to be significantly strengthened — against everyone in the world who provides their 'services' in destroying lives." Moscow's defence ministry, as reported in the morning brief, said it struck military-industrial, energy and transport targets, claiming it hit Kyiv's Northern Bridge because it was being used to move weapons and a plant it says produces Flamingo cruise missiles. Russia has not responded to the KN-23 claim, and the missile identification is preliminary — examinations at the sites are ongoing.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the interception rate is the number that matters. Kyiv's power, heating and water situation was already described by the mayor as "extremely difficult" after the overnight barrage, with temperatures near freezing. If only one in nine ballistic missiles can be stopped, every new salvo lands harder — and the Patriot shortage is now the bottleneck Zelenskyy is pressing Washington on, openly, in the days before negotiators sit down. The talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11 go ahead as planned; nothing in Saturday's reporting suggests the date has changed.

In short: of the 36 ballistic missiles Russia fired at Kyiv overnight, Ukraine intercepted only four — and preliminary findings say North Korean KN-23 missiles were used against a key energy facility. Zelenskyy is publicly pressing the US for Patriot interceptors before Tuesday's Abu Dhabi talks, arguing Russia is bombing to gain leverage at the table.

Poland: gunman kills six in Krakow's Main Market Square — police say far-right influence is being investigated

A gunman drove into Krakow's Main Market Square on Saturday afternoon and opened fire at random from his car, killing six people and wounding 15, before police shot and detained him. It is the worst mass shooting in Poland in living memory, in a country that has long seen itself as safer from such attacks than its western European neighbours. The shooting happened at around 3 p.m. local time — just as this morning's brief was being published — so this is the first brief carrying it.

The facts as Polish authorities have confirmed them: the attacker was a 24-year-old Polish citizen who drove into the historic square — packed with tourists and locals on a Saturday afternoon — and fired at people from his vehicle. Police said he wore military-style clothes, used an AR-15 rifle he held a firearms licence for, and had "many weapons and ammunition" in his vehicle. Officers fired at him three minutes after the first report came in, according to Interior Minister Marcin Kierwinski, who praised them for preventing "a much larger massacre." Two police officers were wounded in the shootout, one of them shot three times and in critical condition. The six dead were four women and two men; one of the dead was a British citizen, and five of the injured were foreign nationals, including a Portuguese woman. Krakow's mayor declared city mourning through November 11, and President Karol Nawrocki said he would declare national mourning on November 8 and 9.

What is still unclear is why he did it. Kierwinski told a news briefing that "everything points to the fact that the shooter was influenced by far-right ideology, a matter that will also be subject to investigation" — but police have not named a motive, and the ideology angle is an early indication, not an established finding. The shooting also follows a string of school stabbings in Poland in October that authorities linked to violent extremist content online, which Reuters noted as part of the unease in the country. Polish media reported that three members of the gunman's family were found dead at his home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski earlier in the day; police have not confirmed that, though Reuters reported Internal Security Agency agents were sent to the family home as well as the scene.

The political reaction was immediate and, unusually, restrained. Prime Minister Donald Tusk, who travelled to Krakow after meeting the security services, called the shooting terrorism and appealed to politicians "of all stripes" not to exploit the tragedy, saying "we will establish all the circumstances of this crime." President Zelenskyy called it "shocking" and offered condolences, and EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said it was "a tragedy that will be felt far beyond Poland." The suspect is in hospital in serious condition after being shot by police; the only known perpetrator is in custody, and police say there is no continuing threat to the public.

In short: six people were killed and 15 wounded when a gunman opened fire at random in Krakow's Main Market Square on Saturday afternoon. Police shot and detained the 24-year-old suspect within minutes; the interior minister says early indications point to far-right influence, but the motive is still under investigation. National mourning runs November 8–9.

Tech: AI stocks whipsawed after the midterms — and a memory crunch is now squeezing Apple, Nvidia and PC prices

Wall Street's AI trade had its wildest two days in months. On Thursday, November 5, the Nasdaq Composite plunged 5.52% to 24,315 — its steepest drop since April 2025 — with the S&P 500 down 4.36% and the Dow down 3.44%. Nvidia fell 9.26%, AMD 17.26%, Micron 17.46% and SanDisk 22.26%. The trigger, according to market reporting, was post-midterm anxiety: with Democrats taking control of the House and Senate, investors feared tighter oversight of AI data centers and federal AI spending. On Friday the market bounced back part of the way — the Nasdaq rose 1.09% to 24,579.42, with AMD up 11.04%, Micron 5.12% and Nvidia 2.78% — but the index still lost 3.4% on the week, its fourth straight weekly decline, and the S&P 500 lost 2.7%.

Underneath the whipsaw sits a slower, more structural squeeze: memory. AI servers built around Nvidia and AMD accelerators need huge amounts of high-bandwidth memory, DRAM and fast storage, and memory makers have been steering wafers toward those customers. TrendForce forecasts conventional DRAM contract prices up another 10%–15% this quarter and NAND flash up 15%–20%, with DRAM supply staying tight into 2027. The sellers are making extraordinary money — Micron reported an 81% operating margin on $54.23 billion of quarterly revenue (up 379% year on year), SanDisk 80% and SK hynix 80% — while Samsung said its 2027 HBM supply is already sold out and SK hynix said supply will fall short of demand through 2030.

Apple is the clearest case of a buyer paying up. Its fiscal 2026 annual report says it is experiencing "supply constraints and rising costs driven by industry-wide supply-demand imbalances" and expects them to intensify for memory and storage. Apple guided its December-quarter gross margin to 45%–46%, citing memory costs first and the cost of its Siri AI ramp second, and its manufacturing purchase obligations rose by $33 billion in a single quarter, to $89.7 billion. Lower-margin products have less room to absorb it: PC makers are shipping smaller SSDs to hold prices down, and AMD's gaming revenue fell 40% in the third quarter. Nvidia, meanwhile, is moving deeper into the memory stack itself — asking Samsung, SK hynix and Micron to build the base dies of its custom NVHBM on TSMC's 3nm process — and TSMC confirmed it has begun planning 3D DRAM at Nvidia's request, though it says it will stay a logic foundry.

For ordinary buyers, this is the part that lands in the wallet: the same AI boom that lifted chip stocks is making the memory inside phones, laptops and consoles more expensive, and the crunch is expected to last into 2027. Apple's approach so far has been to absorb the cost rather than raise prices, but its margin guidance shows the pressure is real.

In short: AI stocks crashed 5.5% on Thursday on post-midterm regulation fears, then bounced 1.1% on Friday — but the Nasdaq still ended the week down 3.4%. Behind the swings, a memory shortage is pushing DRAM and NAND prices up another 10%–20% this quarter, memory makers are earning ~80% margins, and Apple is guiding its own margins down because of memory costs.

Yemen: Houthis report 25 killed in Saudi strike east of Sanaa

Houthi media reported at least 25 people killed, including women and children, in a Saudi airstrike on al-Shariyah, a town east of the Yemeni capital Sanaa, on Saturday. Al Masirah television, run by the Houthis, broadcast footage of rescuers pulling bodies from rubble, and the Houthi-run Saba news agency said Saudi Arabia carried out the attack. The Saudi-led coalition had not commented at the time of reporting, and the claim is single-source — it has not been independently confirmed. Hours before the strike, the coalition said it had intercepted a missile heading toward Riyadh, and Houthi leader Mahdi al-Mashat vowed the group would take revenge for the latest attacks. The UN says more than 1,600 people have been killed and over 300,000 displaced since fighting resumed in Yemen. This follows Friday's disputed market attack in Taiz, covered in the morning brief, where at least 30 were killed and each side blames the other. Sources · BBC via Security Signal

Cyber: Chainalysis breach hit every user's credentials; Apple flaw joins the KEV list

Chainalysis breach widened sharply — catch-up, first reported here. The blockchain analytics firm, whose software is used by the FBI, IRS Criminal Investigation and banks, said on November 4 that names, usernames, password hashes and live tokens for *all* of its users had been exposed to a malicious actor, and that some KYT transaction-screening accounts appear to have been accessed without authorization. The company first disclosed the incident on October 30, describing an attacker using "offensive agentic techniques." It took KYT, Reactor, its login page and other products offline, and began restoring KYT and Address Screening on November 6 behind IP allowlisting, with mandatory password resets and API key rotation. Chainalysis says it has no evidence investigations data was accessed or its attribution data manipulated, but the investigation is still open. Sources · Brandefense · Rescana

CISA added an Apple flaw to the KEV catalog. CVE-2024-27834, an improper-authentication flaw in multiple Apple products (CVSS 5.5), is now listed as actively exploited, with a federal patch deadline of November 18. It was fixed long ago — Safari 17.5, iOS/iPadOS 17.5 and 16.7.8 — so the action is to make sure old devices are actually updated. The SonicWall SSL-VPN flaw CVE-2026-19868, added Friday, was covered in the morning brief. Sources · ThreatPodium

OpenSSH "SSHole" exploitation is spreading. What was 8 attempts from a single IP in Japan on Friday is now 17 attempts from 11 IPs in 8 countries, per honeypot data. Fix is OpenSSH 10.6p2; not yet in CISA KEV. Source

QNAP is urging users to update Plex Media Server as the Plex2Shell vulnerability chain is now being actively exploited. Source
