Russia is trying to "divide Ukraine along the Dnipro," Zelensky says, as the grid campaign rolls into a second night

Russia kept hitting Ukraine's power grid through a second night of strikes while the first day of three-way talks in Abu Dhabi ended with no movement — and President Volodymyr Zelensky used his evening address to frame the campaign as an attempt to split the country in two along the Dnipro River. More than 850,000 people were left without power in the east, and the IAEA confirmed a drone strike killed a worker at the occupied Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant on Monday.

The overnight strikes into Tuesday morning — 162 drones and a ballistic missile hitting five major substations across 10 regions, killing two people in Vinnytsia — were covered in this morning's brief. What changed during the day is the scale of the damage and the framing. Energy company DTEK said the strikes damaged key equipment in Dnipropetrovsk region and cut power to large parts of Dnipro and Kamianske in the evening, leaving more than 600,000 people without electricity there and another 250,000 in Kharkiv region, according to reports from Nexus Europa citing DTEK. State grid operator Ukrenergo said energy infrastructure was hit in eight regions and kept scheduled blackouts in force into the evening, with hourly outage schedules for households and capacity limits for industry. In Poltava, strikes also knocked out power and water and hit an enterprise in Kremenchuk.

Caption: High-voltage transmission towers — the power grid Russia has been hammering in its renewed air campaign as winter approaches, with substations in Poltava, Dnipropetrovsk, Vinnytsia and Kirovohrad hit overnight.

Zelensky said in his evening televised address that Russian forces are "trying to divide Ukraine along the Dnipro River" by striking bridges and the power grid, and that the strikes aimed to "make life as difficult as possible for people, for our cities and villages." The Financial Times reported he told European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen the same, and a Ukrainian official told the paper Kyiv believes Russia plans to disrupt transport and ammunition shipments across the river and cut the flow of electricity from Ukraine's west to its east. Russia began heavily striking bridges across the Dnipro in Kyiv in October. Moscow, for its part, confirmed it launched attacks against Ukrainian energy facilities and a commercial container ship, and Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov repeated after day one in Abu Dhabi that Russia will not agree to an immediate ceasefire without knowing what comes next — the security-order-first position covered in this morning's brief. Ukraine hit back as well: Moscow-appointed officials in occupied Luhansk said Ukrainian drones knocked out power across the city and nearby towns, and reported at least three dead and four injured in drone attacks at other frontline points — a claim from Russian-appointed authorities, not independently verified.

The human cost keeps mounting at the edges of the campaign. The IAEA reported that drones killed a Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant employee and a worker at the Zaporizhzhia thermal power plant on Monday, November 9. The occupied Zaporizhzhia plant itself ran on emergency diesel generators for about ten hours on Sunday after losing its only external power line — its 27th blackout of the war — before power was restored. For ordinary Ukrainians, this is the fifth winter of the war, and the grid attacks are landing as temperatures fall toward freezing: homes across six regions woke up without power, and scheduled blackouts are now a daily routine.

In short: day one of the Abu Dhabi talks ended with the same split as it began — Moscow wants a security order agreed before a ceasefire, Kyiv wants the ceasefire first — while Russia's grid campaign rolled into a second night, leaving 850,000+ people without power in the east, and Zelensky framed the strikes as an attempt to divide Ukraine along the Dnipro. Day two of the talks is Wednesday.

France formally blames Iran's Revolutionary Guard for the supermarket freezer cyberattack

France on Monday formally accused Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of carrying out the cyberattack that sabotaged refrigeration systems at roughly 700 sites across France last month — the first time Paris has directly blamed Tehran for a cyberattack — and named the unit and three men it holds responsible. Paris prosecutors have opened a judicial investigation and requested international arrest warrants, and France summoned Iran's ambassador on Tuesday.

The attack, on the night of October 11–12, hit supermarkets — Carrefour, Intermarché, Netto, Coopérative U and Monoprix among them — plus a slaughterhouse, food processing plants and hydroelectric facilities. According to a five-page report from France's Cyber Crisis Coordination Centre, which brings together the national cybersecurity agency ANSSI, the military's cyber command and the DGSE and DGSI intelligence services, the hackers overrode digital thermostats and forced defrost cycles, driving temperatures from as low as minus 16.6 degrees Fahrenheit to above 122 degrees Fahrenheit. At least 15 fires broke out. The same hackers also probed Schneider Electric controllers at several small French hydroelectric plants, with moderate disruption. The report names the unit as Shahid Kaveh, an offensive cyber unit of the IRGC's electronic and cyber centre, and names its head, Hamid Reza Lashgarian, 65, along with Mahdi Lashgarian, 37, and Reza Salarvand, 35, as its commanders. Investigators linked the attack to the unit through Farsi-language traces in the code and infrastructure, matching tactics and tooling from earlier Shahid Kaveh hacks, IP addresses in Iran, and publicity of the attacks on propaganda channels linked to the unit, such as CyberAv3ngers. The French report says the hackers worked through numerous front groups masquerading as vigilante hackers, including CyberAv3ngers and the Cyber Isnaad Front.

Caption: A shopper in front of supermarket freezer cabinets — the kind of refrigeration systems hackers seized control of at roughly 700 French sites on October 11–12, forcing defrost cycles that spoiled food and started at least 15 fires.

France's position is that this was a state-ordered attack of "unprecedented scale and unprecedented aggressiveness," in Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot's words, and it will seek European sanctions against those responsible. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said the bloc's Foreign Affairs Council will take up the incidents on November 16 and called for a strong response if Iran is confirmed responsible. Iran has not formally responded to the attribution, and Tehran routinely denies carrying out malicious cyber operations. Not everyone is convinced: UK cyber security specialist Peter Bassill told The Grocer there is "no evidence factual or anecdotal that points to Iran being the culprit," and France has not published the underlying intelligence. The unit is already well known to US agencies — the Defense Department, FBI and NSA identified Shahid Kaveh earlier this year as another name for CyberAv3ngers, blamed for disrupting operational technology at US water utilities, and the State Department has offered up to $10 million for information on IRGC officials behind the group's 2023 attacks on US water utilities. Iran is also a suspect in refrigeration failures at six or more US military commissaries in August, which Pentagon investigators are treating as a suspected cyberattack; Microsoft had privately linked Iran to the French sabotage weeks before Paris went public, Reuters reported.

For ordinary people, this was an attack on food, not on data: fridges and freezers warming to over 122 degrees means spoiled stock, shuttered shops and, in the worst cases, fires in buildings full of shoppers. It also shows how exposed the everyday systems behind the food supply are — the same Danish Danfoss controllers that run supermarket refrigeration are widely deployed, and the attack needed no exotic capability, just access to poorly protected remote management. Proofpoint researcher Saher Naumaan told Reuters the sabotage showed less about Iran's skill than about how exposed such systems are.

In short: France has put a name and three faces on the October freezer attacks — Iran's IRGC cyber unit Shahid Kaveh — and is pursuing arrest warrants and EU sanctions, while Iran has not formally responded and at least some independent experts question the attribution. The EU takes it up on November 16, and the same unit is already suspected in similar refrigeration failures at US military grocery stores in August.

Ex-cop Matthew Farwell convicted of killing pregnant Sandra Birchmore

A federal jury in Boston convicted former Stoughton police officer Matthew Farwell on Tuesday of killing Sandra Birchmore, 23 and pregnant, and causing the death of her unborn child — a verdict that came a day after the same jury told the judge it was deadlocked. Farwell, 41, faces a mandatory life sentence when he is sentenced on February 3, 2027.

The jury of eight men and four women deliberated for roughly 14 hours over two days. On Monday afternoon they sent Chief U.S. District Judge Denise Casper a note saying they were deadlocked and did not expect that to change; the judge sent them home and told them Tuesday morning to keep working. At around 2 p.m. on Tuesday they returned guilty verdicts on both counts: killing a witness or victim under 18 U.S.C. § 1512(a)(1)(C), and causing the death of a child in utero under 18 U.S.C. § 1841. People could be heard crying in the courtroom gallery and Birchmore's family and friends hugged each other after the verdict was read, according to WCVB, and U.S. Attorney Leah Foley said afterward: "Despite Mr. Farwell's attempt to silence Sandra Birchmore, today her story is told… Justice has indeed been served."

Caption: Matthew Farwell, 41, in a photo provided by the Stoughton, Massachusetts, Police Department. A federal jury convicted him Tuesday of killing Sandra Birchmore and her unborn child; he faces a mandatory life sentence at sentencing on February 3, 2027.

Prosecutors said Farwell strangled Birchmore in her Canton apartment on February 1, 2021, and staged the scene to look like a suicide by hanging, to stop her from exposing a sexual relationship that began when she was 15 and he was a 27-year-old officer who met her through the Stoughton Police Explorers program for kids. In closing, prosecutor Brian Fogerty argued Farwell killed her to "clean up his mess" because he was about to be "exposed" — Birchmore had told him she was ready to come forward, and he killed her the day before his wife gave birth to their third child. The defense, led by attorney Kimberly Stevens, did not deny the affair but argued Birchmore took her own life the same night Farwell told her he was breaking things off, saying she had "built a fantasy world" around marrying him. Prosecutors also revealed at trial that DNA showed the fetus belonged to another man, not Farwell, and that Birchmore had become pregnant in December 2020 after demanding Farwell have a child with her or she would expose the affair. Her death was initially ruled a suicide by state investigators; the medical examiner later changed the manner of death to undetermined, and federal prosecutors took up the case, arresting Farwell in August 2024, more than three years later.

For Birchmore's family, the verdict ends nearly six years of waiting since a death first dismissed as suicide. For Stoughton and Massachusetts policing, it closes a case that exposed how a police officer used a youth program to groom a child — and how the original investigation, which prosecutors criticized for unrecorded interviews and missing warrants, failed her. Farwell has been in custody since his 2024 arrest.

In short: after a near four-week trial and a Monday deadlock that looked like it might end in a mistrial, the jury convicted Matthew Farwell on both counts Tuesday afternoon. He faces mandatory life in prison at sentencing on February 3, 2027.

Houthis claim four more strikes on Saudi Arabia

The Houthis announced on Tuesday that they carried out four strikes against Saudi Arabia — hitting King Khalid International Airport, the financial district in Riyadh, a facility owned by Aramco, and Saudi troop concentrations — and warned they will keep operating deep inside Saudi territory. They said the attacks were a response to a recent Saudi strike on a market in Taiz in southern Yemen. The claim came a day after the coalition said it intercepted three Houthi missiles overnight (a ballistic and a cruise missile toward Riyadh, another ballistic missile toward the south) plus a drone over the Eastern Province, with no casualties reported. The Houthis also said on Tuesday they attacked the East-West oil pipeline in Riyadh Province — while denying the Saudi assertion that they hit the pipeline in the Medina region on Monday, where the Saudis reported a limited fire that was extinguished with no casualties and no effect on operations. Riyadh's deputy governor inspected the damaged Aramco administrative building in the capital on Tuesday and said the attack showed the Houthis targeting civilian facilities. (AzerNews · SRN News (Reuters) · TBS News)

Iran — Araghchi presses the US to meet Tehran's demands; no answer yet

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said on Tuesday that "the conflict with Iran will not be resolved through military means, nor by intensifying sanctions," and that the only solution is diplomacy that addresses Iran's demands in its two-day proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — ending the war on all fronts, unfreezing Iranian assets, lifting oil sanctions and ending the US counter-blockade of Iranian ports. Tehran also denied reports, carried by Fars News Agency, that it had agreed to negotiate reopening the strait in exchange for $8 billion in frozen assets, calling them completely false. The US answer to Tehran's proposal was still due today, with Qatari officials in Tehran; Reuters reported oil flows through the strait have recovered to around 80 percent of prewar levels. The split inside Tehran continues — President Pezeshkian talking negotiation, security chief Rezaei insisting the US "submit to the seven conditions." (Arab News · Stockbiz / Fars · IranWire)

Israel — Knesset sworn in; speaker vote set for November 17

Israel's 26th Knesset was sworn in on Tuesday afternoon, with 46 first-time members, and the opposition bloc submitted the signatures of 67 lawmakers to replace Speaker Amir Ohana, a Netanyahu ally, with Matan Kahana of Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party. Ohana said he has scheduled the vote for Tuesday, November 17, at 4 p.m. The Eisenkot bloc also filed bills on its first day for a state commission of inquiry into the October 7, 2023 attack, a limit on a prime minister's time in office, and a repeal of the judicial overhaul — and agreed Avigdor Lieberman will chair the Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee. Netanyahu, still caretaker prime minister, used the ceremony to attack the government Eisenkot is trying to form. Eisenkot has 28 days from November 8 to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. (Silent Dossier · The Insight International · Hathalyoum / Anadolu)

FTC v. Amazon — economics hearing: judge presses the FTC on consumer harm

At Tuesday's economics hearing in the FTC's antitrust case against Amazon, Judge John Chun pressed the FTC on what consumer harm it is actually trying to prove — lower quality, higher prices — and on whether it can show what the market would have looked like without Amazon's conduct. The FTC said it is challenging a course of conduct that keeps rivals from competing, which indirectly raises prices for sellers and shoppers, but conceded it cannot quantify a "but-for world." The judge also noted the FTC's case mirrors a consumer case he oversees, Hogan v. Amazon, which he said "is going pretty poorly." Separately, Milan prosecutors have closed an investigation into Amazon over the circulation of 1,519,613 non-EU goods in Italy's customs territory, with €3,549,612 in allegedly unpaid taxes and duties covering 2021 to March 2026 — the next step would be a request to send the case to trial. The FTC's Prime "dark patterns" jury trial continues in Seattle. (Typefully · EL7)

Cyber — "SSHole" exploitation still climbing; KEV deadline is Thursday

Previdian's sensors have now logged 26 exploitation attempts against OpenSSH's "SSHole" flaw (CVE-2026-123158) from 17 unique attacker IPs across 12 countries, first observed November 6 and last observed today, November 10. The Hacker News reported on the same CISA batch today — four flaws added to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on Monday (GPUStack, SignalWire FreeSWITCH, OpenSSH "SSHole," IBM Aspera Faspex), all with a federal remediation deadline of Thursday, November 12. No new KEV additions today. (Previdian · Tech Insider)
