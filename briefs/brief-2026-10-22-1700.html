1. Iran/US + Yemen/Saudi + Hormuz

Oil above $113 as reports of a post-midterm US-Israel strike plan rattle markets

Thursday's big new development in the Gulf was not a fresh missile — it was a report about what may come next, and markets reacted as if it were. According to the New York Times, reported Wednesday evening by the New York Post and UBS, US and Israeli officials have drawn up plans for a "short but intensive" operation against Iran to begin after the US midterm elections on November 3. President Donald Trump told reporters late Wednesday that "something one way or another" will "happen fairly soon" in Iran — while repeating his pledge, covered in earlier briefs, that there will be no US strikes on Iran before the midterms.

Brent crude rose 4% to $113.17 a barrel on Thursday, with West Texas Intermediate up 2.4% to $99.47, according to the Post's market roundup; earlier in the day Brent had traded around $112–112.68, near its five-month high. The Nasdaq fell 2.6% to 26,008.86, its worst day since June, with the S&P 500 down 1.21%, as traders weighed both the Iran headlines and worries about tech companies' AI spending. Meta and Tesla led tech lower despite strong earnings, and Nvidia fell 4.4% on the day.

The two sides of the strike-plan story frame it very differently. Washington's public line, via State Department spokesman Tommy Pigott, pushed back on one part of the speculation: he denied a report that the US is preparing to order federal employees at other Gulf embassies to leave, saying the departure order covers Saudi Arabia specifically because of Houthi attacks there. That order, issued Wednesday, covers non-emergency US government staff and families in Saudi Arabia. Iran, for its part, has warned through the Revolutionary Guard Navy that continued US provocations could trigger a wider response against American bases across the Middle East — a warning repeated in Thursday's coverage of its vessel claims. Market analysts split on what the price move means: CIBC's Rebecca Babin told clients that "continued kinetic activity and the risk of renewed escalation following the midterms are keeping a floor under prices," while other traders noted distillate inventories rose against expectations, arguing against a sustained rally without actual supply losses.

Thursday also brought firmer, grimmer numbers on the Saudi attacks already reported this morning. The death toll from Wednesday's attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam rose to two — a Saudi citizen and an Indian national — with eight injured, according to The National's live coverage and Asharq News reporting; the civil aviation authority said operations at the airport have since resumed. A separate drone attack on Aramco's Shaybah oil field killed two workers, a Filipino and a Nepali contractor, Saudi authorities said, though operations at the 1-million-barrel-a-day field were unaffected. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted four drones over Eastern Province on Wednesday night and four more over the Riyadh and Medina regions on Thursday, and Aramco told essential staff at its Dhahran headquarters to keep working from home. The Houthis, meanwhile, warned Saudi Arabia against targeting Yemeni tribes and vowed to keep striking deeper inside the kingdom until attacks on Yemen stop and the blockade is lifted; they claim more than 2,900 Saudi strikes over the past 100 days killed or wounded over 562 people, mostly women and children — a Houthi tally that has not been independently verified.

For ordinary people, the squeeze is already visible and not abstract. US national average gasoline prices have stayed stubbornly above $4 a gallon, the Post notes, and one analyst cited in the morning brief warned prices are unlikely to fall below $4 for gasoline and $6 for diesel before the midterms without clear de-escalation. The IMF now forecasts a 4.8% contraction for Gulf economies as the Iran war hits exports, according to The National's live blog, and US Energy Information Administration data cited in Thursday coverage showed Saudi crude supply at 5.97 million barrels a day in August, down from 8.24 million in July. The stakes of the Yanbu claim from this morning also became clearer Thursday: Yanbu is the Red Sea terminal of Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the main route for exporting crude without passing through the Strait of Hormuz — so an attack there threatens the workaround keeping Gulf oil moving, even if no barrels were lost this time.

In short: no new US strike has been ordered, and the post-midterm plan is a reported plan, not an announcement — but oil above $113, a stock selloff, and a US departure order from Saudi Arabia show how seriously markets are taking it. Watch for any White House confirmation or denial of the Times report, for whether other Gulf missions follow the Saudi departure order, and for any independent confirmation of the vessel and Yanbu claims.

2. Ukraine/Russia + Poland

"Humanitarian" row erupts as Kyiv's toll rises and its air defences run dry

The overnight Kyiv barrage covered in the morning brief grew grimmer — and more politically charged — during Thursday. Updated Ukrainian figures put the toll at two killed and 34 injured across Kyiv and 12 regions, including five children, according to Ukrinform's newsletter and the Washington Examiner; three of the ballistic missiles fired at Kyiv carried cluster warheads, Zelenskyy said. The Times reports, citing Ukrainian media, that Kyiv was unable to shoot down any of the missiles after running out of interceptors for its US Patriot air-defence systems — a claim that, if true, explains the scale of the damage, but which has not been confirmed by Ukrainian officials or the US.

The political flashpoint was a word. US envoy Steve Witkoff, after meeting Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg on Wednesday and securing the release of detained American Stephen Hubbard, told Putin: "You are a humanitarian also, sir. I salute you, and I commend you." Zelenskyy answered on X on Thursday: "Three waves of ballistic missiles have been launched at Kyiv by Russian 'humanitarians' since yesterday evening. They used 21 missiles." Trump, asked about the remark in the Oval Office on Wednesday, said Witkoff could have meant it for the "particular purpose" of Hubbard's release and added, "I wouldn't say anything one way or the other." The UK, per Ukrinform, rejected the "humanitarian" characterization of Putin outright.

The arguments behind the row show the real divide. Zelenskyy, in an interview with podcaster Steven Bartlett that aired Thursday, said holding the current front lines would already be "a huge compromise," and argued: "Trump can bring Putin to the negotiating table very easily. The way President Trump talks about me, he needs to adopt that kind of rhetoric about Putin." He said Wednesday he would accept a deal freezing fighting at the current lines. Trump, earlier in the week, said it may be "time for somebody else to negotiate on behalf of Ukraine" and that Zelenskyy "should have had this war settled a long time ago." European commentary collected by Eurotopics was sceptical of the talks themselves: The Spectator argued Putin is "using this to play for time ahead of a winter in which he hopes to make substantial progress," while the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung argued recent US moves have left the Kremlin convinced it has "the greater staying power" and can play Washington and Europe off against each other. Moscow's position, via aide Yuri Ushakov, remains talks only after November 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi, focused on a post-war security system — with no ceasefire agreed and major disagreements over Donetsk and NATO membership unresolved.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the human impact is the same story sharpened: homes on both sides of one Kyiv resident were hit, windows blown out, a neighbour killed, in the words of residents quoted by The Times; shopping malls, a monastery, a railway station and metro infrastructure were damaged; and with winter approaching, each night of strikes on energy infrastructure raises the prospect of cold, dark weeks. Ukraine also says it sent over 600 drones toward Moscow overnight, with Moscow's mayor reporting two people wounded — the exchange continuing even as negotiators talk about talking.

In short: the talks are still not scheduled, the date is still disputed, and Thursday's row over a single word captured why Kyiv doubts Moscow's intent — while the reported exhaustion of Patriot interceptors, if confirmed, would be the most consequential military detail of the day. Watch for confirmation of the interceptor shortage, for any US response on resupply, and for a confirmed talks date and venue.

3. Tech: Apple, Nvidia, Google

Nvidia's Berlin pitch: AI factories in a shipping container — and a warning about the money behind the boom

At GTC Berlin, Nvidia's big announcement was less a chip than a building: DSX POD, a reference design for complete Vera Rubin AI factories built by partners in modular containers, integrated and validated before delivery. According to Futurum's analysis and a StorageNewsletter summary of Nvidia's release, the factory-built units cut deployment at a prepared site from six months to as little as 14 days, with 17 partners set to deliver from early 2027 — Bull, Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Schneider Electric and Vertiv among them. Alongside it came OpenShell, an agent-security layer, and Sentry, extending Nvidia's stack from silicon to the agent runtime. The pitch to Europe, in CEO Jensen Huang's framing, is that Europe can produce AI rather than simply consume it — though Futurum notes the binding constraint in Europe remains access to grid power, which a container does not solve.

The supply-chain side landed the same day: Nvidia formalised a strategic partnership with Samsung Electro-Mechanics, which will expand dedicated AI semiconductor packaging lines in Sejong and Busan for Nvidia's chip substrates, Aju Press reports — the first formal partnership of its kind between the two. TechTimes reports Nvidia also committed $1.1 billion to chip-testing firm KYEC and locked in six supply-chain agreements aimed at doubling testing capacity. LG Innotek, meanwhile, unveiled a 3-megapixel camera module and a Multi-modal Vision System built for Nvidia's open-source Holoscan Sensor Bridge, which routes sensor data straight to the GPU without passing through the CPU.

Huang's other Thursday headline cut the other way. Speaking to CNBC at GTC Berlin, he said demand from "neoclouds" — firms that rent out GPU capacity, like CoreWeave, Iren and Nebius — is "off the charts," but "what is missing in the middle is the working capital to fuel that growth," adding that Nvidia wants its chips treated as a "financeable asset" with residual value as an asset class. That landed on a day AI stocks sold off hard: Nvidia itself fell 4.4%, AMD 8.2% and Micron 6.3%, with CoreWeave down 10.3%, per the Post's market roundup — investors weighing whether the AI spending boom pays off. In short: Nvidia is selling speed (14-day factories) and supply security, while its own CEO concedes the customers buying it all are short of cash, not customers.

4. Open source

VoiceStudio: a fully local, open-source ElevenLabs alternative — up 13,000 stars in a week

VoiceStudio (debpalash/VoiceStudio, ~81,000 stars, +13,000 in 7 days per FindARepo's Oct 22 ranking) is an open-source, fully local voice-cloning and text-to-speech alternative to ElevenLabs — no cloud, no per-minute billing. Why it matters: voice cloning is one of the most subscription-locked AI capabilities, and a local option that runs on your own hardware changes who can use it and what it costs. FindARepo

morluto/rea: point your coding agent at any app and learn how it works (+9,392 stars)

The fastest-growing repo in GhTrends' Oct 22 daily window, morluto/rea is a toolkit that lets a coding agent reverse-engineer software without source code — inspecting native binaries through Hopper or Ghidra, JavaScript and Electron apps, .NET assemblies and websites — then explaining how a feature works, with the evidence and limits behind each conclusion. Analysis runs locally, from the terminal. Note: this is a separate project from the REA tool covered in earlier briefs, despite the similar name. GhTrends

dumbtv: strip the ads and tracking off a Fire TV, reversibly (+~1,200 stars)

humblemuzzu/dumbtv removes ads, tracking and clutter from Fire TV over ADB, no root, with every step undoable, and ships its own open-source home screen — the Fire TV sibling of the CleanYourTV Android TV debloater covered in the morning brief. Why it matters: same practical payoff for Amazon's TV platform, where the home screen is even more ad-heavy. offhype

Also trending: Ebony-Vinyl/dsh-our-free-model (+3,515, a plugin putting free frontier models one install away in the DeepSeek Harness shell, no login or API key), and croffasia/itsaplan (open-source, self-hosted Linear/Plane alternative where teams and AI agents plan side by side). 5. Cybersecurity

Follow-up: US agencies issue urgent PHP2Shell alert — while the exploitation timeline still doesn't add up

CISA, the NSA and the FBI issued an urgent alert Thursday urging every organisation running PHP to patch CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 (PHP2Shell, CVSS 9.2, unauthenticated remote code execution) immediately — to 8.5.12 / 8.4.27 / 8.3.36 / 8.2.35 — or take PHP systems offline if they can't patch, with a web application firewall only as a stopgap. But the alert contains a tension worth flagging: CISA says it has not observed exploitation as of publication, while Amazon Threat Intelligence, reported by BleepingComputer on Thursday, says attackers began targeting the flaws four days before the patches were released — consistent with the SecurityWeek analysis in the morning brief. Both can be partly true (different visibility, different definitions), but the safe reading is the NSA's: upgrade or disconnect. Ordinary users are not being told to stop using the web; this is a server-side patching emergency. Finadium (CISA alert) · BleepingComputer via IT Security News

ClickFix is surging — and fake AI installers are its most convincing lure yet

BleepingComputer's Oct 22 report finds ClickFix attacks — the fake-error-message trick that gets users to paste malicious commands themselves — have never been bigger, with cybercriminals using fake AI tool installers, malicious search results and trusted chatbot platforms to lure victims. Why it matters: it bypasses most technical defences by getting the user to run the payload, and AI hype is now the bait. BleepingComputer via IT Security News

Operation Serengeti III: nearly 11,000 cybercrime networks disrupted across Africa

Law enforcement agencies disrupted nearly 11,000 cybercrime networks and arrested more than 1,400 people in Operation Serengeti III, BleepingComputer reports Thursday. Why it matters: one of the largest coordinated takedowns of the year, targeting the infrastructure behind scams and ransomware rather than a single gang. BleepingComputer via IT Security News

WAGO industrial controllers: root command execution via web management

CISA published an Oct 20 advisory, surfacing in Oct 22 roundups, for two vulnerabilities in WAGO OS Linux firmware (below 4.10.0) on CC100, PFC100 G2, PFC200 G2, TP600, Edge Controller and WP400 devices: CVE-2026-80466 (root-privileged command execution) and CVE-2026-88850 (unauthorised PROFIBUS configuration changes), exploitable by an authenticated low-privileged user. No confirmed exploitation reported; WAGO recommends firmware 4.10.0 or later, and keeping control devices off the public internet. Security Boulevard daily roundup

6. Major court cases

Russell Brand trial, Thursday (Day 10): a 999 call from 22 years later, and a friend's account of an alleged 2009 rape

Thursday's prosecution evidence at Southwark Crown Court included a 999 call played to the jury from an MTV worker who alleges Brand attacked her in 2001 — grabbing her and trying to drag her into a Soho bar toilet — and who only reported it 22 years later, in 2023, after seeing media reports about Brand's alleged misconduct. On the call she said her account "could be insignificant" but "shows what he is capable of," and told police she did not think Brand would remember the incident. Jurors also heard from a friend of a second complainant, who said the woman told her soon after an alleged 2009 encounter at Brand's home that it had "really, really hurt" and that she was crying and upset. Brand, 51, denies all seven charges (3 rapes, 3 sexual assaults, 1 indecent assault, 1999–2009); his barrister has put to complainants that encounters were consensual and later "reimagined." The defence case is expected next week, and it is still unclear whether Brand will testify. Trial expected to run up to 10 weeks. BritBrief (999 call) · British Bulletin (friend's testimony)
