Morning brief — Wednesday, November 11, 2026

Abu Dhabi talks end with a prisoner swap deal — but no ceasefire, and the grid attacks went on anyway

The first three-way Ukraine talks in nine months ended in Abu Dhabi on Wednesday with one concrete result — a major prisoner-of-war release — and little else. US mediators were blunt about why: after two days of meetings there is, in Jared Kushner's words, "no trust right now between the sides," and the hardest question, territory, is still untouched. The fighting, meanwhile, did not pause for the talks: Russia hit six more Ukrainian power substations overnight, two of its missiles crossed into Moldovan airspace, and Ukrainian drones set fire to a Russian Baltic port — triggering shelter alerts across three NATO states.

The talks ran Tuesday and Wednesday in the Emirati capital, the first direct Russia-Ukraine negotiations with US participation since February. Ukraine's delegation was led by foreign intelligence chief Rustem Umerov, Russia's by Kremlin aide Vladimir Medinsky, and the US side by special envoy Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. The first day lasted about four hours and focused largely on humanitarian questions — prisoner exchanges, the return of Ukrainian civilians held in Russia, and the fate of Ukrainian children taken to Russia. On the second day, the delegations agreed to a major release of prisoners of war — the scale discussed was 1,000-for-1,000, possibly 1,500 — but announced nothing else concrete, and issued no joint statement. Kushner said the talks had touched on territorial issues and that "these would be the hardest to resolve," adding that "some areas we see places where we can find compromises, other we need to have more discussions, but I think this was a very good step." Ukrainian negotiator David Arakhamia called the talks tough — Russia's delegation arrived with "strict directives" — but said they provided "good momentum" towards a solution before the end of the year. Medinsky said understanding had been reached on some issues, but was cool on the idea of European countries joining the talks.

What each side argues has not moved. Moscow wants the shape of a future European security order agreed before any ceasefire, and insists Ukraine surrender the parts of the Donbas it still holds before a peace deal can be struck. Kyiv wants the fighting to stop first and refuses to cede territory, proposing instead to freeze the conflict along the current front line; European capitals back that order, and a joint EU-US statement from Abu Dhabi said any settlement must respect Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. A US official briefing reporters said both sides had privately shown some flexibility on territory and that security guarantees — possibly a defence commitment modelled on NATO's Article 5 — would be central, but neither government has said anything similar in public. Europe's place at the table was itself contested: European officials and NATO representatives met the US and Ukrainian teams in Abu Dhabi, but Russia's delegation declined to meet them. Kushner said he and Witkoff will travel to Moscow on Thursday to meet Putin — and raise broadening the talks to include the Europeans — then fly to Ireland to meet Zelenskyy.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the talks changed nothing overnight. Russia launched 163 strike drones and a Kh-59/69 guided missile at Ukraine overnight from Tuesday evening, including 91 jet-powered variants, according to the Ukrainian Air Force; 130 drones were shot down or neutralised. Six major electrical substations were hit in Vinnytsia, Kirovohrad, Zhytomyr and Cherkasy, Prime Minister Sergiy Koretskyi said, and grid operator Ukrenergo reported partial outages in eight regions. A heat supply facility in Kryvyi Rih was struck, cutting heating to residential buildings as winter sets in; a guided bomb damaged a school in Kherson, five apartment buildings were damaged in Kharkiv, and at least 14 people were injured. In the Odesa region, strikes on energy infrastructure left about 135,000 consumers without power, and an attack on a busy market killed three people, the regional governor said. Two Russian Banderol cruise missiles crossed into Moldovan airspace during the attack, Moldova's Defence Ministry reported, flying over villages in the south before re-entering Ukrainian airspace. And the war's spillover reached the Baltics on Wednesday: Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia briefly issued shelter alerts over unidentified drones, and Finland imposed temporary airspace and maritime curbs in the eastern Gulf of Finland — all later withdrawn — after Ukrainian drones hit Russia's Ust-Luga port area on the Baltic, setting it on fire and injuring three. Drone debris killed two people in Russia's Smolensk region, and one person was killed in a Ukrainian drone attack in Belgorod, according to local authorities.

In short: two days of Abu Dhabi talks produced a prisoner swap and not much else — no ceasefire, no movement on territory, and no trust, by the Americans' own account — while Russia kept hammering Ukraine's power grid and the war's drones kept straying into NATO airspace. The next step is Kushner and Witkoff in Moscow on Thursday, then with Zelenskyy in Ireland; whether the prisoner release actually happens, and whether the Europeans get a seat, are the things to watch.

Iran and the US are telling two different stories about the same war

Washington and Tehran cannot agree on whether they are even talking — and that disagreement is now the story. On Tuesday, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio declared that Operation Epic Fury, the US-Israel war on Iran that began February 28, "is concluded" because its objectives were achieved, and President Trump paused Project Freedom, the naval mission to guide stranded ships out of the Strait of Hormuz, saying "great progress" had been made toward a final agreement. On the same day, Iran's top security official, Mohsen Rezaei, went on Lebanon's Al Mayadeen TV and called reports of negotiations "a pure lie," said the Strait of Hormuz "will not reopen until the US meets Tehran's seven conditions," and insisted Washington was talking up talks to ease economic pressure and bring down oil prices.

The details of the two accounts barely overlap. According to Rubio, speaking at a White House briefing on Tuesday, "The Operation Epic Fury is concluded. We achieved the objectives of that operation," and "we're not cheering for an additional situation to occur. We would prefer the path of peace." Trump announced the Project Freedom pause on Truth Social on Tuesday, saying it was "based on the request" of Pakistan and other countries and that the blockade would stay in full force while the two sides try to finalise a deal. Reuters and Axios reported the US has floated a one-page memorandum of understanding to formally end hostilities and open a 30-day window for detailed negotiations — under it, Iran would commit to a moratorium on nuclear enrichment while the US lifts sanctions and releases frozen Iranian funds, and both sides allow transit through Hormuz. But Trump struck a more conditional note in a social media post on Wednesday, writing that Epic Fury would "be at an end" only if Iran "agrees to give what has been agreed to" — and "if they don't agree, the bombing starts, and it will be, sadly, at a much higher level and intensity than it was before." The IRGC navy said on Wednesday that safe transit through the strait would be ensured, and the US said it had evacuated 22 crew members held on an Iranian container vessel to Pakistan as a confidence-building measure.

Tehran's version is the opposite. Rezaei said Iran is still waiting for an explicit US response to its seven conditions — which include lifting sanctions and the US naval blockade and ending the fighting on all fronts, especially Israeli attacks on Lebanon — and that the initial US response was "insufficient and deceptive." Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi told state news agency IRNA that "the conflict with Iran will not be resolved through military means, nor by intensifying sanctions," and that Hormuz could reopen within two days if the demands are met. Both Rezaei and Araghchi rejected reports of a meeting with US Vice President JD Vance as baseless. Meanwhile the attacks on shipping have not stopped: the IRGC claimed three oil tankers caught fire on Tuesday after striking naval mines on an "unauthorised route" in the eastern strait, and the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency confirmed reports of two tanker incidents, including one struck by an unknown projectile northeast of Oman's Lima village. Those claims are unverified — no vessel names or casualties have been given — but maritime monitors count at least 37 ships attacked in and near Hormuz in October, the highest monthly total since the war began, with 10 more so far in November. Iran's Fars news agency also claimed a US warship was hit with two missiles near Jask on Monday; the US military denied it, and no independent evidence has confirmed it. CENTCOM says US forces have helped move more than 900 million barrels of crude through the strait since early May.

For ordinary people, the gap between the two stories is the danger: oil is the tell. Brent crude rose about 0.9% to $92.83 a barrel on the tanker attacks, while the International Maritime Organization estimates up to 20,000 seafarers are stranded aboard roughly 2,000 vessels in the Gulf. If the US account is right and a deal is near, shipping and fuel prices could ease quickly; if Iran's account is right and there is no deal, the strait stays shut, the mines stay in the water, and the blockade — which Rubio called a defensive measure and Iran calls an act of war — keeps squeezing both sides.

In short: the US says its war on Iran is over and a deal is close; Iran says there are no talks at all and the strait stays shut until its seven conditions are met. Both can't be right. The test is whether the one-page memorandum Axios described actually gets signed — or whether Trump's warning of resumed bombing "at a much higher level" is what comes next.

Nvidia: today is the House deadline — and the pressure on Huang is now coming from inside his own party

Today, Wednesday, is the deadline House Foreign Affairs Chairman Brian Mast set for Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to answer a request for a closed-door briefing on November 18 about his interactions with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, whom US agencies allege was recruited by Chinese intelligence. Mast told ABC News on Tuesday he is "absolutely" prepared to subpoena Huang if he does not respond by today. No subpoena has been issued, and Nvidia has not publicly said whether Huang will attend. The pressure is no longer just from the House: on Tuesday evening, Senator Kevin Cramer of North Dakota, a senior Republican on the Senate Banking Committee, became the first sitting senator to call on Huang to step down, telling POLITICO "the potential for extortion is so tremendous that something as sensitive as Nvidia chips, I just think he's got to go." And on Wednesday morning, Baird downgraded Nvidia to Neutral from Outperform and slashed its price target to $200 from $500, citing less visibility into 2027 data-centre forecasts, cooling hyperscaler spending, and uncertainty over whether Huang stays CEO through 2027.

The board's own investigation is still running. Nvidia's independent directors hired former acting US Attorney Joon H. Kim and the firm Wachtell Lipton on Tuesday to lead it, after the board opened the inquiry on Sunday, November 8, into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. The board has said it has "no reason to believe" national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised, but called that a preliminary position, and Huang remains CEO with the board's stated confidence. Huang admitted in a staff email on Sunday that he was unfaithful to his wife and apologised; neither his message nor the board's statement addressed the chip sales at the centre of the reporting.

The story has widened well beyond the affair, and several developments from the last two days had not been reported in this brief before. Nvidia and Huang were sued on Monday in a proposed securities class action accusing them of defrauding shareholders by concealing Huang's ties to a suspected Chinese spy, Reuters reported; Trump said he had spoken with Huang and that the FBI is "looking into it." The Information reported on Tuesday, citing two people who know Alice Huang, that the relationship helped enable at least $7.5 billion of Nvidia chip shipments, with ties dating back to 2023 — Reuters said it could not independently verify that report. A New York Times investigation published Monday found the Commerce Department cleared roughly two dozen Chinese companies to buy or handle Nvidia's H200 chips — including Baidu, BYD, Alibaba, Tencent and ByteDance — but two approved server assemblers had empty offices at their listed addresses, and several approved firms have ownership ties to US-blacklisted companies or Chinese military suppliers. Bloomberg reported that Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter called Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler in late summer 2025 about a probe into suspected chip smuggling by Singapore-based Megaspeed, and that Nvidia's inventory tallies given to the US government undercounted chips sold to Megaspeed via hardware partners by about 50,000 units. Bank of America removed Nvidia, Micron and Lam Research from its US 1 List of top stock picks.

What each side argues is now a three-way fight. Lawmakers — Mast, Moolenaar and Crawford in the House, Cramer in the Senate, and Democratic leaders Schumer and Jeffries — say the reported relationship raises a national-security question about who has access to the world's most important AI chips, and want answers under oath or in a closed briefing. Nvidia's position is that its own checks found no evidence its chips were diverted, that Megaspeed denies diverting them, and that the board's independent inquiry will examine all available information. Wall Street is split: Baird sees a leadership overhang and a cooling data-centre cycle, while the stock bounced about 5% in premarket trading on Wednesday after closing Tuesday at $168.89, down 5.5% — a sector-wide lift led by CoreWeave, up about 17% after beating earnings. The scale of the slide is stark either way: Microsoft passed Nvidia in market value intraday on Monday, about $4.035 trillion to $4.032 trillion, the first time since June 2025, and Nvidia's value is down roughly $1.7 trillion since October 6.

In short: today is the day Huang has to answer Congress — subpoena threat on the table, a Republican senator now calling for his resignation, a Wall Street downgrade, and a securities lawsuit — while the board's investigation runs and Nvidia reports earnings on November 17, the day before the requested briefing. The allegations that secrets were compromised remain unproven, but the political and market pressure is now coming from every direction at once.

Houthi/Saudi — missiles fired at Riyadh again on Wednesday morning

The Saudi-led coalition intercepted missiles fired at Riyadh on Wednesday morning, according to FirstSquawk — three cruise missiles, per one report, two per another — with no casualties reported. The reports are single-source and the count differs, so treat this as developing; it follows Monday night's interception of three Houthi missiles (a ballistic and a cruise missile over Riyadh, plus a ballistic missile over the south). No Houthi claim of responsibility had been published by brief time. (CryptoBriefing · Internet Zarada)

Khor Mor — production reported restored, but reports conflict

Dana Gas confirmed on Wednesday that production at the Khor Mor gas field in Iraqi Kurdistan is back to normal after Sunday night's drone attack, and the Kurdistan Regional Government said 24/7 electricity supply was restored on Sunday. The strike had cut 2,500–2,800 MW — roughly 70–80% of the region's power. One report this morning still described the field as shut and gas not resumed, so the resumption is not yet unanimous in the reporting — but the operator's own confirmation is the stronger signal. No group has claimed responsibility for the attack. (Kurdistan Chronicle · Tiger Brokers · Hathalyoum)

Tech

Google's November Pixel Drop is out — Pixel Tag goes on sale today

Google's November Pixel Drop began rolling out on November 10, and the Pixel Tag — Google's AirTag rival, first shown in August — goes on sale today, November 11, at $29.99 for one or $99.99 for a four-pack, with ultra-wideband guidance through Android's Find Hub network. The drop brings Gemini Intelligence to Pixel 10 phones, including Rambler, a voice-typing tool that strips fillers and formats messages, plus Gemini calling on Pixel 10 and 11 (US and English first) and Pixel Search on every Pixel back to the Pixel 6. Google Fi adds automatic switching between two 5G networks and encrypted Wi-Fi. This is a feature rollout, not a new phone — the Pixel Tag is the only new hardware, and it was announced three months ago. (Google blog · TechDogs)

Cyber

Microsoft's November Patch Tuesday fixed about 1,094 flaws — only one confirmed exploited

Microsoft's November Patch Tuesday addressed roughly 1,094 vulnerabilities, with 63 rated Critical — the second straight month over 1,000. The only one confirmed exploited is the Outlook zero-day already in CISA's KEV catalog (CVE-2026-124194, an NTLM hash leak, federal deadline November 24). No new KEV additions were published overnight; the November 9 four (SSHole, GPUStack, FreeSWITCH, IBM Aspera Faspex) carry a federal deadline of tomorrow, November 12, and the Barracuda pair is due Friday, November 13. (Rod Trent / Security Check-in)

Railway published its incident report on the October 21 attack; Nitori disclosed a breach

Cloud platform Railway published a detailed incident report on the security attack it suffered on October 21 — worth a read if you run anything on it. Separately, Japanese furniture retailer Nitori Holdings disclosed unauthorised access discovered on November 6; scope is still unclear. (Rod Trent / Security Check-in)

Open source

GitHub trending, November 11 — expo/exact, a Maybe fork, and a Rust web framework

  • expo/exact — write an app once, run it natively on web, macOS, iOS and Linux, and an AI agent can build, run, see and test it on every platform. Rust, about 2.4k stars, up ~1,100 a day.
  • we-promise/sure — a self-hosted personal finance app, a community fork of Maybe. New and climbing the daily board.
  • tokio-rs/topcoat — a full-stack web framework for Rust, rendered on the server, from the Tokio team.

Still leading the boards: meta/astryx (~22k, Meta's open design system), sherlock (~109k) and storytold/craft-anything (~28k). (GitDiscover · Trendar · Daily Diff)
