Night brief · Sunday, October 25, 2026

Night brief — Sunday, October 25, 2026 (~10 PM CT)

A quieter Sunday evening than the afternoon suggested. The "maybe tomorrow" deadline that hung over the weekend resolved into something concrete: an explicit no-strike-today from Trump, and Qatari mediators on the ground in Tehran. The Saudi missile campaign did not pause, though — Sunday's interception count and the first debris injuries of the weekend are now on the record.

World escalation

"No strike today": Trump rules out a Sunday attack on Iran as Qatari mediators work Tehran

President Donald Trump said Sunday there would be no American strike on Iran "today," closing out the weekend deadline he set himself on Saturday — while keeping the threat of renewed military action fully open. Asked by a reporter whether a US attack could come as soon as Sunday, Trump replied, according to The Jerusalem Post: "Would I tell you that? The answer is no. There will not be a strike today." Earlier Sunday, on Fox News' "Sunday Morning Futures," he framed the choice still ahead of him in his own terms, as CBS News reported: "Now, my question is, do I make a deal with them, or do I finish them off?"

The details matter because Saturday's version was deliberately vaguer. On Saturday Trump had said the US was "thinking about doing it, and maybe tomorrow," alongside the possibility of a "big, beautiful" agreement, and said he would not disclose his plans. Sunday's answer narrows that to a single day: no strike Sunday, no decision announced for the days after. The Jerusalem Post also reported the prevailing assessment in Washington is that Trump will hold off on a final decision until after the November 3 midterm elections — though US officials quoted by the paper described a joint US-Israeli strike as a question of "when," not "if." No new attack order or military timetable was announced Sunday.

Washington's argument, as Trump put it, is about permanence: the earlier B-2 operation was not enough to guarantee Iran never builds a nuclear weapon, so further action may be needed to "make it permanent." He also claimed the US controls the Strait of Hormuz and said it is monitoring Iran's Pickaxe Mountain nuclear site plus three other sites hit in earlier strikes, adding "there isn't much activity going on there." On Friday he had floated "another little Epic Fury," the name of the joint US-Israeli campaign against Iran that began in February.

Tehran's side of the argument is that it will not be moved by the threat, and that diplomacy is still alive but not on Washington's terms. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Sunday that a resumption of war would not change Iran's positions, and that shipping through Hormuz would not return to its prewar arrangements. Foreign Ministry spokesperson Esmail Baghaei said the window for diplomacy had not closed and messages were still passing through intermediaries, according to AP — but added that Washington kept demanding "complete surrender," which he said no Iranian would accept. And the diplomacy is not just messaging: Reuters reported, citing an official briefed on the situation, that Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday as part of a US-coordinated effort to ease regional tensions, and Al Jazeera reported the same visit.

For ordinary people, the practical picture barely changed Sunday — and that is the point. The US State Department travel advisories updated earlier in the weekend for Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the UAE, Bahrain, Iraq, Lebanon and Israel still stand; the head of US Central Command, Admiral Brad Cooper, flew over the Strait of Hormuz on Sunday and said more than 3,000 commercial vessels had transited the waterway with US military support over the past six months, per AP. Gulf residents still face airport alerts and rerouted flights, and any fresh strike still risks another round of Hormuz disruption and higher fuel prices. The other shadow over the weekend is the intelligence warning reported Friday: US Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton warned Iran had "substantially increased efforts to target US personnel" abroad, with some plots at advanced stages, according to CBS News.

In short: Sunday's threatened strike did not happen, and Trump said so explicitly — but he ruled out one day, not the operation. With Qatari mediators in Tehran and both sides publicly insisting they want a deal on terms the other rejects, the next real decision point is after the November 3 midterms, not this weekend.

Yemen / Saudi: Sunday's barrage counted — 11 missiles over the south, and debris injuries for the first time this weekend

The Institute for the Study of War's October 25 update puts numbers on Sunday's exchange: the Houthis have fired at least 12 missiles and two drones at Saudi Arabia since October 24, with Saudi air defences intercepting 11 missiles over southern Saudi Arabia, including Asir Province, on October 25. A cruise missile aimed at Khamis Mushait was also intercepted. That corrects the morning picture of "no casualties linked": Saudi Civil Defense said debris from two intercepted missiles injured two people — a Saudi citizen and an Asian resident — and damaged three residential buildings and seven vehicles in Khamis Mushait and Ahad Rafidah. The Houthis, for their part, said Saturday they carried out seven missile and drone operations against targets including King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, Najran Airport, an airbase in Khamis Mushait, and Aramco refineries, pumping stations and the East-West pipeline, calling it retaliation for Saudi airstrikes on Yemen. Separately, Saudi Arabia's Interior Ministry warned that recording or circulating footage of interceptions or debris sites could bring legal action, and a fresh Riyadh alert was reported early Monday Saudi time. Source: ISW Iran Update, October 25, EL7.AI (Saudi Press Agency, AP), VGMG

Ukraine: a deep strike on a missile-parts plant, and the shopping-mall campaign's toll

Ukrainian strikes reached the Energia plant in Yelets, in Russia's Lipetsk region, on Sunday afternoon, according to open-source tracking — the plant makes batteries and components for onboard electronics and missile guidance, linked by analysts to the Iskander-M, Iskander-K, Zircon and Kinzhal missiles and the Pantsir-S1 air-defence system. That report is single-source OSINT, not a Ukrainian General Staff confirmation, so treat the damage assessment as unverified. Meanwhile the Institute for the Study of War's October 25 assessment confirms the other end of the exchange: Russian drone strikes on October 24–25 completely destroyed the Epicentr-K retail network's stores in four cities — Kharkiv, Zaporizhzhia, Vinnytsia and Poltava — and included a double-tap strike on emergency workers responding to an earlier hit in Kharkiv. The same assessment, citing the Financial Times, reports that the threat of strikes has cut traffic through Ukrainian ports to fewer than 10 ships passing through Greater Odesa ports in September 2026, against roughly 400 in July. Source: ISW Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, October 25, Daily Ukraine Update, October 25

Open source

  • Microduck — a tiny open-source robot duck that learned to walk. Pollen Robotics' 25 cm, 800 g biped runs Rust software on a Rockchip RK3566, driving 15 servos on a 50 Hz control loop from reinforcement-learning policies trained in simulation (MuJoCo/PPO) and exported to ONNX. It walks, rolls, picks things up and gets back up when knocked over. Apache-2.0 licensed, 10,990 GitHub stars on October 25, and on GitHub Trending that day. Source: DigiSoulHub
  • FirstTake — an open-source Claude Code tool for video shoots. Released October 25, it automates video offload through Claude Code skills: checksum verification, file organisation from shoot metadata, progress monitoring for multi-terabyte batches, and retry logic for unreliable connections. Niche, but a real workflow problem for solo videographers and small documentary teams. Source: Emergent
