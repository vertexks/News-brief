Night brief — Thursday, November 12, 2026

Houthis claim new strikes on Saudi oil and airports as the UN extends sanctions and oil jumps $3

The Houthis widened their campaign against Saudi Arabia on Thursday, claiming drone strikes on a pumping station on the East-West oil pipeline, the Yanbu oil facility on the Red Sea, and an Aramco facility in Riyadh — and on the ground in Yemen they captured two towns in a push to cut the road to Aden. The UN Security Council answered the same day by unanimously extending its Yemen sanctions for another year, and oil prices closed up $3 a barrel as the attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure escalated.

The details, as far as they can be verified, split between what the Houthis claim and what anyone else can confirm. Houthi military spokesperson Yahya Saree said on Thursday that the group had hit a pumping station on the East-West Pipeline with a drone, struck the Yanbu oil facility, and targeted an Aramco facility in Riyadh, a military cargo plane at Sharurah airport near the Yemeni border, and the Khamis Mushait airbase — where he claimed a ballistic missile "scored a direct hit." Reuters reports the pipeline and Yanbu claims, and notes Saudi Arabia did not immediately respond to a request for comment, while the Houthis did not say which day the attacks were carried out. The Saudi-led coalition said separately that its air defences intercepted suspected Houthi drones over the Riyadh region and the Eastern Province on Thursday. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) says it cannot verify the Houthis' claims, and notes the group has hit the East-West Pipeline before — a September attack forced Saudi authorities to close it for about 11 days of emergency repairs.

The ground war moved too. ISW reports the Houthis launched a combined-arms offensive in southeastern Lahij Governorate on November 11, opening with ballistic missile salvos on government staging areas at Ras al Arah and Kharaz and a strike on the government command headquarters in Aden City, then advancing on foot and in vehicles toward al Shatt and al Sarih. Government-aligned forces repelled the first push, but the Houthis captured al Shatt and the al Sarih junction on November 12, and Saudi-Yemeni government airstrikes on Houthi positions in al Shatt later that day indicate the Houthis still hold the town. ISW assesses the aim is to capture the high ground at Jabal Kharaz and cut the Mokha–Aden road, which would isolate government forces on the Red Sea coastal plain — including the units best able to defend Aden City. The Houthis also fired two ballistic missiles at government staging areas in Jawf and Marib governorates on Thursday, according to ISW, as government forces advance toward the town of al Hazm.

What each side is saying is now a straight contradiction on the shipping front as well. Iran's Revolutionary Guards claim two more large oil tankers exploded and caught fire in the early hours of Thursday after hitting mines in the Strait of Hormuz, taking their claimed toll to four tankers on fire. But UKMTO — the maritime reporting body — has logged only one strike off Oman on Tuesday, plus two projectile attacks on tankers reported by ISW, including one that hit an engine room. Reuters says it cannot independently verify the Iranian reports, and ISW assesses the mine claims are likely meant to shake ship operators' confidence in US escort operations and push vessels into Iranian waters. Meanwhile the UN Security Council voted unanimously on Thursday to extend Yemen-related sanctions for one more year — asset freezes and travel bans — while demanding an end to Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia. The Houthis again denied targeting Mecca, after Saudi civil defence said debris from two intercepted ballistic missiles fell in the Mecca region on Wednesday.

For ordinary people the stakes run through fuel prices and the cost of everything shipped. The East-West Pipeline is Saudi Arabia's workaround for a closed Hormuz, carrying about 4 million barrels a day — roughly 4% of global supply — to Yanbu on the Red Sea. Oil rose $3 a barrel on Thursday on the attacks and the Hormuz disruption, and German shipping giant Hapag-Lloyd says the strait closure is already costing it about $60 million a week in fuel and insurance. The Houthi campaign has also kept Riyadh's King Khalid airport disrupted for days, and the US Embassy still requires special approval for government staff to use Saudi airports.

In short: the Houthis claim they hit the pipeline that lets Saudi oil bypass Hormuz, plus Yanbu, Riyadh and two airbases — none of it confirmed by Riyadh — while on the ground they took two towns in a push toward Aden, and the UN answered with another year of sanctions. The verified facts are the coalition intercepts, the sanctions vote, and oil up $3. Watch for a Saudi damage statement on the pipeline and any UKMTO confirmation of the tanker claims.

US–Iran diplomacy: Trump says talks are progressing, Tehran says the strait stays shut until its seven conditions are met

Donald Trump said on Thursday that US–Iran talks are progressing and a deal to end the war could be within reach, as Tehran says it is still reviewing a US proposal delivered through mediator Pakistan. At the same time, Iran's parliament speaker said the Strait of Hormuz will not reopen until Washington meets seven Iranian conditions — and Iran, Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan agreed to keep talking, after their top military officials met in Tehran.

The details, as reported by Al Jazeera, are that Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said the US proposal remains "under review" and Tehran will send its response to key mediator Pakistan once it has "finalised its views." Pakistan's Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, a key mediator between Iran, the US and Israel, said he was "hopeful" the current momentum would lead to peace. Trump, for his part, said the conflict "will be over quickly" as Washington pushes for a deal over Tehran's nuclear programme and the Hormuz blockade. But the other side of the table is digging in: parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said in an interview on Wednesday that Washington has not formally responded to Iran's own terms, that "the Strait of Hormuz will not be opened until those seven conditions are implemented," and that Iran has proposed reopening the strait six days after the US meets the demands, with negotiations on a final agreement starting the following day.

What each side wants is still far apart. The seven conditions, as named in Iranian officials' statements, include lifting US sanctions, releasing frozen Iranian assets, ending the naval blockade of Iranian ports, compensation for war damage, and a halt to fighting on all fronts, including Lebanon. Iran's Tasnim news agency separately denied reports that Tehran had offered to reopen the strait in exchange for the US lifting the blockade, calling them "a lie and fabrication." Meanwhile the regional track moved: Iran said it will continue discussions with Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey after their top military officials met in Tehran on Monday — hosted by Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei and attended by Saudi Defence Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir and Turkish intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin — focused on a regional non-aggression network, the US–Iran negotiations, Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia, the strait and the US blockade. A senior Pakistani official told Reuters that Munir, with Trump's blessing to pursue diplomacy, had presented Iran with a proposal meant to offer something to both Washington and Tehran, with the Mecca-pact states and Qatar as possible guarantors if accepted.

For ordinary people the cost of the standoff keeps compounding. Hapag-Lloyd says the Hormuz closure is costing it about $60 million a week in fuel and insurance as companies avoid the waterway, and oil jumped $3 a barrel on Thursday on the Saudi attacks. In Lebanon, the fighting continued despite the US-brokered ceasefire: Al Jazeera reported late Thursday that at least 14 people were killed in Israeli air strikes across southern Lebanon, including 13 security personnel in a strike on a government building in Nabatieh, and that the Israeli army said it had hit 40 Hezbollah sites in the past day.

In short: the diplomacy has momentum — a US proposal on the table in Tehran, a four-country regional track, and Trump sounding upbeat — but Iran's public price for reopening Hormuz is seven conditions Washington hasn't answered, and the shooting hasn't stopped in Lebanon or the strait. Watch for Tehran's formal response to the US proposal, expected via Pakistan.

Google and Samsung's AI glasses go on pre-order — from $395, on sale December 10

Google and Samsung opened pre-orders on Thursday for their first Android XR smart glasses — called Intelligent Eyewear — with Warby Parker frames starting at $395 and Gentle Monster frames at $449 to $489. They go on sale December 10 in the United States and South Korea, with demos at more than 360 Warby Parker stores and seven Samsung Experience Stores.

The details: the glasses are display-free for now — no screen in the lens — and run on Qualcomm's Snapdragon AR1 Gen 1 chip, weighing about 41 grams with a battery lasting up to nine hours and a charging case that recharges them up to seven times. A 12-megapixel ultra-wide camera, open-ear speakers and microphones feed Google's Gemini assistant, which you summon with "Hey Gemini" or a tap on the temple. Google says Gemini can identify what you're looking at, give turn-by-turn directions through Google Maps in your ear, translate speech in real time across more than 70 languages, and handle busywork like adding something you see to a grocery list or checking your calendar from an event poster. A feature called Guided Vision gives conversational audio descriptions for people who are blind or have low vision, and an Aira integration can stream the camera feed to a trained human visual interpreter. There are 30 frame variations across the two brands — Warby Parker's Alden, Reid and Wren styles, and six Gentle Monster designs — with prescription options for most frames. They work with Pixel 7 and newer, select Samsung Galaxy phones on One UI 9 or later, and iPhone 13 Pro and newer. Google says a version with displays built into the lenses is being tested by people outside the company but gave no date for it.

What each side is saying splits on privacy, which is the whole argument about camera glasses. Google and Samsung lead with it: camera visuals from Gemini on the glasses are not saved in Gemini History and aren't used to train AI models or target ads, bystanders' faces are automatically redacted before Gemini processes them, an indicator light shows when you're recording and the glasses stop recording if the light is covered, data on the glasses is encrypted, and every pair has an off switch so they work as regular glasses. Samsung says voice and video data isn't used to train AI models by default. The open question is whether buyers and bystanders trust that — Meta's Ray-Ban glasses have already normalised camera glasses in public, and Google is clearly trying to out-promise Meta on the privacy front rather than compete on it.

For ordinary people this is the first real test of whether Google's version of AI glasses is a product or a demo. At $395 with prescription options and demos in hundreds of stores, it's priced and sold like normal eyewear, not a developer kit — but only in the US and South Korea at launch, and only if you own a recent Pixel, Galaxy or iPhone. If it lands, it's Google's first consumer AI hardware with Gemini built in from the start.

In short: Google and Samsung's Gemini glasses are real, buyable from December 10 at $395 and up, with a heavy privacy pitch aimed at the bystander problem — but no display in the lens yet, and only two countries at launch. Watch whether the December 10 launch sells out or sits, and when the display version appears.

Cyber

N-able follow-up: no confirmed customer compromises yet — but a follow-up hotfix is still coming

N-able says that as of November 12 there is no evidence any customer environment was successfully exploited through the Able2Shell flaws, though researchers counted 21 compromised servers running the new 4EverAble2Shell chain earlier in the day and scanning activity using the public proof-of-concept code is ongoing. The company's hosted servers were upgraded to Hotfix 2 (build 2026.4.1.26) by November 11, and a follow-up hotfix is in preparation for the two new exploit chains published November 11. N-able says the new server attack needs the Legacy API turned on and exposed to the internet — off by default and not recommended. Shadowserver counts 148 exposed N-central servers online now, down sharply from 1,338 on Monday. If your provider runs N-central, ask whether the hotfix is applied, whether the Legacy API is off, and what their log review found. (BleepingComputer · Field Effect · Dark Reading)
