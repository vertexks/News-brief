1. Ukraine/Russia + Poland

Missiles over Kyiv, drones over Moscow — and a freeze offer still on the table

The war's heaviest exchange in days kept rolling overnight, even as both sides kept talking about talks — and the clearest peace offer on the table is now a freeze, not a settlement. According to TBS News, reporting the October 22 overnight barrage in full on Friday morning, Russia fired at least 21 ballistic missiles at Kyiv in three waves, Ukraine sent 630 drones toward Moscow according to Russian officials, and the two sides still exchanged war dead (252 bodies handed over by Moscow, 25 by Kyiv, per Belarusian agency Belta). The fuller toll from that night, per regional officials cited by TBS: two killed and 36 injured in a strike on a market in Lozova, near Kharkiv; three killed in Zaporizhzhia; one more killed in Dnipropetrovsk; and a Russian "massive strike" on port infrastructure in the Odesa region and on vessels Russia says Ukraine's military uses.

Overnight into Friday, the energy war continued. According to an OSNT daily brief summarising the night of October 22–23, a DTEK thermal power station was struck and ceased electricity generation entirely — the latest in a campaign President Volodymyr Zelenskyy says has hit Ukrainian energy facilities more than 170 times since October 11. The same brief reports Ukrainian drones hit the 75th Arsenal military plant in Serpukhov, near Moscow, linked to Buk and S-300 air-defence systems — reported by open-source trackers matching explosion footage to the site, not yet confirmed by either government. Treat both overnight claims as developing.

The arguments about what a deal even looks like are now unusually public. Zelenskyy, in a Fox News interview on Wednesday, said he would accept a deal freezing the war along the current front line. "Yes, right now we have to freeze, and I think this is a real way to end this war," he said, adding that security guarantees could be discussed afterwards — while refusing to give up the Donbas, which he called "not fair." Russia's position, via Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov after US envoy Steve Witkoff's three-hour meeting with President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg, is talks only after November 10, possibly in Abu Dhabi, with the "nuances" from the Oslo round not entirely satisfying Moscow. A source close to the Kremlin told TBS the US proposals included Ukraine ceding Donbas in return for a NATO security guarantee — single-source, unverified, and flatly at odds with Zelenskyy's stated red line. Putin, interviewed on Russian television, confirmed he will resume US-mediated talks next month, saying the aim was security for Russia, Ukraine and Europe.

The US-Ukraine rift over Starlink is still unresolved, and both sides are now saying opposite things about it. President Donald Trump told reporters SpaceX chief Elon Musk had restored Ukraine's Starlink access, declining to say whether he had called Musk. Zelenskyy, speaking alongside Finland's prime minister, said speed on Starlink systems had not been restored, and a person familiar with the issue told TBS the curbs largely remain in place, with some drone units reaching 200 kph but "no official explanation of what's going on." On air defence, the picture is similarly contested: the Times, citing Ukrainian media, reported Kyiv could no longer shoot down ballistic missiles after running out of Patriot interceptors, while Russian state agency TASS reported Ukraine's military admitted it had not intercepted any Russian ballistic missile — both are claims from interested parties, not verified counts.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the human impact is winter arriving on a grid that has been hit more than 170 times in under two weeks, with homes, a monastery, shops, cafes, a market and a port all in the latest strike lists. For ordinary Russians near Moscow, the exchange runs the other way: two people wounded near the capital by drones, and one death in Belgorod, per Russian officials.

In short: the fighting is getting heavier even as a concrete proposal — freeze where the lines are, argue about guarantees later — is on the table from Kyiv and a talks window after November 10 is on the table from Moscow. The gap between those two positions is Donbas, and nothing overnight closed it. Watch for a confirmed talks date and venue, for any resolution of the Starlink and Patriot disputes, and for whether the energy strikes pause before winter bites. 2. Iran/US + Yemen/Saudi + Hormuz

No strike before Israel's election, says its army chief — while the missiles over Saudi Arabia keep coming

The most useful new voice on the reported post-midterm strike plan is a dissenting one from inside Israel's own military. According to the Times of Israel, citing Channel 13, Israel Defence Forces Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir said he does not currently see an operational need to strike Iran before next week's Knesset election on October 27. "We do not see any indications of a need to strike Iran before the election. If we determine that a strike on Iran is necessary, we will carry it out," Zamir was quoted as saying. That sits in direct tension with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's campaign line this week — that regime change in Iran is "the only way to end this" and his directive to the IDF, Mossad and security agencies to topple it — and with President Donald Trump saying the US "may do it a little bit further" after already inflicting significant damage on Iran. A Jerusalem Post report cited by All Israel News added that IDF sources dispute that preparations for an imminent attack, like those before the June 2025 strike, have even been ordered. None of this cancels the Times/NPR reporting of a planned early-November window, but it does mean the plan is contested inside Israel, not just in Tehran.

Overnight, the Saudi front did not pause for that debate. The National's live blog, updated Friday morning, reports coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki saying ballistic missiles launched toward Riyadh and Najran were intercepted overnight. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree, meanwhile, claimed four "qualitative" operations over the past 48 hours — the defence ministry in Riyadh, King Fahd airport in Dammam, Aramco in Yanbu and Abha airport — with direct hits and a fire at Yanbu, and said Saudi Arabia has launched more than 2,900 strikes on Yemen in 100 days, killing and wounding more than 562 people, mostly women and children. The Houthi casualty claim for Yemen is unverified, and the scale of any damage from the ministry claim remains unconfirmed, with Saudi reports emphasising interceptions. The Dammam airport death toll from Wednesday's attack has risen to two, per The National.

In the Strait of Hormuz, the fog has thickened rather than cleared. Iran's claim that seven tankers were damaged overnight — four set ablaze during ship-to-ship transfers, three immobilised by mines — still has no ship names, flags or casualty figures and no independent confirmation. Trump accused Tehran on Truth Social of fabricating hits, mines "and everything else" to drive up oil prices before the US midterms, claiming more oil is now moving through Hormuz than before the conflict. Separately, reports on Friday said an oil spill reached beaches in Umm Al Quwain in the UAE, with beaches closed and clean-up teams deployed — attributed to regional reporting, still developing, and a reminder that even unverified claims sit on top of real environmental damage. One tracker put December Brent at $114.47 on Friday, up from $113.17 on Thursday.

For ordinary people, the travel squeeze tightened again: Etihad extended its suspension of flights to Riyadh and Dammam until November 5, will run only daytime flights to Jeddah, and noted Dammam airport has reopened while Riyadh's remains closed. That is the practical shape of the war for Gulf residents and travellers: airports that open and close, flights that only run in daylight, and fuel prices that move on claims nobody can yet verify.

In short: the strike plan is real enough to be argued about at the top of Israel's military, but not so settled that its own chief of staff sees a pre-election need for it; the Houthi campaign grinds on regardless; and Hormuz remains a place where claims move markets faster than facts can catch up. Watch for Netanyahu's October 27 election, for any CENTCOM word on the claimed Sirik strike from Thursday, and for independent confirmation — or debunking — of the seven-tanker claim. 3. Tech: Apple, Nvidia, Google

Apple's first foldable goes on sale — and immediately sells out

Apple's first foldable iPhone, the iPhone Duo, officially launched on Friday, October 23, in more than 70 countries — and for most people who did not pre-order, the launch is already over. According to MacRumors, pre-order deliveries are arriving, but supply is extremely tight: shipping estimates for newly placed orders slipped to January 25 within the first 30 minutes of pre-orders opening on October 16, Apple warned walk-in stock would be limited, and US stores are limiting purchases to two per customer. 9to5Mac reports lines formed worldwide overnight — 30–40 people at 4:15 AM in Las Vegas growing to 150+ — with Apple Store workers telling disappointed shoppers to check the Apple Store app on Saturday at 10 AM ET for restock details. In Sydney, the first store to sell the phones, a queue formed before the 8 AM opening; in Shanghai, buyers queued from 6 AM.

Caption: Apple's first foldable iPhone, the iPhone Duo, shown open. It matters because this is the product Apple itself calls the most transformational change to iPhone since the original — and the one it cannot currently make fast enough to meet demand.

Caption: A queue outside Apple's Jing'an store in Shanghai on launch morning. It matters because the launch-day lines — in Sydney, Shanghai, Las Vegas and New York — are the visible proof of the supply story: demand far outrunning the limited stock Apple warned about.

The details: the Duo starts at $1,999 for 256GB, rising to $2,199 (512GB), $2,599 (1TB) and $3,199 (2TB), in Star White and Night Sky. Walk-in purchases are possible in the US, UK, Canada, Australia, Mexico and several European countries, but reservation-only in France, China, Hong Kong, India, Japan, Malaysia, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand and the UAE. Carrier credits soften the blow in the US — up to $1,200 at AT&T, $1,000 at T-Mobile and $840 at Verizon with eligible trade-in, spread over 24–36 months — and 28 more countries get the phone on October 30.

The arguments about the launch are already running. Apple's framing, via chief executive John Ternus — celebrating the launch in Austin, with marketing chief Greg Joswiak at Fifth Avenue in New York — is that this is a new category, not a Pro Max alternative, and the response has been "nothing short of extraordinary." The sceptical case, put by Bloomberg's Mark Gurman in his Power On newsletter on Friday, is that Apple made a "major mistake" starting pre-orders on October 16 knowing manufacturing constraints were severe, rather than waiting until it had more inventory. Loop Capital downgraded Apple to Hold on October 20, cutting its price target from $370 to $330, citing weaker confidence in an iPhone shipment recovery and rising memory costs as a margin risk.

For ordinary buyers, the human impact is simple: a $1,999-and-up phone that most walk-in shoppers will not get today, a January wait for new online orders, and a second chance on Saturday morning via the store app. For Apple, it is the first major launch test for Ternus, barely a month after he took over as chief executive, landing just before earnings.

In short: the Duo is real, it is selling, and it is sold out — the launch-day story is supply, not the device itself, and the first real verdict on the phone will come from the people who actually got one. Watch for Saturday's restock check, for first hands-on reviews from launch-day buyers, and for whether the January delivery window moves.

4. Open source

MOSS-Image: a 5B open image model built for design work, not pretty pictures — OpenMOSS and Mosi released MOSS-Image, a 5-billion-parameter diffusion transformer trained from scratch, with Apache 2.0 code. It is aimed at design work: a frozen Qwen3.5-9B text encoder, prompts up to 4,096 tokens, and structured JSON layout input with bounding boxes so a headline and a product land where asked. QbitAI reports 1.3 million downloads in its first four days across Hugging Face mirrors, top of the site's text-to-image trending board, first among open-weight models on the LLTB text-rendering benchmark at 96.3% coverage, and second only to GPT Image 2 in a blind design eval at 1,698.70 Elo. An 8-step distilled variant has already passed 75,000 downloads, with GGUF, INT8 and MLX builds alongside. Benchmark and download claims are from the secondary write-up, not independently verified. SiliconNoon Open Source Radar, Oct 23

MiniMax M3.1-Flash weights published — The model that went on MiniMax's API on October 19 has its weights on Hugging Face as of Friday morning: 428 billion parameters in total, 23 billion active per token, native text, image and video input, and a 1M-token context. MiniMax's own card claims 73.7% on DeepSWE v1.1 — behind DeepSeek V4.1-Flash-1017 at 77.0, ahead of Kimi K3 and GLM-5.3 — with 61.7 on OSWorld 2.0 and 82.2 on CyberGym, all vendor-run numbers. The engineering note worth reading is serving: sparse attention through every layer, quantization-aware training inside the routed experts, and a draft head for speculative decoding, which is why the checkpoint lands near 220 GB. The license is MiniMax's own, not Apache — read it before building a product on it. SiliconNoon Open Source Radar, Oct 23

Nvidia and the University of Washington release RFD4-Proteina, an open-source protein-design model — Available through Google Cloud, Hugging Face and OpenAI, per an October 23 AI-news roundup. Single source so far; the model itself is the notable part for researchers, not the announcement. TheJOAI

5. Cybersecurity

Plex2Shell and SiteGuard: federal patch deadline is TODAY — The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities deadlines for Plex Media Server CVE-2026-96654 and CVE-2026-96656, plus SiteGuard CVE-2026-106931, fall today, October 23. The KEV listing itself was reported on October 20–21; today is the day the deadline bites. Attackers are exploiting the Plex2Shell chain to deploy crypto miners and proxy malware, per The Hacker News, and the fix is Plex Media Server 1.43.3.10861 or later — a patch that has existed since August, which means victims are unpatched installs, not zero-day victims. The other Plex pair (CVE-2026-96652/96655) and Siemens Software Center CVE-2026-110936 are due November 3. If you run Plex exposed to the internet, patch today, don't wait for the deadline to pass. SecNews · CyberNetSec

Azure Kubernetes Configuration Service: a CVSS 10.0 flaw, published Oct 22 — CVE-2026-83938 is missing authentication for a critical function in Microsoft's Azure Kubernetes Configuration Service, allowing an unauthenticated attacker to elevate privileges over a network. CVSS 10.0, published October 22, not on CISA's KEV list, no public exploit reported. Check whether you run the service, then apply the vendor fix. ThreatRoundup

PHP2Shell, day after: PHP itself tops GitHub trending — php/php-src is GitHub's #1 trending repository on Friday (287 stars in the daily window, 41,292 total), a sign of how many people are now auditing the interpreter itself after this week's patches. The advice is unchanged from the evening and night briefs: patch to 8.5.12 / 8.4.27 / 8.3.36 / 8.2.35 or take the system offline; interim php.ini mitigations reduce exposure but do not make an installation safe. LuongNV GitHub Trending, Oct 23
