Morning brief — Monday, October 26, 2026 (~9 AM CT)

A Monday shaped by a decision deferred, not resolved. Trump says it is "decision time" on Iran but ruled out a strike on Sunday; mediators are in Tehran today; oil eased on the diplomacy talk but is still near six-month highs. In Saudi Arabia, the month's flight cancellations now have a hard number — nearly nine in ten Riyadh flights gone. And in Ukraine, overnight strikes hit power infrastructure again, while a slow Ukrainian push in the east is forcing Russia to pull marines and tanks off planned offensives.

World escalation

"Decision time" on Iran: Trump weighs a deal or a new bombing campaign, as mediators work Tehran

President Donald Trump said Sunday it is "getting to be decision time" on Iran: make a deal, or "finish it off." In an interview with Fox News' "Sunday Morning Futures," he put it in his own terms, as Bloomberg reported: "I have a decision to make: Do I finish it off, which would be very easy to do, or do I make a deal? That's all. We don't play games." Later Sunday, leaving the White House, he told reporters there would be no strike that day — a one-day ruling-out, not a decision, and the same line he gave the previous night.

Washington's side of the argument is that the earlier campaign did not settle the question permanently, and that the threat picture is getting worse. Bloomberg reports Trump has suggested US strikes could resume after the November 3 midterm elections and has sent additional forces, including a third aircraft carrier group, toward the Persian Gulf. US officials warn the stretch between the election and the new Congress taking office could be especially dangerous, with a negotiated deal seen as the most likely way to end the conflict. The US is also reducing diplomatic and military staffing in parts of the Middle East, Bloomberg reports, as Iran intensifies attacks on ships near the Strait of Hormuz and Houthi threats against Americans grow. An American official told Bloomberg on Friday that staffing changes go beyond Saudi Arabia, where departures were first acknowledged.

Tehran's side, as described by people familiar with its thinking, is that it is bracing for a fresh bombing campaign around the midterms and preparing to retaliate across the Middle East, including against Israel and the United Arab Emirates. Iran is also now attacking more ships in or near the Strait of Hormuz — and causing more severe damage — than earlier in the conflict, deploying guided missiles rather than attack drones, according to shipping and maritime security officials cited by Bloomberg. "All indications point to a resumption of war and a major bombing campaign, although the timing remains uncertain," Aslı Aydıntaşbaş, a Middle East expert at the Brookings Institution, told Bloomberg. Iran's own public line, repeated through the weekend, is that renewed attacks would not change its positions and would not produce a different outcome from previous operations.

The diplomacy is real but narrow. Qatari mediators travelled to Tehran on Sunday as part of a US-coordinated effort to ease tensions, Reuters reported, and on Monday top officials from the Mecca Pact countries — Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan — are set to meet Iran's leaders in Tehran, their first high-level contact since Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia became a pressure point in the wider conflict. Israel adds its own clock: Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth made an unannounced stop in Israel to meet Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu just days ahead of Israel's election on Tuesday, the first since the Hamas attacks in 2023. Bloomberg notes that if Netanyahu loses, his chief rival, Gadi Eisenkot, may not be as eager to begin his term restarting the Iran war.

For ordinary people, the immediate effect showed up at the pump and on the markets rather than in a new strike. Brent crude fell 1.63% to $114.82 a barrel and US West Texas Intermediate fell 2% to $101.19 by mid-morning GMT on Monday, Reuters reported, after Brent gained about 6% last week — but both remain near six-month highs, and analysts warned the calm may be temporary. "With the jeopardy doubled by Houthi attacks into Saudi, any Brent price dips back to the lows of last week will find grateful buyers," PVM Oil Associates analyst John Evans told Reuters. Gulf residents still face airport alerts, rerouted flights and the standing US travel advisories for the region.

In short: no strike Sunday, no deal announced, and no decision taken — but the decision window is now explicitly framed as deal-or-bombing, with mediators in Tehran on Monday, a third carrier group moving, and Washington's own assessment pointing past the November 3 midterms. Watch the Mecca Pact meeting in Tehran today and Israel's election on Tuesday for the next shift.

Sources: Moneyweb (Bloomberg), Euronext (Reuters), Oilprice

Riyadh's skies, by the numbers: 86% of flights cancelled this month, and the airport's partial reopening met a fresh Houthi claim

The Houthi campaign against Saudi aviation now has a full-month tally, and it is stark. About 86% of scheduled flights at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport — 4,071 out of 4,736 — were cancelled between October 8 and October 25, according to aviation analytics firm Cirium, as Reuters reported Monday. Across all Saudi airports, more than 7,800 flights, or 45% of scheduled departures, were cancelled in the same period, peaking on October 12, when cancellations exceeded 60% nationwide. Dammam saw 42% cancelled and Abha 46.7%, while Najran, near the Yemeni border, had all flights cancelled.

Saudi authorities partially reopened Riyadh's airport on Sunday after a halt that began on October 10, when an attack killed 13 people — the deadliest in the kingdom in more than a decade. Domestic flights resumed from Terminal 1, while national carriers were allowed to operate international services from Terminal 2. But the reopening did not hold the news cycle for a day: the Houthis said they had attacked Riyadh's airport again on Monday, calling it a "military target." Reuters could not independently confirm damage from the Monday claim, and Saudi authorities have not confirmed a successful strike on the airport in the latest claim — a gap worth keeping in mind, since Houthi claims and confirmed impacts have diverged before.

The two sides are arguing past each other in familiar terms. The Houthis present airports, ports and oil facilities as legitimate targets and retaliation for Saudi airstrikes on Yemen, including strikes on Sanaa and other areas over the weekend that they cite for the latest attacks. Riyadh's position, shown in its actions rather than a single statement, is to keep the airport running where it can — partial reopening, domestic first — while intercepting incoming missiles and drones, as it said it did repeatedly over the weekend. The airlines are voting with their schedules: international carriers have suspended services to the Saudi capital into November, Delta Air Lines has postponed the launch of its Riyadh route until next year, and several events in Riyadh have been cancelled or postponed, including Saudi Arabia's marquee investment conference.

For ordinary people, this is the war arriving as a cancelled flight, a postponed conference, and a longer, more expensive route around the Gulf. Riyadh's airport is the kingdom's second-busiest after Jeddah, and the disruption has spread well beyond the capital — nearly half of all Saudi departures gone for more than two weeks is a national transport shutdown in all but name, even with Sunday's partial reopening.

In short: the numbers confirm what travellers already felt — Riyadh's airport has been operating at a fraction of normal all month, the partial reopening is real but fragile, and the Houthis are already claiming to have tested it on its first full day back. Watch whether international carriers extend their suspensions past November, and whether the Terminal 1/2 reopening survives the week.

Sources: WNCY (Reuters), Moneyweb (Bloomberg)

Ukraine: overnight strikes hit power plants and a port again, while a slow eastern push pulls Russian marines off the attack

Russian strikes overnight hit Ukrainian cities and infrastructure again, while the front line in the east moved — slowly, but in Ukraine's favour, according to the Institute for the Study of War. A Russian strike on the southeastern city of Zaporizhzhia injured four people overnight, regional governor Mykhailo Semikin said on Telegram on Monday, Reuters reported, and two more people were injured in strikes on the Chernihiv region. Russia's defence ministry said on Telegram that it hit a power station in Kharkiv, a warehouse in the surrounding region, and another power station in the Khmelnitskiy region, plus port infrastructure in Chornomorsk and a cargo vessel en route to Odesa that it said was used for military purposes. Reuters could not independently verify the ministry's claims of what was hit, and the Ukrainian side's account centres on the civilian injuries and the power targets.

The political argument around the strikes sharpened in a Sunday interview with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He said Kyiv would halt its strikes on Russian oil refineries only if Moscow stops hitting Ukrainian power infrastructure — or, alternatively, if the United States allows the use of Starshield over Russia to detect missile launch sites, or supplies Ukraine with Patriot missiles. The US, Reuters noted, has pressed Ukraine to suspend the refinery strikes, arguing they add to high energy prices — a sensitive issue for the Trump administration amid its war on Iran and with midterm elections approaching next month. Zelenskyy's framing turns the American ask into a trade: stop the refinery campaign in exchange for either Russian restraint on the grid or the tools to defend it. Moscow has not publicly accepted either version.

On the ground, ISW's October 25 assessment reports Ukrainian forces retook three settlements near Oleksandrivka — Perebudova, Komar and Fedorivka — and advanced toward Skudne, likely pushing Russian troops out of a salient northwest of the Fedorivka-Burlatske line, though Russian forces still hold the northeastern edge of Perebudova. The pressure has already changed Russian deployments, ISW says: elements of the Baltic Fleet's 120th Naval Infantry Division and the Pacific Fleet's 40th Separate Naval Infantry Brigade were redeployed to defend the Oleksandrivka direction instead of supporting offensives toward Hulyaipole, and elements of the 90th Tank Division earmarked for Druzhkivka were also pulled in. Those are among Russia's more combat-capable formations, ISW notes, and using them defensively is a sign of strain. Elsewhere the picture is mixed — Russian forces recently advanced in the Novopavlivka direction, southwest of Filiya — and Ukraine's jet-powered drone interception rate slipped from about 77% to about 74% during the October 14–24 Starshield disruption period, though ISW also notes Ukrainian officials confirmed unrestricted Starlink and Starshield access resumed on October 24.

A third thread could matter more for civilians than either front line: the local ceasefire in Oleshky. Ukraine and Russia agreed on Friday that a ceasefire in the Russian-controlled town in Kherson Oblast would take effect on October 27, allowing aid deliveries and evacuations through humanitarian corridors. On Monday, Russia's defence ministry accused Ukraine of striking the town 18 times with artillery and carrying out more than 120 drone attacks on it and nearby roads since October 19, saying this thwarted food and medicine deliveries and stopped people leaving. Reuters could not independently verify that claim, and there was no immediate response from Ukraine, which has in the past accused Russia of not letting aid in or people out. Treat the accusation as unverified — but the ceasefire itself starts Tuesday, and whether aid actually moves will be the test.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the pattern is unchanged and grinding: power stations as targets as winter approaches, port infrastructure under threat at Chornomorsk, and a rare local ceasefire that could, if it holds, let civilians in one occupied town get food, medicine and a way out.

In short: another night of strikes on the grid and the ports, a Ukrainian advance in the east that is forcing Russia to spend its better units on defence, and an Oleshky ceasefire due Tuesday that both sides are already arguing about before it starts. Watch Tuesday for whether aid moves in Oleshky, and whether the refinery-strike argument between Kyiv and Washington hardens into a condition or a concession.

Open source

  • CleanYourTV 3.0 — the Android TV debloater gets a major update. First reported here as a viral experiment on October 19, the project has now shipped version 3.0, announced October 25: it debloats Android and Google TVs over Wi-Fi, swaps in a clean Apple TV-style launcher, and now supports 300 models, with Home Assistant lights/locks and Pi-hole pause controls from the couch, phone-based cleaning with no laptop needed, and 52 new TVs and 28 languages from 169 contributors. It passed 37,000 GitHub stars and added 3,831 stars in a single day on the trending charts. The restore script undoes every step, which is the part that makes it safe to try. Source: WCCFTech, GhTrends, October 26
  • gpu-unlock — a kernel module that restores full FP16/BF16 rates on RTX 30 cards. It loads before the unmodified Nvidia driver binds, with no patched driver or UEFI app to maintain, and user reports on the 3090, 3080 and 3070 show large BF16 GEMM and LLM prefill speedups, while memory-bound decode and INT8 stay flat and compared outputs came back bit-identical. Up 3,008 stars in a day — a genuinely useful free performance unlock for owners of last-generation cards, with the usual kernel-module caution attached. Source: GhTrends, October 26

Cybersecurity

  • Tailscale SSH flaw ("Tailworm") — high severity, no CVE and no patch yet. Security researchers from Cyberdione Labs report that an attacker who has already compromised one device on a network, and where SSH port forwarding is enabled in Tailscale SSH, can abuse connection handling to reach system sockets and read sensitive files or run commands with root privileges on other servers — without an extra password or key. The issue was rated high severity in testing October 22–24 and identified in Tailscale version 1.104.2 and Headscale 0.29.5. Tailscale has acknowledged the report and is investigating with external experts. If you run Tailscale SSH with port forwarding, watch for the patch and review which devices your access rules allow to connect. Source: ThaiCERT, October 26
  • Microsoft Authenticator for Android — patched token-theft flaw. CVE-2026-80097 (CVSS 8.6) let a malicious Android app with no declared permissions steal work-account access tokens through improper authentication in Authenticator's broker component. Microsoft classified it as a local elevation-of-privilege issue and has patched it — update Authenticator if you sign into a work account on Android. Source: Cyber Security News, October 26
  • PHP2Shell is now reported exploited in the wild. The two PHP remote-code-execution flaws patched October 21 (CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, CVSS 9.2 each) are now reported exploited, per KrebsOnSecurity via the October 26 SecureResearch brief. If you took a site offline as a precaution last week, this is the confirmation that patching — to 8.2.35, 8.3.36, 8.4.27 or 8.5.12 — is not optional. Source: SecureResearch Daily Cyber Brief, October 26
  • GitLab — new CVSS 10.0 flaw in the October 26 brief. CVE-2026-110264, an unauthenticated code execution issue in GitLab CE/EE, is listed with fixed releases including 19.5.1 and 19.4.3; internet-facing servers should be prioritised and assessed for compromise. This is a different flaw from the GitLab CI/CD issue patched October 12. Treat the brief's listing as the alert and check GitLab's own advisory for your version. Source: SecureResearch Daily Cyber Brief, October 26
  • Today is the CISA KEV federal patch deadline for Arista VeloCloud (CVE-2026-114625) and Collabora Online (CVE-2026-110447), both already reported here and actively exploited. If you run either, the grace period is over. Source: The Perimeter, October 26
