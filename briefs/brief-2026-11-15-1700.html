Evening brief — Sunday, November 15, 2026

World escalation

Iran fires drones at a US base in Bahrain as its allies roll back the Saudi campaign in Yemen

Iran launched a drone attack on a US military base in Bahrain on Sunday — its second strike on the Gulf kingdom in three days — while Saudi-backed forces in Yemen pulled back from the Bab el-Mandab Strait to defend Aden, handing the Houthis their biggest gain in weeks. The two fronts moved together: Tehran is pressing the United States directly, and its allies in Yemen are pressing Saudi Arabia, with shipping through the Strait of Hormuz caught in the middle.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards said six drones targeted a US air-defence radar at the Al-Riffa helicopter base in Bahrain, calling it a "second phase" of retaliation for what it described as hostile American actions. Bahrain sounded its first nationwide threat alert since September, urging residents to stay calm and move to the nearest safe place, and said its air defences intercepted and destroyed the incoming attacks before they could reach their objectives. Explosions were heard around 4 a.m. and again around 6:30 a.m. local time, and sirens sounded across the capital, Manama. No damage or casualties have been confirmed by either Washington or Manama, and the Guards offered no evidence for their claim that the radar was hit. Two days earlier, on Friday, the Guards said they had destroyed a US early-warning radar in Bahrain; US Central Command said it shot down two drones, with no injuries or damage. Bahrain hosts the headquarters of the US Navy's Fifth Fleet.

In Yemen, the Southern Armed Forces — the main military force fighting for the internationally recognised government in the south — announced a redeployment of its forces from Dhubab Airport and the Bab el-Mandab area, west of Taiz governorate, saying the priority was now securing the capital, Aden, and neighbouring governorates. The announcement effectively ends a six-week government counteroffensive to drive the Houthis from the mouth of the Red Sea. Yemeni military sources told Reuters the forces had fallen back to al-Suqyah, about 150 km west of Aden, while Houthi fighters are now positioned just outside Tur al-Bahah, roughly 80 km from Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city, which the Houthis have besieged. There was no immediate response from the Houthis or from Saudi Arabia. The Houthis had already captured the Red Sea coast and the strait in September; a Saudi-backed offensive last month retook positions around it but never fully cleared the area or retook the city of al-Mocha.

The Houthis, meanwhile, kept up their missile campaign against Saudi Arabia. On Sunday the group said it attacked King Khalid International Airport and a financial centre in Riyadh with two ballistic missiles, and two pumping stations on the East-West oil pipeline. There was no immediate Saudi confirmation of those claims. The Saudi-led coalition said separately that it destroyed two Houthi drones launched toward the Mecca region and intercepted another toward Medina; Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree denied the group targets Mecca or Medina, saying its operations aim only at Saudi oil facilities and military bases. The Muslim World League condemned what it called continued Houthi attempts to target Mecca and civilian areas. Riyadh has rerouted about 4 million barrels a day of crude through the East-West Pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu — roughly 4% of global supply — as Hormuz traffic stays disrupted.

In the strait itself, the Revolutionary Guards said two oil tankers were on fire on Sunday after striking naval mines, and Iranian state broadcaster IRIB reported five tankers had hit mines over 48 hours, with the ISNA news agency saying one supertanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude had sunk. Late Sunday, Iran's army navy said separately that a tanker caught fire after hitting a mine east of the strait. None of these claims has been independently verified — no vessel names, flags or owners have been given, and maritime authorities have not confirmed any ship struck a mine. One shipping-tracking desk noted that satellite fire readings showed no heat signatures at sea across 16 passes over the strait on November 13–14. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araqchi, is due to visit Oman on Tuesday for talks; Oman holds the strait's southern shore and has acted as a mediator.

Tehran's position is that vessels using what it calls unauthorised routes with their navigation systems switched off are fair game, and that it is answering American military action against Iran. Riyadh and its coalition partners say the Houthi attacks are indiscriminate — aimed at airports, holy sites and civilian infrastructure — and that their interceptions are defensive. Washington has stayed out of direct strikes, offering intelligence and targeting help instead. Reuters reported on Saturday that Trump has said in recent days he might hold off attacking Iran if the US faces no new provocations from Tehran or the Houthis, while keeping the naval blockade in place — a calculation the Bahrain strikes now test, coming weeks after the war contributed to a midterm defeat for his party.

For ordinary people, the war is now a daily fact of life across the Gulf: Bahrain residents were woken by sirens and told to shelter, Riyadh faces regular missile alerts, Saudi airports remain disrupted, and shipping crews transit one of the world's most important oil chokepoints under threat of mines. In Yemen, the human cost keeps climbing — about 300,000 people have been displaced by the latest fighting, around half of them in Taiz, where aid cannot get in and civilians cannot get out. Brent crude settled Friday at $95.84 a barrel, with futures reopening late Sunday; the weekend's claims had not yet been priced in.

In short: Iran struck directly at a US base in Bahrain for the second time in three days, its Houthi allies forced a Saudi-backed retreat from Bab el-Mandab and kept firing at Riyadh, and Tehran's latest tanker-mine claims remain unverified — while the US president's stated reason for holding off on strikes, no new provocations, is now in question.

Ukraine hits a Su-34 test airfield and a drone base deep in Russia; Kyiv counts a week of strikes topping 2,300 drones

Ukraine's General Staff said on Sunday that its forces struck the Akhtubinsk military airfield in Russia's Astrakhan region and a drone storage and launch facility at Tsimbulova in Oryol region, in the latest deep strikes of a campaign aimed at the infrastructure behind Russia's nightly drone waves. The two sides also kept up their exchange over Ukrainian cities, with Kyiv reporting four people killed and 69 injured across the country over the past day.

The Akhtubinsk airfield houses the 929th State Flight Test Centre and is a primary base for Su-34 fighter-bombers that launch guided aerial bombs against Ukrainian cities; the General Staff said the strike triggered secondary explosions at an ammunition depot storing guided aerial missile components and hit a mobile radar altimeter. The governor of Astrakhan region said 31 drones were shot down in the northern part of the region overnight. The second target, the Tsimbulova drone base, is a facility from which Russian forces launch attack drones toward Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure. The strikes follow Saturday night's hits on the Nizhnekamskneftekhim petrochemical complex and a refinery in Tatarstan and a defence plant in Bryansk, which were covered in this morning's brief. As with those, the extent of the damage could not be independently verified.

President Volodymyr Zelensky said that over the past week alone, Russian forces launched more than 2,300 attack drones, 1,590 glide bombs and 15 missiles of various types at Ukraine, and that on Sunday the national grid operator, Ukrenergo, imposed emergency disconnections in parts of Zaporizhzhia and Dnipropetrovsk after strikes damaged electrical equipment. Russian officials reported two fatalities after Ukrainian drones damaged electrical substations in Taganrog, in the Rostov region. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched 132 long-range drones overnight Saturday into Sunday, 106 of them intercepted, with hits at 20 locations — figures reported in the morning brief.

Zelensky also said a 161 km/h speed cap on Starshield satellite terminals operating over Russian-occupied territory has now persisted for five consecutive days, and that it is letting more Russian drones through because Ukraine's frontline interceptor drones cannot catch the fast, jet-powered Shaheds, which travel at 450–500 km/h. Kyiv says it will raise the issue with partners ahead of Defence Minister Yevhenii Khmara's Brussels visit and the Ramstein contact group meeting on Tuesday, November 17. Khmara also announced that Canada is funding PAC-3 Patriot interceptor missiles for Ukraine through the JUMPSTART mechanism.

Kyiv's argument is that Russia is systematically dismantling the power supply ordinary Ukrainians depend on as temperatures drop, and that the US is undercutting Ukraine's defences at the same time. Moscow frames its strikes as aimed at military and logistics targets — railways, ports and substations feeding the war effort — and says its air defences are swatting down Ukrainian drones in large numbers. SpaceX has not publicly explained the Starshield limits, and Washington has said nothing new about the dispute.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the practical effect is the same as it has been all autumn: power cuts, damaged homes and disrupted trains, with emergency blackouts now a regular feature of the week. Ukraine's deep strikes into Tatarstan, Astrakhan and Oryol are meant to raise the cost for Moscow and hit the bases launching the attacks, but they have not stopped the nightly drone waves.

In short: Ukraine hit a Su-34 test airfield and a drone launch base deep inside Russia, Russia kept hammering Ukraine's power grid with a week of attacks topping 2,300 drones, and Kyiv is heading into Tuesday's Ramstein meeting pressing allies over the Starshield speed cap and air-defence supplies.

Nvidia: Huang is weighing a comeback as allies organize a "war room" to lobby the board

Jensen Huang is considering a return as Nvidia's chief executive, days after he resigned over reports of extramarital affairs — including one with a woman described in reports as a suspected Chinese spy — the Financial Times reported on Sunday, citing people familiar with the matter. The report marks a new phase in the leadership crisis at the world's most valuable chipmaker, with the ousted founder's allies now organizing openly to get him back.

According to the FT, Huang has discussed the possibility of returning with confidants, and one ally said they were putting together a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives to lobby for his return and would try to garner support from the White House. "Everyone that matters in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen," the ally said, adding that the efforts had Huang's support. Elon Musk and Epic Games chief Tim Sweeney have already backed a public letter calling for Huang's reinstatement, and a petition urging the board to bring him back passed 135,000 signatures over the weekend. Two large shareholders, Brad Gerstner of Altimeter Capital and Gavin Baker of Atreides Management, wrote to the board on Friday urging a slow, measured search and asking it to keep Huang's potential return on the table — without calling for immediate reinstatement.

The pushback is real, though. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent on Sunday called for a broader leadership shake-up at the company, according to reports, and the board has already approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas as a possible successor, the FT reported on Friday. Nvidia has not yet hired a search firm, and interim chief executive Colette Kress — the company's chief financial officer — leads her first earnings call on Tuesday, November 17, with a company-wide meeting scheduled for Wednesday. Huang has accepted a closed-door congressional appearance before Thanksgiving and is scheduled to testify under oath before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3; House Republicans have separately asked him to sit for a closed-door, transcribed interview on December 1, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 10. The board's independent investigation, run by the law firm Wachtell Lipton, continues, and the Justice Department has not concluded whether Alice Huang — the former Megaspeed executive at the centre of the affair reports — was a spy, a claim Beijing denies.

Huang's supporters argue he built Nvidia into the engine of the AI industry, that he deserves due process while the investigation establishes the facts, and that removing him mid-crisis risks the company. The board's defenders — and the critics pushing for a clean break — argue the questions go beyond the affairs themselves: Reuters reported Huang misled Nvidia's general counsel and chief marketing officer about the nature of his dealings with Alice Huang, describing them as standard business, and the reports raise unresolved questions about exports of advanced AI chips to China. Nvidia's stock has fallen more than 30% from its October 6 closing high, wiping out more than $1.8 trillion in market value.

For ordinary investors and the wider tech industry, the stakes are immediate: Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday, its first since Huang's resignation, and the company's leadership — and with it, the direction of the AI chip business that underpins much of the industry — is now openly contested between the board, the founder's allies, and Washington.

In short: Huang is weighing a comeback and his allies are organizing to lobby the board and the White House, the board is still searching for a successor and has approached Arm's chief executive, and Tuesday's earnings will be the first public test of the company without him.

Tech products

Apple: Gurman says Face ID on the iPhone Duo 2 is a toss-up — report, not confirmed

Bloomberg's Mark Gurman said in his Sunday Power On newsletter that Apple has built iPhone Duo 2 prototypes with Face ID on the outer display, but engineers are still working to integrate it and the company is running out of time to lock in the hardware for a fall 2027 launch — so the feature may slip to the Duo 3. Gurman's four goals for future Duo models: Face ID, an Action button, a telephoto camera, and a thinner, lighter design. Apple has said nothing about the plans, and customer feedback on the current Duo won't change the hardware until the third generation. This is a roadmap report, not an announcement.

Google: Gemini's Wallet link can now read bank accounts too — opt-in, read-only

Follow-up to this morning's brief: Google's Gemini-to-Wallet connection, which rolled out in the US on Friday, November 13, also lets users optionally link bank, credit card, loan and investment accounts through the fintech firm Plaid. Gemini can read the numbers to answer questions about spending, budgets, cash flow and subscriptions, but cannot move money, pay a bill or buy anything. The feature is opt-in, off by default, English-only at launch, and limited to users 18 and older with a personal Google account.

Cybersecurity

Microsoft's November Patch Tuesday: ~1,093 fixes, one exploited Outlook zero-day that leaks login hashes — catch-up

Microsoft's November 2026 Patch Tuesday, released Tuesday, November 10, fixed roughly 1,093 vulnerabilities, 63 of them rated Critical — the second straight month over 1,000. The one that matters most is an actively exploited Outlook zero-day, CVE-2026-124194 (CVSS 6.5, rated Moderate): opening a specially crafted message file makes Outlook connect to an attacker-controlled server without the usual external-content warning, leaking the user's NTLMv2 hash, which an attacker can crack or relay to impersonate the user. The Preview Pane is not an attack vector — the file has to be opened — but CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog the same day, with a federal remediation deadline of November 24. The fix ships in the Office/Microsoft 365 updates, not the Windows cumulative update, so a Windows patch alone does not close it. A second flaw, CVE-2026-123158 in Mariner, is also in KEV. Counts vary by source (1,093 per Ivanti and Splashtop; up to 1,259 by another tally), but the exploited Outlook flaw is the same in every count.

Sources: S&P CZ Zone · Colada Technologies · SOC Prime

No new CISA KEV additions; Check Point and UnrealIRCd federal deadline is Monday

CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog still stands at 1,781 entries with no new additions since Friday, November 13. The federal patch deadline for the two newest entries — Check Point Gaia CVE-2026-123039 and UnrealIRCd CVE-2026-114713 — is Monday, November 16.

Major court cases

Russell Brand: wife speaks out from prison message; six more women have come forward

Russell Brand, sentenced on November 9 to ten years and six months for rape and three sexual assaults, sent a message from prison on Sunday through his wife, Laura Gallacher, who spoke about the conviction on the couple's weekly YouTube series, "Sunday Service with the Brands." Brand, 51, says he is innocent and is being held at HMP Littlehey in Cambridgeshire — a low-security prison holding around 1,200 male sexual offenders — after being transferred there on Friday from HMP Wandsworth. He must serve two-thirds of his sentence before parole and will remain on the sex offenders register for life. Separately, six additional women have accused Brand of sexual misconduct in incidents between 2005 and 2015 that could not be included in the recent trial, and police are reviewing reports from roughly six to eight more women, which could lead to further charges. One accuser, Mia Latter, has waived her anonymity and alleges Brand orally raped her in 2006 when she was 16. The government's independent adviser on sexual offences, Professor Katrin Hohl, said on Sunday that the public attention around the trial should prompt a proper examination of how people who report sexual offences are treated in court, including the use of false ideas about sexual assault and the directions judges give juries.
