World escalation

Netanyahu's bloc gains as the count moves on — but still no majority

Israel woke up on Wednesday to a count that has moved in Benjamin Netanyahu's favour since the exit polls, without giving anyone a government. With about 71–77% of votes tallied in the morning, Israeli media projected Netanyahu's right-wing bloc at 54 seats, Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc at 47, and Arab-led parties at 19, according to Reuters — still seven seats short of the 61 needed in the 120-seat Knesset. Later partial counts put the same split at 54–47–19 with 76.8% of ballot boxes counted, and one tracker had it at 54–49–17 with 84.66% counted. The count is not final: more than half a million double-envelope ballots, including soldiers' votes, are due to be counted from Wednesday night into Thursday evening, with definitive results expected Thursday and official results due by November 4.

The two camps are reading the same numbers in opposite ways. Netanyahu told supporters overnight that Israel must form a "strong, right-wing national government," and, according to France 24, ruled out a government backed by Arab parties. He also raised the prospect of a renewed joint move against Iran with the United States, calling it a "singular historical opportunity." Eisenkot said "the people have spoken against the continuation of Netanyahu's rule," warned against another election, and has warned Netanyahu not to use security for political purposes. Democrats leader Yair Golan went further, writing that "Netanyahu lost" — counting the opposition and Arab-led lists together. The Arab parties are the night's clear gainers: Le Monde reports their two lists could win at least 18 seats, nearly double their 2022 result, driven by record turnout among Palestinian citizens of Israel, and their leader Mansour Abbas said the turnout reflected "despair and frustration that has turned into hope."

The market reaction was swift and negative. Reuters reports the shekel weakened about 2% to 3.1185 to the dollar, on track for its worst day in 16 months, reversing Tuesday's gains that had been driven by hopes of a change of government; Tel Aviv's benchmark index fell around 3%, and yields on the 10-year government bond rose. Capital Economics' William Jackson said the initial results offered more paths for Netanyahu to remain in power, "though any government that emerged was unlikely to be robust," and added: "The next government will probably be fragile." That matters beyond the markets: until a government is formed, Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister, and if neither bloc can assemble 61 seats, another election in 2027 becomes likely.

For ordinary Israelis, the practical upshot is a longer stretch of caretaker government while coalition talks drag on — with the Iran confrontation, the Gaza aftermath and Netanyahu's own corruption trial all sitting with a government that has lost its old majority. The Arab parties, courted by neither bloc so far, now hold the balance of power in arithmetic terms, even if both main camps have ruled out relying on them.

In short: the count moved toward Netanyahu but stopped short of a majority, the Arab parties nearly doubled their representation, markets sold off on the prospect of a fragile government, and the decisive ballots — the double-envelope votes — are still to be counted. Watch Thursday's near-final result and who gets the first attempt at forming a government.

Washington tells Gulf allies to prepare for a possible post-midterms strike on Iran — but no decision yet

The United States has told several Gulf Arab states to prepare for a possible "short, intense" military campaign against Iran after the November 3 midterm elections, the Wall Street Journal reported on Tuesday, citing people briefed on the conversations. The reporting, carried by multiple outlets on Wednesday morning, says President Trump has not made a final decision but is leaning toward a major post-election strike aimed at Iranian infrastructure and nuclear sites, intended to force Tehran back to the negotiating table. The White House declined to comment on "sensitive military plans that may or may not happen," saying only that Trump "is keeping all options available and ensuring that Iran will never possess a nuclear weapon."

The reported preparations are concrete on the US side. According to the Journal's account, US forces are replenishing ammunition and readying fighter jets and bombers, a Patriot battery has been moved from Texas to the region, troops have been repositioned away from bases vulnerable to retaliation, and an additional carrier strike group and Marine expeditionary unit are expected to join the roughly 20 warships already in the Middle East. Separately, The Hill reports that US Central Command has strike options ready to present to War Secretary Pete Hegseth if the White House authorises them, and that Trump "will leave all of his options open until he has to make the final decision." Analysts quoted in that reporting warn of the other side of the ledger: Iran still holds large drone and missile stocks and can hit Gulf shipping, oil facilities and US bases, so any new round carries a high risk of retaliation.

Tehran's position, as reported in recent days, is that it wants a deal on its own terms — including over control of the Strait of Hormuz — and has threatened regional targets if war resumes. Gulf governments, meanwhile, face the immediate dilemma: prepare for a campaign they did not ask for, while diplomatic efforts involving Iran, Saudi Arabia, Pakistan and Turkey continue in parallel. The Journal's reporting notes that Trump has previously said he would not strike Iran before the midterms, and that he has weighed holding off if ceasefire negotiations were possible — but that slow progress in mediated talks has pushed him toward the strike option. One analyst, Gregory Brew of Eurasia Group, put it bluntly: "it looks pretty likely that the U.S. goes back to military strikes on Iran once the midterm elections are passed."

For ordinary people, the most immediate effect is already visible at the pump and in markets: Brent crude rose past $115 a barrel on Wednesday on the combination of this reporting and the Houthi attacks below, and Gulf states have already begun drawing down non-essential embassy staff in some posts. A short, intense campaign is not a contained one if Iran answers against shipping or oil infrastructure — the pattern of the last eight months suggests escalation spreads fast, and oil is the transmission mechanism to everyone else's fuel and food prices.

In short: the warning to Gulf allies is real and widely reported, the military preparations are under way, but the strike itself is not authorised — the decision point is after November 3, and the next signals to watch are any Trump statement, any Iranian response, and whether the mediated talks produce anything before then.

Sources: Wall Street Journal reporting via Aju Press, October 28; The Hill via Arutz Sheva, October 28; Particle News, October 28

Missiles at Taif and Aden, a refinery claim Riyadh won't confirm — and oil past $115

The Houthi war against Saudi Arabia produced another overnight round on Wednesday, and oil markets reacted before the smoke cleared. The Saudi-led coalition said its air defences intercepted a ballistic missile aimed at Taif, in the mountains east of Mecca, early Wednesday, with no casualties or damage reported from that interception, while Civil Defense briefly raised and then lifted alerts in Jeddah and Yanbu on the Red Sea coast. Separately, a Houthi missile fell within the perimeter of Aden International Airport on Wednesday, according to police cited by Eagle Intel Reports, after which Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree ordered an evacuation of the facility, alleging a Saudi military cargo plane was moving weapons through it. Yemeni government media say two Houthi ballistic missiles targeted Aden airport on October 24 as well — the fourth such attack that month.

The market-moving claim came on Tuesday: the Houthis said they struck Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery — the kingdom's largest, at 550,000 barrels a day, and a major crude-export hub — with a ballistic missile. The Wall Street Journal and Morningstar both report that Aramco did not immediately respond and Saudi authorities have not confirmed a direct hit or any damage, though one report noted a fire near Aramco facilities at Ras Tanura had been contained. The coalition did confirm intercepting three ballistic missiles aimed at the Eastern Province and four drones aimed at the south on Tuesday, with Civil Defense reporting damage from falling debris in Riyadh. The gap between claim and confirmation matters: traders are pricing the risk, not a verified outage. December Brent rose to about $115–116 a barrel on Wednesday, up roughly 1–2%, and WTI to about $101–102, after falling nearly $2 on Tuesday on hopes that talks involving Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Pakistan and Iran might ease tensions.

The two sides' accounts diverge in a familiar pattern. The Houthis present the strikes as pressure on Saudi oil and military infrastructure, and have vowed continued attacks on airports and oil facilities. The coalition presents its interceptions as successful defence and says it has struck Houthi launchers, storage tunnels and a missile depot in Yemen — claims that are unverified. Houthi-run media, meanwhile, reported Saudi airstrikes on the coastal city of Al-Mokha on Wednesday. Civilians sit in the middle of both stories: airport closures and evacuation warnings disrupt travel and aid, debris from interceptions damages buildings in Riyadh, and every unconfirmed refinery claim moves the oil price that sets fuel costs far beyond the Gulf.

In short: Taif was intercepted, Aden airport was hit and ordered evacuated, the Ras Tanura refinery strike is claimed but unconfirmed, and oil is up sharply on the risk rather than on verified damage. Watch for any Saudi or Aramco statement on Ras Tanura — that, more than the next claim, is what will move prices next.

Sources: Wall Street Journal via Morningstar, October 28; Eagle Intel Reports, October 28; Coalition account via blog report, October 28

Ukraine: a quieter night, a staged plan for Oleshky, and Kyiv building around its broken bridges

After the heavy barrage of the night into Tuesday, the night into Wednesday was quieter — but the war's logistics campaign has not stopped, and Kyiv is now planning around it. Ukraine's Air Force morning report, as tallied by one open-source roundup, counted 88 drones launched overnight into Wednesday (71 downed) and one Iskander-M/S-400 ballistic missile, with hits at 8 locations — down sharply from Tuesday night's 280 drones and up to 16 ballistic missiles. That is still a night of strikes, not a pause: the week to Wednesday morning totals 787 drones launched and at least 26 civilians killed and 261 injured in strikes reported by Ukrainian officials, according to the same roundup. Russia and Ukraine both deny deliberately targeting civilians; the figures are Ukrainian official counts and have not been independently verified.

The other track is the Oleshky operation, which now has a timetable. The local ceasefire around Russian-occupied Oleshky took effect at 3 a.m. Tuesday, was widened beyond the town to parts of the Kherson region for convoy movement, and is due to last until November 1, according to a senior Ukrainian official cited by Roic News. The plan is staged: roads are to be cleared of mines on Thursday, with the humanitarian mission itself starting October 29 and civilian evacuations planned for Friday, according to earlier reporting confirmed in the new accounts. Ukraine's human rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets says Kyiv is honouring its commitments, and Russia's defence ministry says it has stopped shelling toward Kherson to facilitate the operation. Fox News' inside account adds that the mission will be led by civilians from both countries — keeping the two militaries apart — and that four Newsmax journalists will accompany it to document residents' choices. Residents themselves, quoted in the coverage, fear they may not be allowed to return to Ukrainian-held territory, or may have their phones examined for signs of Ukrainian support; the Russian-installed Kherson head claims most want to leave for Russia, an account that could not be independently verified.

Kyiv, meanwhile, is adapting to the bridge campaign rather than waiting for it to end. Reuters reports the city plans to allocate 1 billion hryvnias (about $22 million) to build water crossings over the Dnipro — pontoons, fords or similar low structures — after Tuesday's ballistic strike closed the Southern Bridge to road and metro traffic. Mayor Vitali Klitschko said the city's defence council discussed the crossings and access roads, though he gave no further details. Kyiv has six main bridges, and October has seen repeated strikes on them, disrupting travel for hundreds of thousands of people who live and work on opposite banks. The Finnish and Norwegian leaders' Tuesday phone call to Putin, already reported, drew a familiar response: Støre said afterwards that big differences remained, and the Kremlin's position — that any settlement must account for Russia's "legitimate interests" — is unchanged.

In short: a quieter night is not a ceasefire, Oleshky's real test comes Thursday and Friday when demining and evacuations are due to happen, and Kyiv is spending on river crossings because it expects the bridge strikes to continue. Watch whether aid actually moves into Oleshky on Thursday, and whether the Abu Dhabi technical talks in early November produce anything beyond a meeting.

Open source

  • DeepSeek open-sources DeepInfer, its production inference engine — with pieces held back. DeepInfer, released October 20 under Apache 2.0, is the inference framework behind DeepSeek's production services, serving its DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash model. It ships production kernels, speculative decoding (DSpark) and deterministic sampling — but, as the project's own README states, it covers offline inference only, leaving out the KV-cache layer, prefill/decode disaggregation and load balancing a full online service needs. It currently supports Nvidia SM100 GPUs and Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs only, so it's a study and infrastructure-team release, not a drop-in vLLM replacement. Major court cases

Brand takes the stand: "No" — and says the allegations consumed his life

Russell Brand took the witness box on Wednesday at Southwark Crown Court in London, the first day of his defence, and answered his own lawyer's central question loudly and clearly: asked whether he had committed any of the alleged crimes, he said "no." Brand, 51, faces three counts of rape, three counts of sexual assault and one count of attempted indecent assault involving six women between 1999 and 2009, and denies all seven charges. Prosecutors, who completed their case earlier on Wednesday, have described him as a sexual predator; the defence case now has to answer that framing, not just deny it.

Brand's testimony so far has been about impact rather than the individual allegations. He told the jury the 2023 documentary and newspaper investigation that preceded the charges had a "devastating" effect: friends and colleagues turned against him, and, he said, not a single person would give evidence on his behalf — asked by his lawyer Ian Winter whether they would "touch you with a bargepole," he replied: "No." He said he was barred from the hospital where his son was receiving open-heart surgery after the documentary aired, and that his "entire life has been consumed by these events." On the substance, he told the court that consent is "fundamental, essential, necessary and indeed more important than sex itself," and that the first complainant had willingly had sex with him. The jury has previously been told all six complainants came forward to detectives after the 2023 documentary and related reporting — a point the defence is likely to press, and prosecutors will counter by arguing that coming forward after publicity does not undermine the accounts themselves.

The human stakes here cut both ways, and the trial's structure reflects that. For the complainants, the case is about allegations spanning a decade, now tested in cross-examination after nearly two weeks of prosecution evidence. For Brand, the defence opening is an argument that public ruin preceded — and shaped — the criminal case, and that the absence of defence witnesses is itself evidence of that pressure. Neither framing settles the charges; the jury's job is the specific counts, not the broader public argument. The judge has already amended one charge, downgrading indecent assault to attempted indecent assault for lack of evidence on the original form.

In short: Brand is now testifying, he denies everything, and his defence so far rests on the devastating personal cost of the allegations and the claim that no one will stand up for him in court. The trial continues, with cross-examination to come — that, more than the opening statement, is where the defence case will be tested.

Sources: Reuters via Duke FM, October 28; Guardian account via Suara Garut, October 28
