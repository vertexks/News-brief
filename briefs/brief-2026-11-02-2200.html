World escalation

Russian bombs kill at least 26 at a humanitarian aid centre in Zaporizhzhia

Russian aerial bombs struck a school being used as a humanitarian aid distribution centre and a nearby shelter in the front-line village of Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's Zaporizhzhia region, on Monday, killing at least 26 people and injuring five, according to Ukrainian officials. The toll rose through the day — from an initial 18 reported by President Volodymyr Zelensky, to 24 confirmed by Ukraine's State Emergency Service (DSNS), to 26 in Zelensky's Monday evening address — and officials warn it could rise further, with around 20 people still reported missing.

The details, as reported by the DSNS via Political.org, BBC News via EuropeSays, and Deutsche Welle via Augerton, describe a double strike. Russian forces first hit the school building where residents collected humanitarian aid, then dropped nine more bombs — ten in total, several of them reported as 3,000-kilogram weapons — directly on the shelter where people had taken refuge after the first strike. Among the dead are two DSNS rescue workers, Denys Konovalenko and Oleksii Sushchevskyi, both 44, and four police officers, killed after arriving at the scene. Rescue work through the evening was repeatedly interrupted by Russian drone attacks in the area, and responders searched rubble by flashlight as night fell. Komyshuvakha sits about 20 kilometres from the front line. Authorities declared Tuesday, November 3, a day of mourning across the Zaporizhzhia region.

The two sides' positions are, in this case, an accusation met with silence. Zelensky said Russian forces "could not have been unaware" that residents collected aid at the site every week, called the attack "utterly horrific," and framed it as a signal against de-escalation: the strike came the day after the six-day Oleshky humanitarian truce expired, a pause he said had created an opening for further steps toward peace. "With such killings, Russia is sending the exact opposite signal — Russia is counting on further killings," he said. The Russian military had not publicly commented on the attack as of Monday evening. Russia has repeatedly denied deliberately targeting civilians, a standing denial that does not address the specific facts of this strike. The strike itself was in Zaporizhzhia, not Kherson, so it did not technically breach the local Oleshky arrangement — but Ukraine's General Staff said Monday that Russia used that truce to rotate units, fortify positions and move ammunition, and Kyiv's broader argument is that the window the truce opened has now slammed shut.

For ordinary people, the human impact is the point of the target itself: this was not a military depot or a rail yard but the place where residents of a front-line village collected weekly aid — food and essentials for people who have stayed within range of guided bombs. The deaths of the rescue workers and police who came to help, and the drone attacks that kept interrupting the search for survivors, show how the danger compounds after the first explosion. The timing also matters for diplomacy: the strike landed eight days before US-mediated working-level talks between Ukrainian and Russian delegations are due in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11, where, according to US envoy Steve Witkoff, the agenda covers post-war security arrangements, de-escalation and humanitarian initiatives.

In short: at least 26 people are dead after a double bomb strike on an aid centre and shelter in Komyshuvakha, including the rescuers who came to help; Kyiv calls it a deliberate signal against peace, Moscow has said nothing about it, and the toll may still rise as the missing are searched for under drone threat.

Poland's court crisis deepens: Tusk demands Nawrocki swear in the judges, prosecutors get five complaints, Tuesday's assembly is the test

Poland's Constitutional Tribunal standoff moved from street confrontation to legal and political trench warfare on Monday, with both camps now accusing the other of a coup — and Tuesday's general assembly of tribunal judges shaping up as the next flashpoint. Prime Minister Donald Tusk wrote on X on Monday that the conflict "can be resolved simply and without amending the constitution" if President Karol Nawrocki recognises the judges lawfully elected by the Sejm and lets them take up their posts, accusing Nawrocki of choosing "a forced confrontation that could cost Poland dearly" and accusing PiS leader Jarosław Kaczyński of orchestrating a destabilisation campaign with the president's cooperation.

The details, as reported by Reuters, Euronews and Pollar, show how fast the crisis has formalised. On Friday night, the tribunal's longest-serving judge, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, suspended tribunal president Bogdan Święczkowski and his deputy and declared herself acting president; Nawrocki cut short a trip to Africa, returned to Warsaw, and was filmed pushing past Korwin-Piotrowska at the tribunal doors, calling the events "a forcible takeover of a constitutional body." By Monday afternoon, Warsaw prosecutors had received five formal complaints about the October 30–31 events: the PiS parliamentary club alleges Korwin-Piotrowska and two others attacked a constitutional organ by exceeding their powers, while a regional councillor alleges Nawrocki forcibly entered the tribunal and judges' bodily integrity was violated. A general assembly of tribunal judges chaired by Korwin-Piotrowska is scheduled for Tuesday, but Reuters notes its ability to decide anything depends on securing a quorum of 10 of the court's 15 judges. The European Commission has effectively sided with Tusk, with Justice Commissioner Michael McGrath affirming parliament's right to appoint judges and saying the tribunal must function in line with the constitution and EU case law; the US ambassador in Warsaw, Tom Rose, said it was not his place to interfere but expressed concern for Poland's stability, security and democratic future.

Each side's argument runs in opposite directions from the same facts. Tusk's camp says the Sejm lawfully elected new judges, Nawrocki refused to swear them in, and the tribunal under Święczkowski — appointed during PiS rule — blocked them from hearing cases, so the suspension restores a court that can function; Tusk told the nation Saturday that "a politician cannot enter a court or tribunal and give orders or intimidate judges, even if he is the president." Nawrocki's camp says the suspension itself was unlawful, the takeover was forced, and the president was defending a constitutional organ — a framing echoed by former Russian president Dmitry Medvedev's claim that a coup took place in Poland, which Tusk cited in his Monday post as evidence of who benefits from the chaos. The dispute dates to 2015, when PiS appointed judges to seats already filled, and the court has been effectively paralysed since, with nearly 1,000 pending cases.

For ordinary people, the stakes are less abstract than a judges' roster: Reuters notes the crisis could affect next year's parliamentary election and push Poland closer to the Trump administration and away from the European mainstream, while economists at Polish bank PKO BP warned Monday that a prolonged dispute could raise institutional risks and weigh on assessments of Poland's creditworthiness. The tribunal's own functioning matters too — a court that could not even adopt its 2027 budget this year now faces a Tuesday meeting that may or may not reach quorum.

In short: Tusk gave Nawrocki a simple off-ramp on Monday — swear in the Sejm-elected judges — prosecutors are now holding five competing complaints, Brussels has backed Tusk, and everything hinges on whether Tuesday's judges' assembly can muster 10 of 15 judges.

Israel: Liberman backs Eisenkot, Eisenkot rules out any Netanyahu government — but the arithmetic still doesn't add up

Israel's opposition hardened into a clearer shape on Monday, even as the numbers showed why forming a government remains difficult. Yisrael Beytenu chairman Avigdor Liberman announced his party will recommend Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot to form the next government, saying, in remarks reported by i24NEWS and Channel 12 analyst Amit Segal, that "the world will turn upside down before I sit with Netanyahu," even if offered first place in a rotation — and ruling out a minority government dependent on Arab-party support. Separately, public broadcaster KAN reported Eisenkot himself has ruled out serving in any Netanyahu-led government, even if US President Donald Trump asks him, telling a closed-door meeting: "I will not sit in a government led by Netanyahu."

The details, as reported by Clash Report, The Cradle and the Insight International/Anadolu account of the KAN report, leave the opposition one seat short of a governing majority on its own terms. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 of 120 Knesset seats on October 27, the Jewish opposition parties 48, and the Arab-led parties 19; a government needs 61. Liberman's 7 seats bring the Jewish opposition to 48 recommendations for Eisenkot, and the two Arab lists are expected to recommend him too, taking him to a projected 67 recommendations — but recommendations are not coalition seats, and Liberman refuses a government that includes or relies on Arab parties, while the Joint List says it will help replace Netanyahu but will not join a government. Opposition leaders are due to meet Tuesday to coordinate; President Isaac Herzog begins consultations Wednesday, starting with the smaller parties, and whoever he taps gets 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension, to win a confidence vote. Netanyahu, for his part, said Monday he is open to talks on a broad government but insisted he must lead it, and rejected a coalition dependent on the Arab parties.

For ordinary Israelis, the practical consequence of the deadlock is continuity without resolution: Netanyahu stays on as caretaker prime minister until a new government is sworn in, and the two live alternatives — a minority arrangement Liberman has ruled out, or a unity government Eisenkot has now ruled out joining under Netanyahu — are both blocked by the very people who would have to agree to them. The viral claim circulating Monday that Eisenkot will be sworn in on November 10 is false: that is the new Knesset's opening session, not a swearing-in.

In short: the opposition now has a single candidate in Eisenkot and a firm set of refusals from both Liberman and Eisenkot himself, but 48 Jewish-opposition seats plus Arab recommendations that Liberman won't govern on still leaves no agreed 61 — and Tuesday's opposition meeting is where that contradiction has to be faced.
