1. Ukraine/Russia + Poland

Missiles over Kyiv hours after Moscow agreed to resume talks

Overnight into Thursday, Russia launched a large combined missile and drone attack across 12 Ukrainian regions, with Kyiv as the main target — hours after Vladimir Putin agreed in principle to resume US-brokered trilateral talks. According to AP, the Kyiv strikes killed one person and injured 22, with fires in the Dniprovskyi district on the eastern bank of the Dnipro injuring six including a child; the Guardian's account puts the national toll at at least two killed and 22 wounded including four children, with later Ukrainian figures citing 34 injured across Kyiv and other regions. Tolls are still being updated, so treat the exact numbers as developing.

Caption: A Guardian photograph from Kyiv showing the aftermath of the overnight bombardment — the kind of residential and infrastructure damage reported across the capital, including homes, an ambulance station and the southern bridge over the Dnipro. It matters because the strikes landed the same night diplomacy supposedly moved forward.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said 21 missiles were fired at Kyiv in three waves from Wednesday evening, and that the southern port city of Odesa was also badly hit. The targets reported across the capital included civilian houses, energy infrastructure, an ambulance station, office buildings, a monastery and the southern bridge over the Dnipro River. Zelenskyy argued the timing showed no real change in Moscow's attitude, thanked Western partners who acknowledged that, and called for stronger air defences, Patriot interceptors and more sanctions.

The diplomatic side is where the two accounts diverge. The Kremlin's version, via foreign-policy adviser Yuri Ushakov, is that Putin agreed "in principle" to resume trilateral negotiations involving Russia, the US and Ukraine after November 10 — a week after the US midterm elections — possibly in Abu Dhabi, focused on a post-war security system. Zelenskyy welcomed the prospect of talks but was openly cautious — "We'll see what happens this time," he said shortly before air raid alerts sounded across Kyiv — and he has been pushing for negotiator-level talks no later than early November, not mid-November. President Donald Trump, meanwhile, said a settlement is "closer than ever before" and signaled he will not impose new sanctions on Russia for now.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the practical meaning is grim and familiar: another night of air raids, another round of damage to the energy grid weeks before winter, and a peace process that keeps producing meeting dates while the missiles keep flying. Ukrainian officials believe Russia's immediate aim is to degrade energy infrastructure before the cold sets in, leaving millions without light and heat. One small sign of continued cooperation: Zelenskyy confirmed US intelligence early warnings of missile launches have resumed, though the White House has not reversed a slowdown affecting Starshield, the military version of Starlink that Ukraine says it needs to intercept drones and carry out mid-range strikes. Russia, for its part, said it shot down hundreds of Ukrainian drones overnight, and local authorities in Belgorod said one man was killed and another wounded there.

In short: Moscow said yes to talks after November 10, then bombarded Kyiv the same night. Kyiv wants talks sooner and doubts Moscow's intent; Washington says a deal is closer than ever and is holding off on new sanctions. The gap between the talking and the bombing is the story — watch for a confirmed date and venue, and for whether the next night is quieter.

2. Iran/US + Yemen/Saudi + Hormuz

Thursday's Gulf escalation: Houthi claims widen, Iran claims seven ships hit, oil tops $112

Thursday morning brought a sharp new round of Gulf escalation on two fronts at once — and the markets moved immediately. Yemen's Houthis said they launched attacks on Saudi Arabia's defence ministry in Riyadh, Saudi Aramco facilities in Yanbu, and King Fahd International Airport in Dammam. Reuters, carrying the Houthi statement, notes there was no immediate Saudi confirmation of attacks in Riyadh and Yanbu — so treat those two targets as claimed, not confirmed. The Dammam airport attack itself was already confirmed Wednesday (one killed, eight injured); Thursday's claim extends the campaign to the defence ministry and the Red Sea oil terminal city of Yanbu.

Caption: A file photograph of Houthi forces used as the lead image on the Reuters report of Thursday's claims. It matters because the Houthis' claimed target list is widening from airports and oilfields to the Saudi defence ministry itself — a claim Riyadh has not confirmed.

At the same time, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy claimed seven vessels were hit near the Strait of Hormuz in the past 24 hours: four vessels allegedly involved in ship-to-ship oil transfers in the northern Gulf were struck and caught fire on Wednesday, and three more allegedly struck naval mines west of the strait. The IRGC also warned the US military that continued provocations could trigger a wider response against American bases across the Middle East. That seven-vessel figure has not been independently verified — maritime authorities had confirmed only two incidents in the relevant period, and one separately reported tanker strike near Oman had all crew safe. If the full claim were verified, it would be the largest single-day vessel toll since the Iran war began on February 28.

The two sides of this tell very different stories. Iran presents its actions as enforcement against "illegal" oil transfers and unauthorized routes, with vessels running dark treated as hostile. The US and Gulf states present it as an assault on commercial shipping and civilian infrastructure: Washington ordered non-emergency staff and families out of Saudi Arabia on Wednesday, and Reuters reports Gulf stock markets fell Thursday morning — Saudi Arabia's benchmark down 1.2% toward an eighth weekly decline, Aramco down 0.5%, Dubai down 0.8%, Abu Dhabi down 0.5% — as fresh Houthi strikes fueled fears of broader escalation. Riyadh says it has answered with strikes on missile launcher sites and intercepted many Houthi ballistic missiles.

For ordinary people, the impact is already at the pump and in the markets: Brent crude topped $112 a barrel early Thursday, up more than 2–3% on the day, near its five-month high, with WTI near $99–100. One US fuel-price analyst warned gasoline and diesel prices nationally are unlikely to fall below $4 and $6 per gallon respectively before the midterms until there is clear de-escalation between the US and Iran and between Russia and Ukraine. Shipping through the strait remains severely disrupted, with transit counts far below pre-crisis levels.

In short: the Houthis claim a wider target list, Iran claims its biggest shipping day yet, and neither claim is fully verified — but oil above $112 and falling Gulf markets show the world is pricing in escalation anyway. Watch for Saudi confirmation or denial of the Riyadh and Yanbu claims, independent confirmation of the seven vessels, and whether other Gulf US missions follow Wednesday's departure order.

3. Tech: Apple, Nvidia, Google

Google's SynthID Detector goes global — now checks rivals' AI watermarks too

Google's SynthID Detector is now available globally at SynthID.com, and it can check uploaded images, audio and video against watermarks not just from Google but from partner companies including OpenAI, NVIDIA and Kakao, with Apple support described as coming soon. Until now users could only check content via Gemini, limited to Google-made material. The catch, which Google itself states: a negative result is not proof content is human-made — it only covers SynthID partners' watermarks, not AI content in general. Why it matters: one upload point for the most common watermarked AI media is genuinely useful for spotting synthetic content, but it is a watermark checker, not a general AI detector. Pivot News

Google open-sources TorchTPU, its native PyTorch stack for TPUs

Google has released TorchTPU, the native PyTorch software stack for its Tensor Processing Units, as open source on GitHub, letting developers run existing PyTorch workloads on Google's AI chips with minimal code changes. Why it matters: Google's TPUs have traditionally leaned on JAX, a learning curve for PyTorch-native developers, and CUDA is a big part of Nvidia's moat — TorchTPU is Google's bid to make its chips feel like a drop-in alternative. Early caveat already filed: a GitHub issue reports BatchNorm running statistics not updating correctly in training mode. Finance News International

Apple/Nvidia: quiet overnight — no new launches; iPhone Duo launches Friday Oct. 23, with walk-in stock expected to be limited.

4. Open source

DeepSeek's DeepInfer tops GitHub trending — its production inference engine, now open

DeepSeek's DeepInfer, the inference framework behind DeepSeek's own production services, released for offline serving, gained ~11,700 stars in the latest trending window. It runs DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with optimized kernels (FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP), MoE expert parallelism with load balancing, and asynchronous batch scheduling, targeting Nvidia SM100 GPUs or Huawei Ascend 950 NPUs. Apache 2.0, built largely on vLLM. Why it matters: this is DeepSeek's actual production serving stack, not a demo — a serious open option for running large MoE models at scale. GhTrends

CleanYourTV: debloat any Android TV over Wi-Fi, swap in a calm launcher

Gathin23/cleanyourtv (+~4,200 stars) turns an ad-filled Android TV home screen into an Apple TV-style one: over the same Wi-Fi, its ADB scripts back up the TV, disable telemetry, sponsored rows and preloaded apps without uninstalling anything, then install a tiny launcher with a dock of four apps. A restore script undoes every step, with tested lists for Xiaomi, TCL, Sony and more. Why it matters: a reversible, no-root cleanup for the ad-heavy TV home screen — practical if your TV's interface has become a billboard. GhTrends · offhype

Also trending: free-my-tv (+~1,200 stars, fast ad-free Google TV home screen, built with Claude Code), quiver-launcher (+163, modern launcher for downloading/installing/running apps from GitHub and GitLab releases with a personal library), and itsaplan (open-source, self-hosted Linear/Plane alternative where teams and AI agents plan side by side). 5. Cybersecurity

Follow-up: PHP2Shell exploitation started days BEFORE the patches, NSA says upgrade or disconnect

SecurityWeek's Oct. 22 analysis, drawing on Amazon, Cloudflare, Censys, VulnCheck, VirusTotal and watchTowr data, finds PHP2Shell exploitation began days before the emergency patches landed — not just after. CISA's KEV catalog (version 2026.10.21) still lists neither CVE-2026-109618 nor CVE-2026-109619, and no EPSS score has been published yet. The NSA's line is blunt: upgrade or disconnect affected systems. Reminder of the scale problem: ~79–80% of PHP sites run versions that no longer receive support, so for them there is no official patch — only upgrade, isolation, or a WAF as a stopgap. Patch targets remain 8.5.12 / 8.4.27 / 8.3.36 / 8.2.35. NeoTech summary of SecurityWeek/CISA

CISA adds six exploited flaws to KEV — Plex2Shell chained in the wild, plus Siemens and SiteGuard

CISA added six vulnerabilities to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog: the Plex2Shell pair (CVE-2026-96655 authenticated SSRF + CVE-2026-96656 arbitrary file write → code execution in Plex Media Server before 1.43.3.10861), chained by attackers to breach servers — early in-the-wild targeting hit honeypots deploying a cryptominer — plus CVE-2026-110936 in Siemens Software Center (uncontrolled search path, linked to the Romulus ClickFix delivery platform; update to 4.0.9.8+) and CVE-2026-106931 in EG Secure SiteGuard Server Edition (OS command injection, flagged exploited late last week). Federal patch deadlines apply; everyone else should treat KEV additions as patch-now. BleepingComputer

FBI/CISA: SafePay ransomware has hit 600+ organizations

A joint FBI/CISA advisory warns SafePay ransomware has hit more than 600 organizations, with TTPs and IOCs published and mitigations urged: patch exploited firewall/VPN flaws urgently, apply Zero Trust, restrict admin privileges, enforce MFA, isolate compromised hosts, and keep backups attackers cannot reach. SafePay was previously linked to a 2025 attack on tech distributor Ingram Micro. TrendinTech

TeamUpdraft breach: stolen keys used to backdoor WordPress sites

A breach at TeamUpdraft exposed UpdraftCentral connection keys, and attackers used the valid keys to execute commands and install backdoors on connected WordPress sites — bypassing perimeter defenses through the remote-management channel itself. Fix: update UpdraftPlus to 1.26.13 (free) or 2.26.13 (Premium) or later, and delete UpdraftCentral connection keys from Settings → UpdraftPlus Backups → Advanced Tools → UpdraftCentral → Manage existing keys. Sites may remain at risk even after credentials are revoked — check for malicious plugins. BleepingComputer via PwnSpectrum digest

Follow-up: LiteLLM hijack — no attribution yet, full IOC list published

No threat actor has been publicly attributed for the LiteLLM 1.107.0 hijack as of Oct. 22; LiteLLM suspects GitHub tokens leaked through logs of an internet-exposed internal deployment, unconfirmed. Researchers published a fuller IOC list: litellm-1.107.0.dist-info, cook.pth / cook.py in site-packages, /tmp/remote_code_loader.lock, /tmp/.pc_once_72129118c59f5636c0bb, egress to litellm.dev/healthcheck, and the compromised container image digest. A missing marker does not prove a host is clean. Last safe release remains 1.106.2. Malware.news

6. Major court cases

Judge orders Meta to pay New Mexico $25 billion in privacy case — Meta will appeal

A New Mexico judge on Wednesday ordered Meta to pay the state $25 billion, one of the largest state verdicts against a company in US history, in a case stemming from the Cambridge Analytica scandal. A Santa Fe jury had found in September that Meta misled New Mexico residents about its data collection practices and its efforts to limit harmful content, committing more than 43 million violations of the state's Unfair Practices Act. Each violation carried a potential penalty of up to $5,000; Judge Francis Mathew's filing first calculated the statutory maximum at $219.5 billion, then reduced it to $25 billion, plus 15% annual interest until paid.

Caption: Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg, photographed at Meta Connect. The $25 billion judgment against his company is the largest penalty Meta has faced, but it will not be paid until appeals conclude — which legal experts say could take years and could substantially reduce the figure.

The two sides frame the case very differently. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez called it a warning to all of big tech, saying the state's evidence showed Meta "lied to its users, to consumers, and to people all over the world" about how much control they had over their profile information, and that the money would go to the state's education fund — "we may have paid for the construction of schools for the next 100 years." Meta's chief legal officer C.J. Mahoney countered that the ruling "makes it all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico," calling the lawsuit "a tax on speech to enforce new rules for what people can say online," and said the company is confident it will prevail on appeal. Meta has argued New Mexico did not prove any consumer was actually misled, and that a multi-billion penalty would violate due process and excessive-fines protections.

Legal experts told Reuters on Thursday that Meta has a strong basis for getting the penalty slashed on appeal — most vulnerably, whether a violation counts even without proof a particular person saw a misrepresentation or relied on it to their detriment. The state had asked for $35–45 billion; Meta had argued for roughly $3.45 billion.

For ordinary people, the practical point is narrow but important: this is a civil penalty paid to the state, not a consumer payout — Facebook users get no money from this case (the separate $725 million consumer class-action settlement already closed its claim deadline). Any benefit to New Mexicans would come indirectly, through school funding, and only if the judgment survives appeal. Meta's shares actually rose ~2% on Wednesday, as investors had priced in a large penalty after the September verdict.

In short: a record $25 billion number, a certain appeal, and a legal fight over whether 43 million violations can stand without proof anyone saw the statements. Watch the appeal — the final number, if any, is likely to look very different.
