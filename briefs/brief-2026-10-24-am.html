1. Ukraine/Russia + Poland

Overnight, both sides hit each other's war machine — and Crimea went dark

The overnight exchange was a mirror image: Ukraine says it struck three Russian defence-linked sites, Russia fired 126 drones at Ukraine, and occupied Crimea lost power after a substation attack in southern Russia. None of it touches the narrow Oleshky humanitarian deal, which is still stuck on its own start-date dispute.

The details, in order. According to Reuters, Ukraine's General Staff said its forces hit the Proton-Elektroteks plant in Oryol, the Tolyattikauchuk petrochemical complex in Tolyatti, and an aircraft at the Yeysk airfield in Krasnodar region during operations on October 23 and overnight into Saturday. Kyiv says Proton makes components for cruise and ballistic missiles, and that Tolyattikauchuk's synthetic rubber can go into solid rocket propellant. Tolyatti mayor Ilya Sukhikh confirmed damage at several industrial facilities but did not name them, and reported no deaths or injuries there in preliminary information. At Yeysk, local authorities confirmed an attack but said it caused only minor damage to two apartment buildings — a much smaller account than Kyiv's aircraft-hit claim, so the scale there is unresolved. Separately, Oryol governor Andrei Klychkov said three people were killed Friday in a Ukrainian missile strike on an unspecified civilian facility in the region; Ukraine's General Staff did not report casualties, and the reports do not establish whether the two accounts describe the same strike. Russia's Defence Ministry, for its part, said air defences destroyed 1,297 drones, one French-made SCALP cruise missile and four Ukrainian Neptune missiles over the previous 24 hours — a ministry tally, not an independently verified count.

Russia's overnight barrage, according to Ukraine's Air Force via Anadolu Agency, was 126 drones, of which 101 were shot down, with strikes recorded at 14 locations. In Kyiv, the city military administration said two people were injured after an attack damaged a private residential building in the Obolonskyi district. Kharkiv's mayor reported a drone strike on the city's Industrialnyi district. Russia's Defence Ministry said it struck a facility at the port of Chornomorsk in the Odesa region used for producing uncrewed surface vessels, and a dry-cargo ship it said was carrying supplies for Ukraine's armed forces. In Russia's Rostov region, governor Yury Slyusar said drone debris damaged a transformer substation in the settlement of Kalinin, leaving more than 170 homes without power, and one person was injured.

The Crimea blackout is the night's biggest infrastructure story. According to the Kyiv Independent, via monitoring channels and NASA FIRMS satellite imagery, an attack damaged the Taman substation in Russia's Krasnodar Krai, which supplies occupied Crimea through the Kerch Strait energy bridge. The monitoring channel Exilenova+ reported on the evening of October 23 that "the entire peninsula is without electricity." Sevastopol's Russian-installed governor, Mykhailo Razvozhaev, confirmed on Telegram that the city was without power "as a result of an enemy attack on energy infrastructure outside of Sevastopol." Local authorities later claimed power was restored in multiple cities, but the Crimean Wind channel reported renewed blackouts in Simferopol, Yalta and Alushta, and internet watchdog NetBlocks reported widespread internet outages across the peninsula. Ukraine has not commented on the Taman attack, and the Kyiv Independent could not independently verify the extent of the blackouts. A Kyiv-based outlet separately reported electricity and water restrictions imposed after the blackout, with Sevastopol trolleybus service temporarily suspended.

What each side argues has not moved, but the rhetoric sharpened overnight. In an ABC News interview reported Saturday morning, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy described the Trump administration's recent actions toward Kyiv as "not supportive," and repeated that Starlink and Starshield speed restrictions are still in effect and interceptor-missile supplies are still delayed — directly contradicting President Trump's claim that access was restored. Zelenskyy also took a swipe at US envoy Steve Witkoff, who recently praised Vladimir Putin as "a humanitarian." Separately, the Independent's live blog reports that US lawmakers have written to Elon Musk giving him until October 31 to answer five questions about his control over Ukraine's military communications, warning that personal control over a foreign country's military communications raises corporate-governance and disclosure questions. That letter is reported by the Independent's live blog, not yet confirmed in a standalone report, so treat it as developing. On the Black Sea thread from Friday night, Moldova has now strongly condemned the attack on the civilian vessel near Romania's territorial waters, saying it stands in solidarity with Romania, Ukraine and Azerbaijan and urging a halt to attacks on civilian shipping.

For ordinary people, the Oleshky question is still the one with a human clock on it. Both Kyiv and Moscow now say their forces will stop firing around Oleshky at 4 a.m. local time on October 27 — Ukraine's human rights ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets told the Associated Press the truce starts Tuesday and lasts several days, beginning with demining, then aid deliveries and evacuations — while the White House statement still gives October 29. Until the aid operation itself confirms a date, the roughly 1,700–2,000 mostly elderly residents still there wait. And the Crimea blackout is a reminder that the energy war cuts both ways: Ukrainian cities have lived through this all month, and now Sevastopol, Yalta and Simferopol are reporting the same kind of night.

In short: no breakthrough, no general ceasefire — just a grinding exchange of strikes on defence plants, ports and power grids, a still-unresolved Starlink dispute now drawing congressional questions, and an Oleshky aid operation whose start date Kyiv and Moscow agree on but Washington does not. Watch for whether Crimea's power holds through the weekend, for any Musk response ahead of the October 31 letter deadline, and for which Oleshky date the convoys actually run on.

2. Iran/US + Yemen/Saudi + Hormuz

Another missile at Riyadh, and Brent crude tops $117

Saturday morning brought another Houthi missile at the Saudi capital and another leg up in oil prices — the two threads of this crisis moving together, as they have all week.

The details first. According to the Saudi-led coalition, via TBS News and AFP, air defences intercepted and destroyed a ballistic missile launched by the Houthis toward Riyadh early on Saturday, after civil defence activated an early-warning system for residents in Riyadh, Najran and Wadi al-Dawasir. Two explosions were heard in the capital, an AFP journalist reported. Coalition spokesman Turki al-Malki said a drone was also intercepted in the Eastern Province earlier on Saturday morning. No casualties were reported from the Saturday interceptions. Tehran, for its part, has denied involvement in the Houthi attacks after Saudi Arabia accused Iran of encouraging the escalation — a denial to weigh against the coalition's attribution, which is the standard pattern in this war: coalition claims, Houthi claims, and little independent confirmation of what was hit or missed.

On oil, the numbers are now the story. Brent crude briefly rose above $117 a barrel on Friday, its highest since the war with Iran began in February, and settled at $116.72, with WTI settling at $103.27 — a 22-week high — according to market reporting summarised by ForexRev, drawing on FXStreet and Yahoo Finance data. The immediate trigger, per an Aalimi Nation report from New Delhi, was a new claim from Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy that two supertankers struck naval mines in the southern Strait of Hormuz after using what Tehran calls an "unauthorized route," leaving both vessels disabled with engine-room fires and crews abandoning ship, carrying more than 4.5 million barrels combined. None of that account has been independently confirmed, and it sits alongside the still-unverified Iranian claim of seven tankers hit on October 21–22, which President Trump publicly dismissed as fabricated, saying Iran is inventing hits to lift oil prices before the November 3 US midterms and that more oil is moving through the Gulf than before the war. What is confirmed is the direction of the market and the shipping risk: October is already the worst month on record for Hormuz shipping attacks (28 attacks, per the Reuters/Washington Institute tally reported Friday), war-risk cover for a Gulf transit is being quoted far above pre-war levels, and about a third of crossings now use the northern route under Iranian control, according to Reuters' Ron Bousso.

For ordinary people, this is the fuel-price channel, not an abstraction. Brent above $117 after settling around $112 on October 22 is the kind of move that shows up at pumps and in heating and freight costs within weeks, and it is driven as much by risk pricing and insurance as by any single confirmed sinking. The US ordered non-emergency government staff and families out of Saudi Arabia earlier in the week, and Riyadh's airport is still only partly open — so the practical picture for travellers is unchanged: postpone non-essential trips, and expect airline suspensions to keep rolling forward.

In short: no newly verified Houthi hit on a Saudi target Saturday morning — the missile was intercepted, by the coalition's account — but the oil market is now pricing this crisis at its worst level of the war, and Tehran's unverified tanker claims are doing as much work as confirmed strikes. Watch for any independent confirmation of the two claimed supertanker minings, for Egypt's parliament vote on military support on October 26, and for whether Brent holds above $117 or retreats.

4. Open source

VoxCPM2: a 30-language open voice model you can host yourself — OpenBMB shipped VoxCPM2, a 2-billion-parameter tokenizer-free text-to-speech model under Apache 2.0: 30 languages with no language tag needed, 48kHz output reconstructed from a 16kHz reference clip, trained on more than 2 million hours of multilingual speech, running in roughly 8 GB of VRAM with a real-time factor near 0.30 on an RTX 4090. The notable feature is voice design — describe a gender, age, tone and pace in plain language and it invents a voice with no reference audio — and LoRA fine-tuning from as little as 5–10 minutes of audio. The commercial terms are the headline: Apache 2.0, no revenue cap. Caveat from the write-up itself: voice-design output varies between runs, so generate a few takes. SiliconNoon, Open Source Radar, Oct 24

5. Cybersecurity

SafePay ransomware has hit 600+ organisations — and it destroys backups first — Catch-up, not overnight news: a joint advisory (AA26-293A) from CISA, the FBI, the US Secret Service and German and UK law enforcement, released October 20, says SafePay has compromised at least 600 organisations across critical infrastructure sectors since August 2024, across North America, South America, Europe and Australia. SafePay is unusual in running as a non-affiliate operation — no outside affiliates, tight operational control. Initial access comes through brute-forced VPN credentials (often via Mullvad VPN exit nodes), phished account profiles, and automated exploitation of five known CVEs on internet-facing devices, including CVE-2024-40711 (CVSS 9.8, Veeam Backup and Replication deserialization). Once inside, it uses living-off-the-land tools (PsExec, WinRM, RDP), harvests credentials, exfiltrates with 7-Zip/WinRAR and Rclone — and then factory-resets NAS devices, deletes shadow copies and kills backup agent processes before encrypting, so victims have no recovery path. Mitigations: patch those five CVEs, enforce MFA on remote access, and keep backups isolated and offline. Why it matters now: it was not in this week's briefs, and its backup-destruction playbook is the detail that changes incident planning. GBHackers · Decryption Digest

A certificate authority quit the TLS business after its private keys turned out to be trivially guessable — Catch-up: Indian CA eMudhra announced October 22 that its board has voluntarily withdrawn from the global SSL/TLS certificate business, after a researcher showed the private keys behind eight of its browser-trusted intermediate certificates were so weak they could be calculated from the public certificates — one key was literally the number 4, and researcher Scott Helme recovered all eight on a laptop in under a second. The keys came from a flawed key-generation ceremony in April 2025; Google, Mozilla, Apple and Microsoft blocked the affected certificates between October 17 and 20, and fixes have shipped in Go, Python, Node.js, Java and major Linux distributions. eMudhra says no end-entity certificates were issued from the affected intermediates, its infrastructure was not compromised and no data was breached, and it will keep revocation services running until all issued certificates expire or are replaced — offering Sectigo-issued replacements. Software that never checks revocation remains exposed until updated. Computerworld · Cyber Kendra

PHP2Shell exploitation attempts now number in the millions, per Cloudflare — New detail on the already-covered PHP flaws (CVE-2026-109618/109619): Cloudflare has reported millions of exploitation attempts, and the FBI's October 23 public service announcement still stands — patch, or take unpatched PHP systems offline. A third PHP flaw, CVE-2026-115560 (session serialisation config), affects only a specific configuration. SecureResearch Daily Cyber Brief, Oct 24
