World escalation

Russia could fire 6,500 jet-powered drones at Ukraine in November — and the strikes are already intensifying

Russia could launch up to 6,500 jet-powered Shahed-type drones at Ukraine in November, a twelvefold increase from about 530 in August, Ukrainian Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi said on Monday in an interview with Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty. The warning landed on a day when Russian strikes kept hitting Ukrainian energy and industry, the EU hit Moscow with fresh sanctions, and Kyiv heads into a pivotal week of defence talks — while the question of whether Ukraine will even be discussed at Thursday's Trump–Putin–Xi summit in Shenzhen is still unresolved.

The details, as reported by RFE/RL and the Associated Press: the jet-powered Shaheds are faster and harder to intercept than the older propeller-driven drones, and Ukraine intercepted only about 55% of the 2,730 jet-powered drones Russia launched in September, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky. Koretskyi said the 6,500 estimate is based on the current pace of attacks, and that the government is working to protect energy facilities and equip critical infrastructure with backup power while preparing to repair what cannot be protected. On Monday alone, Russia launched 158 long-range drones at Ukraine, 77 of them jet-powered, causing outages in six regions. A Russian strike on Dnipro killed at least one person and injured three, another hit a critical industrial facility in the Vinnytsia region, and drone attacks on energy facilities in Cherkasy left consumers without power. Ukraine's first deputy energy minister, Artem Nekrasov, said Russia carried out 116 deliberate attacks on Ukraine's energy and oil-and-gas sectors in the past week, with 161 incidents damaging infrastructure — down from 128 attacks and 219 incidents the week before, but still leaving hundreds of thousands without electricity in Zaporizhzhia and Dnipropetrovsk on Monday. Zelensky said Russia launched more than 2,300 drones, 1,590 glide bombs and 15 missiles in the past week.

The diplomacy is moving in parallel. The EU imposed sanctions on Monday on nearly 50 Russian banks and financial institutions, plus businesspeople linked to the energy sector and the oil trade, while EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas said of the peace talks: "We do not see that at the moment. We see that Russia is already escalating." Kyiv's priorities this week are the Ramstein Group meeting in Brussels on Tuesday — led by the German and British defence ministers, with Ukraine pressing for more Patriots and NASAMS — and a Coalition of the Willing gathering in Berlin, where Chancellor Friedrich Merz said more than 20 heads of state and government are expected to agree funding for Ukraine's defence in 2027 and a unified position for peace talks. Zelensky called the Berlin meeting one that "could be historic." Meanwhile the Shenzhen summit is now confirmed by all three sides: China confirmed on Monday that Trump, Putin and Xi will meet on Thursday, after Washington and Moscow had already announced it. But the agenda is openly disputed — US officials say Ukraine is not on the trilateral agenda and no agreement is expected, while Trump told reporters on Monday before leaving for Asia that Ukraine was one of the subjects he intends to discuss with Xi and Putin. The White House says the meeting will focus on nuclear arms reduction and Iran; the Kremlin has signalled Putin wants to discuss a Ukraine settlement.

Each side is telling a different story about where this is heading. Kyiv's argument is that the refinery and long-range drone campaign is a justified response to Russia's refusal to de-escalate and its strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure — Zelensky has said Ukraine disabled more than 55% of Russia's refining capacity, a figure Reuters could not independently verify. Moscow's argument is that its defences absorbed the biggest Ukrainian drone attack of the war overnight into Monday — 1,336 drones claimed downed — and that the campaign changes nothing. Washington's position is split in public: Trump has called the refinery strikes "a disgrace," while his envoys say further talks between Moscow's and Kyiv's delegations are expected, with no date announced.

For ordinary people, the war is now measured in blackouts and heating as winter approaches. The International Energy Agency warned Ukraine could face electricity shortages this winter depending on the intensity of attacks and the weather, and noted that more than 30% of Ukrainian households rely on district heating — so power cuts push families toward electric heaters and higher demand. In Russia, fuel shortages have returned to at least half a dozen regions. Zelensky warned on Sunday that the Russian ballistic missile threat "remains very serious" and that intelligence shows new strikes are being prepared.

In short: Russia's jet-drone campaign is scaling up sharply — potentially 6,500 in November — while Ukraine heads into Tuesday's Ramstein meeting and a Berlin summit of allies asking for more air defences, and Thursday's Shenzhen trilateral is confirmed but still unclear on whether Ukraine will even be discussed.

Iran claims the southern Hormuz lanes are "fully mined" — but nobody has confirmed it, and oil fell anyway

Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy is now reported to be saying the southern shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz — the ones along the Omani coast that the United States has said are safe and open — are closed and fully mined. The claim, relayed by Saudi outlet Asharq News and picked up by CBS News on Monday afternoon, has not been confirmed by anyone: the Guard itself has issued no statement echoing it, no maritime agency has verified mines in those lanes, and no shipowner has confirmed a halt in traffic. Markets treated it as unconfirmed — Brent crude fell nearly 3% on Monday — but the claim adds a new layer of menace to a shipping war that already has tankers burning.

The details: earlier on Monday, the Guard claimed six tankers had hit mines since Sunday, up from earlier counts, and Iranian state media said two tankers struck mines on Monday leading to explosions — but as before, no vessel names, flags or casualty figures were given, and the claims could not be independently verified. The one incident a neutral agency has confirmed is the UK Maritime Trade Operations report from Monday morning of a tanker hit by an unknown projectile near Oman, causing an engine-room fire with no casualties — already reported in the morning brief. In Yemen, Saudi Arabia's military coalition said it intercepted two Houthi drones launched toward the Mecca and Riyadh regions on Monday. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi travelled from Islamabad to Muscat on Monday as mediation efforts continued, and in Jerusalem, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu warned during a meeting with Czech Prime Minister Andrej Babiš that "Iran's missiles, its terrorism and its nuclear ambitions do not stop at Israel's borders. Europe is not watching from the sidelines. Europe is a target."

Tehran's argument, as stated by its officials, is that vessels sailing with tracking switched off on unauthorised routes are fair game, and that the blockade is a direct threat to its economy and sovereignty. Washington's position, as framed by US officials and the IEA, is that the economic squeeze is working: Middle East exports through Hormuz have largely recovered to pre-war levels, and the IEA said on Friday the world oil market will return to surplus in the fourth quarter — about 1.7 million barrels a day — as exports recover faster than demand. The US has also said its forces cleared mines from the main shipping routes, and three carrier strike groups are now in the region.

For ordinary people, the disconnect between the claims and the market is the story: despite the mine claims and the tanker fires, Brent fell $2.76 to $93.08 a barrel by midday Monday and WTI dropped to $86.15, with Goldman Sachs forecasting Brent averaging $88 next year and slipping to $78 by December 2027. That means cheaper fuel at the pump in the near term — unless a confirmed mine strike or a halt in traffic changes the picture. The thing to watch is a statement from UKMTO, US Central Command or a ship operator confirming or denying mines in the southern lanes.

In short: Iran is now reported to claim the southern Hormuz lanes are fully mined, but the Guard has issued no statement of its own, no agency has confirmed it, and oil prices fell on recovering flows — treat it as a claim by one side in a war until a maritime agency, the US Navy or a shipowner confirms it.

Cybersecurity

Barracuda breached through a vendor key nobody retired — and AI agents are now hunting for AI keys

Barracuda Networks says an attacker got into about 10 of its cloud accounts over 44 hours on November 1–2, and the company spotted it on November 4. The way in was a cloud access key Barracuda had given an AI company for an earlier project: the project ended, the key stayed live, and it ended up in a config file in that vendor's GitHub repository, where the attacker apparently found it in October. Barracuda put it bluntly: "We did not retire the key when the vendor ... finished that work. That is on us." From there the attacker chained credentials through Barracuda's data analytics setup, source code repositories and Kubernetes clusters, hunting for cryptocurrency secrets like private keys and seed phrases, trying to keep access with a container that mounted the host's file system, and trying to create a new cloud admin account, which was blocked. Barracuda matched the attacker's labels, probing and hunting routines to ARTEX, the open-source AI pentesting tool the FBI warned about last week. The attacker reached a system holding customer integration credentials for Barracuda XDR; Barracuda found no sign those were taken but asked some customers to replace them anyway. If you run anything with vendor keys: pull a list of every cloud access key and service account, and disable anything tied to a vendor, contractor or project that has finished.

The same tooling is showing up at scale. Amazon's threat intelligence team ran decoy servers pretending to be popular LLM services, and found attacker-run AI agents — assessed with moderate confidence to be running on ARTEX, operated by separate China-linked groups — hunting for internet-facing AI gateways and app builders to steal the AI provider keys inside them. Targets included One API, New API, Veloera, Sub2API, LiteLLM, Dify, FastGPT, Langflow, RAGFlow, Open WebUI, LobeChat and Ollama, using default admin credentials like LiteLLM's default master key, half-finished installs anyone could claim, admin pages with no login, and secrets left in JavaScript and environment files. Stolen keys were tested and pooled, then used to run the models doing the searching. Google's Threat Intelligence Group published a related report the same day, saying attackers are running multi-agent frameworks such as ARTEX, CAIRN, CyberStrikeAI and PentAGI during live intrusions, and using LLMs to compare patched and unpatched code to turn a fix into a working exploit faster. Google expects simple, mostly automated attacks within about three months. The practical takeaway for anyone running AI gateways: get admin and API pages off the open internet, change default keys, finish or delete half-done installs, and rotate provider keys.

Sources: Carthage Electronics / BleepingComputer / The Hacker News / Amazon Threat Intelligence / Google GTIG

CVE-2026-99288 (NoisyTCP): remote Linux kernel crash, no login needed

Researcher Hyunwoo Kim disclosed NoisyTCP, an 11-year-old bug in the Linux kernel's TCP code. An unauthenticated attacker can send crafted TCP traffic that races SYN cookie handling against socket hashing and ends in a kernel panic — a denial of service, no code execution and no data changes. It hits nearly every distro and works against machines running Nginx, Apache or an SSH server with default settings. The fix was merged into mainline about two weeks ago; Red Hat rates it Important with a draft CVSS of 7.5. No reports of it being used in attacks yet. Watch your distro's security tracker for the kernel update that names CVE-2026-99288, install it and reboot — public-facing web and SSH servers first.

Sources: Carthage Electronics / BleepingComputer

Log4Shell is still opening doors — this time on an old vCenter server

Kaspersky's ICS CERT team wrote up a months-long intrusion at a Russian software developer by a group it assesses as Ukrainian. The attackers came in through an exposed SSH service, then broke into a VMware vCenter 7.0.3 server still vulnerable to Log4Shell (CVE-2021-44228, CVSS 10.0). That gave them root, then the hypervisors, GitLab, Ansible, the domain controller and finally the server used to sign the company's software builds. They hid a backdoor in /etc/cron.hourly/ under the name "pgcache". The target and the politics are far from home, but the path is not: an unpatched vCenter and the same admin password reused across domains. vCenter 7.0 left general support in 2025 — if you're still on it, plan the move to 8.x.

Sources: Carthage Electronics / The Hacker News

Major court cases

Russell Brand: Downing Street calls his prison message "clearly appalling" — Ministry of Justice investigating

Downing Street said on Monday that Russell Brand's prison message — read out by his wife Laura Gallacher on the couple's YouTube programme on Sunday, in which Brand said "I am not guilty for the offenses I have been jailed for, so something strange is happening" — was "clearly appalling" and is being urgently investigated as a possible breach of prison rules, which bar prisoners from making statements that cause distress to victims. No finding has been announced. Separately, Justice Secretary Alex Norris urged the Bar Standards Board on Monday to address the use of rape myths by defence barristers, following criticism of arguments in Brand's trial — barristers who use rape myths could face practice bans — and Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy is meeting leading figures from media, arts and sport today to press for protections for young staff and freelancers against harassment. Brand was sentenced on November 9 to 10 years and six months for one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault; no appeal has been filed, with the windows running to December 3 for conviction and December 7 for sentence.
