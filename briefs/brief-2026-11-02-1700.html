World escalation

Ukraine's refinery war is fully back on — Samara oil hub hit for the fourth time in a month

Ukraine's campaign against Russian oil infrastructure, paused for about two weeks under pressure from Washington, is now unmistakably resumed — and Monday brought a fresh strike deep in Russia's Volga region. Ukrainian drones hit the Samara Linear Production and Dispatch Station (LPDS) near Prosvet overnight, the fourth attack on the facility since early October, with NASA satellite imagery analysed by Bloomberg showing a relatively fresh fire at the crude pumping and storage site. The station is a major hub for receiving, storing and blending crude from Tatarstan, Western Siberia and other producing regions into export-grade fuel, including for Russia's armed forces, according to Ukraine's General Staff.

The details, as reported by OilPrice/Bloomberg, the Kyiv Independent and Ukrainian monitoring channels, show a campaign that restarted Friday and has not let up since. The Novokuibyshevsk refinery in the Samara region — one of the Volga's leading refineries and part of Rosneft's Samara group — was hit Friday, the first Ukrainian strike on a Russian refinery since October 15, according to the Wall Street Journal. Over the weekend, Ukrainian drones hit a Rosneft refinery in Bashkortostan and Lukoil's Kstovo (NORSI) refinery in Nizhny Novgorod, where the governor reported "significant damage" to an industrial facility and the regional government urged motorists to "buy fuel only as needed." At Samara, residents of Prosvet reported explosions, footage showed smoke rising from the facility, and the regional governor confirmed air defences were responding to a drone attack without confirming the locations hit. Russia's Defence Ministry said it downed 212 Ukrainian drones overnight across 16 regions, Crimea and the Black and Azov seas — a figure that, like all ministry claims, is not independently verified. The extent of damage at Samara itself is also unconfirmed; the satellite fire is an anomaly, not a damage assessment.

The political argument around the strikes sharpened Monday too. Senior Ukrainian officials confirmed to the Kyiv Independent that Kyiv never agreed to pause strikes on Russian oil sites on October 31 — undercutting the framing of President Donald Trump's "energy ceasefire," which he claimed to have brokered in mid-October. Trump has pressed Kyiv to halt the refinery strikes ahead of Tuesday's US midterm elections, blaming them for record diesel prices; his administration accuses Ukraine of contributing to a diesel crisis that sent US retail prices to record highs in September. Moscow's line, via Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak, is that the strikes are adding to global fuel-market imbalances. Kyiv's position, stated repeatedly, is that it supports a full ceasefire on energy targets — including its own battered power grid — but that Moscow is unwilling to agree to one.

For ordinary people, the most direct measure is at the pump: OilPrice reports the US national average retail diesel price at $6.29 per gallon as of November 2, up from $3.68 a gallon at this time last year — a year ago there was no Middle East war restricting crude and fuel flows from the Gulf. In Ukraine, the other side of the same exchange continues: Russian strikes on energy infrastructure left hundreds of thousands of households without power over the weekend, and Monday's strikes in Kherson (see below) show the front-line war did not pause either.

In short: Ukraine has now hit at least three refineries plus the Samara oil hub since Friday, Kyiv says it never agreed to the pause Trump claimed, and with US diesel at $6.29 a gallon the strikes are now a domestic American political issue as much as a Ukrainian military one — one day before the midterms Trump cited when demanding they stop.

Doha has a draft, oil plunges more than 7% — but Washington still hasn't answered, and the clock runs out Tuesday

The diplomacy track produced its most concrete movement yet on Monday — and the oil market moved first, hardest, and furthest. Iranian negotiators left Doha on Sunday, but not in a walkout: an official briefed on the talks told Reuters a draft proposal for a deal with the United States is now being circulated, with a follow-up meeting possible. Hours later, crude collapsed: Brent settled near $104.77 a barrel, down 7.6%, and US West Texas Intermediate fell 7.4% to $96.46, back below $100, on the eve of the US midterm elections.

The details, as reported by Reuters, the Associated Press and Iranian officials, show a negotiation that has narrowed to a specific, still-unaccepted offer. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Monday the basis of the talks remains Iran's seven-step plan drawn up in September — the US ends its naval blockade of Iranian ports, Iran reopens the Strait of Hormuz, Washington grants oil-sale waivers and cancels new sanctions, and the sides then move to the nuclear file and other issues — but now condensed so it could be implemented in two days rather than the original seven. The AP adds two conditions not in the morning's coverage: ships would use a route agreed by Iran and Oman and travel under Iranian supervision, and Tehran wants a ceasefire that includes Lebanon. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi stayed in Doha to exchange messages with Washington via Qatari mediators. On the Iranian side, President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei has granted the government additional authority to pursue the talks, and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said a deal is possible if Washington "demonstrates goodwill." Trump, for his part, told reporters Monday that Iran wants to "make a deal and that could happen," while repeating that the US would not strike before Tuesday's vote.

The case for scepticism is not hard to find, and it comes from Tehran itself. Reports Monday note Khamenei has ordered the armed forces to update their target lists and be ready for a geographically unrestricted response to threats — the posture of a leadership preparing for talks to fail, not just succeed. Baghaei himself said Tehran does not expect the US elections to "give birth to a new US," and stressed no final agreement can begin, nor the strait be reopened, unless the blockade ends and Iran's demands are met. The American side has given no public answer to the draft, and the previously reported fallback — a 72-hour post-moratorium strike blueprint drawn up by US and Israeli officials, reported by the New York Times and Axios on October 22 — remains live once Trump's self-imposed no-strike pledge expires with Tuesday's vote.

For ordinary people, the day's oil move is the most tangible link between Doha and daily life: a 7%+ drop unwound a large part of October's war premium, which had pushed Brent to a six-month high — but nothing physical has changed yet. No tanker has moved through a reopened strait, the weekend's mine and projectile incidents in Hormuz remain unattributed, and Goldman Sachs still raised its December Brent forecast to $105 (from $90) on supply risk, warning of $140 in 2027 if Gulf output stays depressed. If the draft becomes a deal, fuel and heating costs could ease; if it collapses after Tuesday, the same prices could run the other way just as fast.

In short: Doha produced a circulating draft and a two-day reopening offer with Iranian supervision of an Oman-agreed route, oil plunged more than 7% on the hope, and Trump says a deal "could happen" — but Washington has not accepted anything, Tehran is simultaneously updating its target lists, and the strike moratorium expires Tuesday, so the whole thing now hinges on an answer that has not come.

Tech products

Apple beats in Ternus's first earnings — a record $114.5 billion quarter, with a margin caveat underneath

Apple reported fiscal Q4 2026 after the close Monday — the first earnings under new CEO John Ternus — and beat Wall Street on both lines: revenue of $114.5 billion, up 12% year on year, against a consensus of about $113.4 billion, and diluted EPS of $2.10, up 14%, against about $1.99 expected. iPhone revenue hit a September-quarter record of $57.58 billion, up about 17%, Services reached an all-time high of $31.57 billion, up about 10%, and Mac rose to $10.79 billion. For the full fiscal year, revenue reached $479 billion, which Apple called its best fiscal year ever, with $28 billion in operating cash flow and $28 billion returned to shareholders; the board declared a $0.27 dividend payable November 16.

The details, as reported by Apple itself, AppleInsider, the Motley Fool and Zacks, give the beat two complications worth knowing. First, the stock fell 1.83% to $338.32 into the results amid a broader tech sell-off, so the beat landed after a bad session, not a celebratory one. Second, gross margin of 47.9% was down from 50.1% the prior quarter, reflecting rising component costs — and it included a favourable impact of about one percentage point from tariff refunds, worth about $0.05 of the $2.10 EPS. That refund benefit is real money, but it is not obviously recurring, so the underlying margin trend is softer than the headline suggests. Third, the quarter ended September 26, so it captures only the opening weeks of the iPhone 18 cycle — and none of the iPhone Duo, which went on sale in October. Ternus called the Duo "the most transformational iPhone since the original" and pointed to the home lineup and "all-new Siri AI" as the holiday-season story; the real demand test for all of that lands in next quarter's report.

For ordinary people, the practical read is simpler than the market reaction: Apple just posted its biggest September quarter ever on the strength of iPhones and services, its installed base hit a new all-time high, and nothing in this report changes prices or products you can buy — but if you are watching whether the iPhone Duo is a genuine hit or a niche experiment, this report does not answer that yet. Next quarter does.

In short: a record quarter and a clean beat in Ternus's debut, with margin pressure and tariff refunds muddying the quality of the beat — and the iPhone Duo verdict still to come.

Cybersecurity

  • Binance — reserve proof now verifiable, next phase Tuesday: Binance completed its zero-knowledge proof-of-reserves verification Monday morning, so holders of the ten fully backed tokens (BTC, ETH, USDT, BNB, SOL, USDC, XRP, USD1, RLUSD, ZEC) can now verify balances 1:1 against the October 31 snapshot; about 95% of the $6.3 billion stolen is covered, the rest in less-liquid tokens still being sourced. The exchange says it will begin progressively restoring withdrawals for additional networks and assets on Tuesday, with another reserves snapshot Wednesday. Outflows are easing: DeFiLlama put 24-hour net outflows at about $3.29 billion, down from an earlier ~$7 billion peak. Co-CEO Richard Teng warned Monday about impersonation scams offering "priority processing" of withdrawals — Binance says it never charges to speed one up or contacts users first. Sources: The Block, Crypto Times on the scam warning, Bitcoin.com on the proof timing
  • Palo Alto — PHP flaws now exploited against firewalls: Palo Alto Networks says the PHP flaws added to CISA's KEV list last week are being actively exploited against PAN-OS firewalls and Panorama management interfaces exposed to the internet. If you run either, patch and — more importantly — do not leave the management interface internet-facing. Source: RunbookLab KEV roundup, updated November 2

Major court cases

  • Russell Brand — defence closing concluded Monday, jury goes out Tuesday: Defence barrister Ian Winter KC finished his closing speech at Southwark Crown Court on Monday, urging jurors to separate Brand's sexual behaviour from the question of crime: "It is not criminal to treat women unkindly before, during or after sex. It is not, I am afraid, criminal to be disrespectful or rude or acting like a crazy idiot. If there is consent it is lawful." He argued Brand "genuinely believed" the women were consenting, that there is "no evidence" he is "some kind of psychopath who rapes people and thinks it's normal," and that the complainants were "thoroughly contaminated" by the 2023 Channel 4 Dispatches documentary and Times/Sunday Times reporting that prompted them to come forward — leaving a "quagmire of doubt and inaccurate recollection." Brand, 51, denies three counts of rape, three counts of sexual assault and one count of attempted indecent assault against six women, spanning 1999–2009. The judge's legal directions come next, with the jury expected to begin deliberating Tuesday, November 3; unanimous verdicts are required at this stage. Nothing here is a finding of guilt. Sources: AAP/PA via AAP News, BritBrief, BlazeTrends (Variety/Sky News)
