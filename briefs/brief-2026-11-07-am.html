Ukraine: Russia fires 36 ballistic missiles at Kyiv overnight — hitting the Northern Bridge and the substation linking two nuclear plants to the capital

Russia launched its biggest overnight barrage on Kyiv in days just hours after Friday night's missile attack, firing 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones at the capital and 12 other regions overnight on November 6–7. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said all 36 missiles were aimed at Kyiv and the surrounding region, and several of them hit the Northern Bridge across the Dnipro — the crossing Zelenskyy warned on Friday was being targeted. Kyiv mayor Vitali Klitschko closed the bridge for repairs and described the city's power, heating and water situation as "extremely difficult," with temperatures near freezing on Saturday morning.

The details show how deliberately the grid was targeted. Ukraine's Energy Ministry said the strikes damaged a substation in Kyiv region that carries electricity from the western Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear plants to the capital and central Ukraine — the transmission lines, not the reactors themselves, but the link the whole region depends on. Energy facilities were hit in at least three districts of the Kyiv region, and substations were also damaged in Vinnytsia, Zhytomyr, Rivne, Kharkiv and Dnipro oblasts, with wider outages in Kyiv, Zhytomyr, Kharkiv, Donetsk and Dnipropetrovsk. Ukrainian air defences reported intercepting 149 of the drones and four of the missiles, with impacts recorded at 24 locations. Two people were killed and 37 injured across Ukraine over the past day, including six injured in Kyiv and a child among them; one person was killed in the Kyiv region and another in Sumy, where a drone strike killed a 52-year-old man.

The two sides describe the same night in opposite terms. Zelenskyy's argument is that Russia is deliberately preparing for Tuesday's talks by bombing the infrastructure it would be negotiating over — "Russia is preparing for trilateral talks by launching ballistic missiles at Ukraine's critical infrastructure," he said — and he is pressing allies for more air defence and tougher sanctions. Moscow's defence ministry said it carried out a massive strike on military-industrial, energy and transport targets, claiming it hit the Northern Bridge because it was being used to move weapons, the Meridian plant because it says it produces Flamingo cruise missiles, plus a power plant, a substation and a munitions facility in the Kyiv region and a substation in Zhytomyr. Russia also claimed it shot down 411 Ukrainian drones overnight; Russian media reported Ukraine hit the Saratov oil refinery, and a fire broke out at the Proton-Electrotex plant in Oryol — neither independently confirmed.

For ordinary Ukrainians, this is now the pattern of the winter: the strikes follow the November 3 barrage that shut down three of DTEK's five thermal power plants, and Zelenskyy said this week that Russia has damaged every power plant in the country. Kyiv's mayor and the central government are still trading blame over who failed to prepare the capital. The next real test is Tuesday, November 10–11 in Abu Dhabi, where Ukrainian, Russian and US negotiators sit down for the first time since February — with Zelenskyy saying Kyiv's priorities are halting strikes on energy and critical infrastructure, opening Black Sea grain shipments and expanding prisoner exchanges. Nothing in the reporting so far suggests the talks' date has changed because of the attack.

In short: Russia fired 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones at Ukraine overnight, hitting Kyiv's Northern Bridge and the substation carrying power from the Rivne and Khmelnytskyi nuclear plants to the capital — 2 killed, 37 injured, and Kyiv facing serious trouble with power, water and heat as temperatures hover near freezing. The Abu Dhabi talks on Tuesday go ahead as planned, but the bombing hasn't slowed.

Iran: US carries out new limited strikes on Qeshm Island — while Trump says talks are making progress and his own advisers split three ways

The US military carried out what it described as limited new airstrikes in southern Iran on Friday night, and President Donald Trump confirmed them himself to reporters — saying a "minor" action targeted an Iranian air defence system on Qeshm Island in the Strait of Hormuz. "They built a little bit of an anti-aircraft device and we knocked it out in about 15 minutes, maybe five," Trump said. Iranian state media reported two missiles hitting the Suza area of Qeshm Island, and the IRGC claimed its forces shot down a US MQ-9 drone over the island — a claim US Central Command did not confirm. It is the first US strike since the Democrats' midterm wins on Tuesday, and follows another round of what the US called defensive strikes on Monday around Qeshm, Hengam and Bandar Abbas.

The strike landed in the middle of a genuine split inside the administration over what comes next. According to Axios reporting picked up across US outlets, three camps are pulling Trump in different directions: Vice President JD Vance and envoy Steve Witkoff argue for a negotiated settlement, saying renewed fighting may not improve the outcome; Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu argue Iran can't be trusted to make or honour a deal and that force should finish the job; and a third group — Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and CIA Director John Ratcliffe among them — advises holding a "no war, no deal" status quo of naval blockade and economic pressure. Trump told Axios he is "in no rush" to decide, after telling reporters in the Oval Office on Thursday that a decision was coming "soon" and that he would "rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I will have to." NBC reported advisers briefed him on a roughly eight-week window — before a new Congress takes office in January — for restarting major combat operations, though a briefing on a window is not an order to strike.

Tehran, for its part, is talking tough and holding its line. Army spokesman Brig. Gen. Mohammad Akraminia warned on Saturday that Iran's armed forces are stronger than before the war began in February and could target American military bases "without geographical limitations" if Washington attacks again, on the orders of Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei. Iran's position remains unchanged: no negotiations and no reopening of the Strait of Hormuz until the US meets its seven conditions — ending the blockade, lifting sanctions, unfreezing assets, war compensation and ending hostilities across the region. An Israeli intelligence specialist quoted by Euronews warned that Tehran is likely to expand any new war from the outset, targeting Gulf states it sees as a pressure point on Washington, and preparing Hezbollah, the Houthis and Iraqi militias for involvement.

At the same time, the diplomacy track is still alive. Trump insisted on Friday evening that "we are not fighting" and that "a lot of progress" has been made with Iran through Qatar's mediation — "We're dealing with them now. They want to make a deal." Qatar rushed to deny reports the talks had collapsed, and its prime minister said at an Oxford event on Friday that "we have worked with colleagues in the US administration who have very genuine intentions to find a way to contain the situation and de-escalate it." Two Middle East officials told AP that Iran is reviewing new Qatari proposals on the nuclear program, though Tehran remains unwilling to negotiate its atomic activities until the US agrees to its core conditions. Trump called Qatar's Emir Sheikh Tamim on Friday night to discuss de-escalation, and Defence Secretary Hegseth spoke with his Qatari counterpart the same day. CENTCOM also announced a reward of up to $20 million for information on Iran's nuclear program.

For ordinary people, the stakes are visible at the pump and on the markets: Brent crude settled at $98.32 a barrel on Friday, down roughly 12% on the week as traders bet on a deal, but flows through the Strait of Hormuz remain below 60% of capacity and tanker attacks are still running at near-record pace. US diesel reserves are below 100 million barrels for the first time in 20+ years, with diesel around $6.27 a gallon — about 75% higher than a year ago. The date to watch is Monday, November 9: a new moon over the region, meaning minimal moonlight — conditions commanders prefer for air operations.

In short: US forces carried out limited strikes on an Iranian air defence system on Qeshm Island on Friday night — the first since the midterms — while Trump says negotiations are making "a lot of progress" and Qatar insists the talks haven't collapsed. Inside the White House, three camps are split between a deal, a return to major combat, and holding the blockade-and-pressure status quo. Iran warns any new attack would draw retaliation against US bases with "no geographical limitations." Watch Monday's new moon, a date commanders have flagged as favourable for operations.

Yemen / Saudi Arabia: 30 killed at a crowded market in Taiz as both sides blame each other — and a Houthi cruise missile is intercepted over Riyadh

An attack on a crowded market in southwestern Yemen on Friday morning killed at least 30 people and wounded eight more, in what would be one of the deadliest strikes since fighting resumed between the Houthis and Saudi-backed government forces. The strike hit the al-Hadara market in al-Waziiya district, Taiz governorate, at around 7 a.m. local time, when the market was full of shoppers. Both sides agree dozens were killed or injured — and each blames the other, with neither account independently verified.

The two accounts come from interested sources and directly contradict each other. The Houthi-run health ministry in Sanaa says Saudi aircraft carried out three airstrikes on the market, killing at least 30 civilians and wounding eight, and called it a "horrific crime," demanding an independent international investigation. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the warplanes took off from Saudi Arabia's Khamis Mushait air base and left Taiz province around 8 a.m. — though AP noted he provided no evidence — and warned the attack "will not go unanswered and unpunished." The Yemeni government says the Houthis themselves fired a ballistic missile at the market; government forces spokesman Col. Majed al-Nazili called it a war crime, and the government-run Taiz local authority said its operations room confirmed no military aircraft carried out airstrikes in the district that day. The Saudi-led coalition has not commented on the market attack. AP's first version of the story carried at least 20 dead and 15 wounded before the toll rose to 30, and the Houthi health ministry warned the death toll could rise further.

The market attack came during one of the most intense rounds of cross-border fire in weeks. On Saturday morning, the Saudi-led coalition said its air defences intercepted and destroyed a Houthi cruise missile aimed at the Riyadh region — the second attempted strike on the capital in as many days, after a drone was intercepted over Riyadh late Friday evening. On Friday morning, Saudi defences also shot down two ballistic missiles targeting the southern province of Najran and a drone near the Mecca region. The Houthis have not publicly claimed responsibility for the Riyadh launches, and the interception claims have not been independently verified — no imagery or debris has been published.

For civilians in Yemen, the picture keeps getting worse: the UN's humanitarian office reported on Friday that more than 323,000 people have been displaced since the escalation began in August, with Taiz governorate taking in most of them, and the UN Human Rights Office has counted 281 civilian casualties since August — 69 killed, 212 injured, more than a third of them children. What to watch: whether the Saudi-led coalition responds to the market attack, whether the UN or Red Cross gains access to the site, and whether either side produces physical evidence outsiders can examine.

In short: at least 30 people were killed at a crowded market in Taiz on Friday, with the Houthis blaming Saudi airstrikes and the government blaming a Houthi ballistic missile — neither account verified. The same day, Saudi defences intercepted a cruise missile aimed at Riyadh, a drone over the capital, and two ballistic missiles toward Najran. More than 323,000 Yemenis have been displaced since August.

Cyber: overnight ransomware claims — N0n leaks 217,000 Higginbotham documents; Nightspire claims 500GB from Meller Group

Nightspire claimed 500GB exfiltrated from UK firm Meller Group (financial records and employee accounting data) and listed IMD Group as a victim. Whitearmor claimed a hit on Maersk — about 100GB from its OCF / LF Logistics platform, including 29,297 driver records, 16,093 with plaintext passwords. All three are unverified leak-site claims; no victim has confirmed them. Sources · QPulse · HookPhish
