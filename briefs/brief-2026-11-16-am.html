World escalation

Ukraine launches its biggest drone attack of the war — and hits the Crimean Bridge

Ukraine struck the bridge linking Russia to annexed Crimea overnight into Monday as part of what Russian defence ministry data showed was the largest Ukrainian drone attack since the full-scale war began in 2022 — 1,336 drones claimed downed across 16 regions, surpassing the previous record of 1,254 on October 29. The Kremlin called the bridge damage minor, but the barrage, aimed at Moscow, refineries and the peninsula's lifeline, shows Kyiv's long-range campaign is still escalating days before a Trump–Putin–Xi meeting in Shenzhen.

The details, as reported by Reuters and AFP on Monday morning: Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said around 600 drones were launched at the Moscow region alone, 191 of them brought down. Sergei Aksyonov, the Russian-installed governor of Crimea, confirmed on Telegram that "the enemy has hit the Crimea bridge," with road traffic restricted until around midday and an emergency headquarters set up in Kerch. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov later said the damage was minor and traffic was continuing; Russia's transport ministry said rail traffic kept running as normal, though four trains to and from the peninsula were delayed. AFP reported the strike appeared to cause only minor damage, while the Ukrainian monitoring channel Crimean Wind claimed an impact on one of the arches of the navigable section — neither account independently verified.

The refinery campaign continued alongside it. Fires broke out at four industrial sites in Azov in the Rostov region, and at an industrial facility in Ilsky in the Krasnodar region — home to an oil refinery — where falling drone debris injured six people, including children. One person was killed in Taganrog. President Volodymyr Zelensky said late Sunday that Ukrainian strikes had knocked out more than 55% of Russia's refining capacity; Reuters could not independently verify that figure, but two industry sources told Reuters last week the renewed attacks had cut refining capacity by about 30%, and Russian officials said fuel shortages had returned to at least half a dozen regions, though without a repeat of the summer's hours-long queues at filling stations.

Russia kept hitting Ukraine overnight too. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched 158 drones, 77 of them jet-powered, into the country on Sunday night into Monday morning; defenders downed or suppressed 131, with impacts at 19 locations. Russian strikes hit energy, industrial and transport infrastructure in Vinnytsia, Kyiv, Cherkasy, Odesa, Kirovohrad, Dnipropetrovsk and Zaporizhzhia regions, with fires at several sites. Russia's defence ministry separately said it struck a cargo ship in the Black Sea that it claimed was carrying military cargo for Ukraine.

Each side is telling a different story about what the exchange means. Kyiv's argument, implicit in the scale of the operation, is that hitting refineries and the bridge degrades Russia's war machine and gives Ukraine leverage — Zelensky has framed the refinery campaign as the pressure that makes Moscow negotiate. Moscow's argument is that its air defences absorbed the biggest attack of the war, the bridge damage was minor, and the campaign changes nothing on a front line that has stayed largely static for months; Putin has previously vowed retaliation "twice over" for refinery strikes. Washington's position is more complicated: Trump has publicly called the refinery strikes "a disgrace," and US officials have said the Ukraine war is not expected to be a focus of this week's Shenzhen meeting between Trump, Putin and Xi Jinping. UN Secretary-General António Guterres, speaking at the ASEAN summit in Manila, called for an immediate ceasefire while acknowledging it was hard to be optimistic.

For ordinary people, the war of attrition is now measured in fuel queues and blackouts on both sides. Russian motorists in at least six regions are facing fuel shortages again; in Ukraine, energy and transport sites were hit across seven regions overnight, and Kyiv heads into Tuesday's Ramstein meeting pressing allies over the Starshield speed cap on its drones and air-defence supplies. The Abu Dhabi talks last week — the first direct peace talks in nine months — agreed a major prisoner exchange but produced no breakthrough on territory, and Russia still holds about a fifth of Ukrainian land.

In short: Ukraine's biggest drone barrage of the war hit the Crimean Bridge and set refineries burning, Moscow says its defences held and the damage was minor, and both sides are still escalating while diplomacy stalls ahead of the Shenzhen summit and Tuesday's Ramstein meeting.

Hormuz mine campaign widens: three more tankers hit, Iran bars nuclear inspectors, third US carrier on station

The Strait of Hormuz mine campaign escalated again overnight. Iran's state TV said on Monday that three oil tankers caught fire after hitting naval mines in the strait, and the Revolutionary Guards' navy said mines have damaged six tankers since Sunday, with pollution now affecting the strait's coastlines. A British maritime monitor independently confirmed a separate projectile strike on a tanker on Monday morning. Meanwhile Tehran said it will not let UN nuclear inspectors visit war-damaged sites until the conflict ends, and the US now has three aircraft carrier strike groups in the region.

The details: Iran's state TV report, quoting the Guards' navy and the army, warned shipping companies that a US-supported route through the strait remains dangerous because of mines. It gave no vessel names, flags or casualty figures, and no shipowner or flag state had confirmed the sinkings or fires by Monday morning. Separately, the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre said a tanker was hit by an unknown projectile while sailing out of the strait at about 05:58 GMT on Monday, causing an engine-room fire; no injuries were reported. The vessel was identified in other reporting as the Panama-flagged On Phoenix. A third tanker reported being approached by a skiff carrying four armed people about 430 nautical miles east of Socotra. The New York Times reported that the US and Israel have drawn up plans for possible renewed strikes on Iran's economic infrastructure, and the USS Theodore Roosevelt entered Central Command waters on Sunday, joining the George H.W. Bush and George Washington already on station.

Tehran's tone hardened on two fronts. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said Iran "will not wait for the US to attack first" and that UN inspectors will not be allowed into facilities damaged in the war until the conflict ends. That follows Sunday's statement from Iran's parliament speaker that if Washington "makes another mistake," Iran's armed forces will determine "the geography of the battlefield." In Yemen, the Houthis accused Saudi Arabia of two strikes on Monday that killed three people, including a child — a drone strike on a house in Sanaa's al-Sarar quarter that wounded seven, and airstrikes on a fuel station in Harf Sufian, north of the capital, that killed two and wounded eight. Riyadh made no immediate comment. Israel's military said it struck a weapons shipment from Syria intended for Hezbollah near Janta in northeastern Lebanon — the first time in months it has said it struck deep inside Lebanon. And in Bahrain, a court sentenced 64 Shiite clerics to life imprisonment over an alleged IRGC-linked plot, with rights groups alleging torture and due-process violations in the mass trial.

Each side's argument is now openly stated. Tehran says vessels sailing with tracking switched off on unauthorised routes are fair game, that the blockade is a direct threat to its economy and sovereignty, and that it will not wait to be attacked. Washington's position, as framed by CNN over the weekend, is that the economic squeeze is working — Gulf oil exports are recovering, prices have fallen from their peaks, and Iran has been unable to export meaningful amounts of its own crude for more than two months — so time is on America's side. Riyadh and its coalition partners say the Houthi attacks are indiscriminate, aimed at airports, holy sites and civilian infrastructure, and that their interceptions are defensive. The Gulf states have asked Trump to hold off broader strikes on Iran.

For ordinary people, the shipping war is now a daily fact across the Gulf: crews transit one of the world's most important oil chokepoints under threat of mines, Saudi airports remain disrupted, and in Yemen about 330,000 people have been displaced by the renewed fighting. Brent crude settled Friday at $95.84 a barrel; Monday's claims had not yet been priced in when the brief was written.

In short: Iran claims three more tankers hit mines and says it won't wait to be attacked first, while a British monitor confirmed a real projectile strike on a tanker, the US now has three carriers in the region, and Tehran is shutting UN inspectors out of its damaged nuclear sites — the standoff is tightening, not easing.

Tech products

Nvidia: the comeback talk is real, the board isn't buying it yet — and earnings land Tuesday

The Financial Times reported on Sunday that Jensen Huang has discussed returning as Nvidia chief executive with close allies, who have formed a war room to lobby the board and seek White House support. The report landed days after Huang stepped down, and it is now the central question hanging over the company's first earnings report without him, due Tuesday after the market closes.

The details: the FT report, citing people familiar with the matter, is unconfirmed — Nvidia has not commented. The "Stand With Jensen" petition passed 160,000 signatures over the weekend, including more than 7,500 people identifying as Nvidia employees. Prediction markets are unconvinced: Kalshi traders put Huang's return at about 17%, up from 9% on Saturday, behind Arm's Rene Haas at 24% and Nvidia's Ian Buck at 19%. On the other side, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has urged Nvidia to hire an external CEO and demanded a response within 10 days on whether seemingly unlawful China sales boosted revenue. Interim CEO Colette Kress — who is also staying on as CFO — has cut the company's data-centre guarantee to OpenAI from $250 billion to about $105 billion, according to Dolphin Research.

The numbers tell the other half of the story. Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter results Tuesday at about 4:20 p.m. ET, with a call at 5 p.m. Analysts expect about $108.7 billion in revenue, just above the company's own $108 billion forecast, and the options market implies a move of about 7% either way — roughly $270 billion of market value. The quarter ended October 25, before the report that US intelligence suspected Huang's wife Alice of gathering information for Beijing and before Huang's departure, so the headline numbers will probably be fine; what matters is the fourth-quarter forecast, where analysts expect about $124.9 billion, and whether the board gives any signal on the CEO search. Shares closed Friday at $162.85, down about 33% from the October peak, with the market cap down to roughly $3.9 trillion from about $5.8 trillion in early October.

Each side's argument is stark. Huang's supporters — including Elon Musk, Epic's Tim Sweeney and Y Combinator's Garry Tan — say the company lost its soul when he left and only he can run it. The sceptics — SkyBridge's Anthony Scaramucci, analyst Dan Ives and Bessent — say this is not a scandal a board can wave through: an FBI counterintelligence review, DOJ chip-smuggling probes, a Wachtell investigation and a relationship with a direct report mean no board can reinstate a CEO it is still investigating. Trump has suggested former Apple CEO Tim Cook for the job.

For ordinary people, the saga matters well beyond Nvidia shareholders: the company's chips power most of the world's AI infrastructure, its spending commitments shape the data-centre buildout, and its stock sits in countless pension funds and 401(k)s. A 7% earnings move either way on Tuesday would be one of the largest single-day swings in market history in dollar terms.

In short: Huang is talking about a comeback and his allies are lobbying hard, but the board, the White House and the prediction markets all say it probably won't happen — and Tuesday's earnings, the first without him, will be judged on the forecast and the CEO search, not the quarter's numbers.

Apple: Health AI research study launches

Apple announced the Apple Health AI Usage Study on November 15, a virtual research study run with OpenEvidence, a clinical AI platform used by healthcare professionals. It will evaluate how people trust, understand and actually use conversational health AI tools, with the aim of informing how Apple designs health AI features. The study is open to up to 200,000 eligible participants aged 21 and older across the United States. First features in scope are real-time translation, writing tools and photo cleanup — Siri AI is not included. This is a research study, not a product launch.

Cybersecurity

Six unpatched flaws in Danfoss fridge controllers allow remote takeover — the supermarket hack has a root cause

Six critical vulnerabilities in the Danfoss AK-SM800A refrigeration controller let remote attackers take unauthorized control of the hardware without any login, according to Dutch security firm Midnight Blue, reporting via The Hacker News. The flaws persist in the latest firmware, 5.4.0, released in November — researchers note the firmware was likely built October 29, before the second wave of attacks and before their vulnerability reports. Government cybersecurity agencies in Germany, Belgium and Ireland have advised operators to disconnect the controllers' management interfaces from the public internet entirely. This is the same controller family behind the October supermarket refrigeration attacks across Europe, where controllers were forced into defrost and freezer temperatures reportedly hit 50°C. If you run AK-SM800A controllers, get their management interfaces off the internet now — there is no patch yet.

Sources: GazeWire / The Hacker News

Coles investigating cyber incident; no evidence of data theft so far

Australian supermarket giant Coles told the ASX on Monday, November 16, that it is investigating a cybersecurity incident, working with the Australian Cyber Security Centre and global security experts. The company has not said what kind of incident it is, when it was detected, or which systems are involved. "Based on the findings of our investigation to date, we have not identified any evidence that customer, team or supplier data has been compromised or removed," Coles said. Stores and online shopping are operating normally; the only disruption identified is a small number of Liquorland click-and-collect orders. The disclosure comes two weeks after the European supermarket refrigeration attacks — no link has been suggested.

Sources: Computer Weekly

Check Point PHP2Shell flaws exploited in the wild since late October

Check Point Research documented attacks against Check Point Security Gateways that began in late October, about a week after patches were released, exploiting the PHP2Shell remote code execution flaws CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. Attackers also used a privilege-escalation flaw in the DynamicCLI interface (CVE-2026-123039) and dropped a web shell, then a proxy, a wiper and other components. Check your gateways and web applications for web shells — that is the generic test for compromise. The federal patch deadline for CVE-2026-123039 is today, Monday, November 16.

Sources: SANS Internet Storm Center

Cursor post-mortem: Claude Code extension compromise started with an exposed credential

Cursor published a post-mortem on the compromise of the official Anthropic Claude Code extension in the Cursor marketplace. The intrusion started with a credential exposed at Fireworks.ai that led to customer communications containing additional credentials. Worth reading if you use Cursor or the Claude Code extension — supply-chain attacks keep starting with one leaked credential.

Sources: SANS Internet Storm Center

Luxor bitcoin mining platform hit by AI-enabled attack

Luxor says attackers compromised its Commander mining-management tool over the weekend and redirected customer computing power to another mining pool, in what the company called a sophisticated AI-enabled attack. Initial reports suggested about 64,000 mining machines (13 exahashes per second) were redirected to the OCEAN pool on Saturday; Luxor has not confirmed those figures and says just over a dozen customer accounts using Commander were affected. Luxor says it patched Commander within two hours of detecting the unauthorized changes and began restoring machines to their original pools, paused most feature development, and pledged full compensation to impacted customers. A Bitcoin address linked to the incident later received two OCEAN payouts totalling about 0.162 BTC, roughly $10,500.

Major court cases

Russell Brand moved to HMP Littlehey; wife reads his "I am not guilty" message

Russell Brand has been transferred from HMP Wandsworth to HMP Littlehey in Cambridgeshire, a category C training and resettlement prison for men convicted of sexual offences — one of the largest such facilities in Europe. He is expected to serve two-thirds of his 10-year-and-six-month sentence before release on licence and will remain on the sex offenders register for life. His wife Laura Gallacher confirmed the move on their joint YouTube podcast on Sunday, reading out a message from Brand: "As you know, I am not guilty for the offenses I have been jailed for, so something strange is happening." Separately, six more women have come forward with new allegations of sexual misconduct in a report by The Times and the Sunday Times, alongside Netflix's documentary "Russell Brand: Open Secret." The Metropolitan Police has passed investigations into two more rape allegations to prosecutors for a charging decision.
