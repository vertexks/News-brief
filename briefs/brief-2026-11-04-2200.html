World escalation

Russian strikes force Ukraine's nuclear plants to cut output as Kyiv's water comes back — and Zelensky briefs US envoys ahead of Abu Dhabi

Ukraine's Energy Ministry said on November 4 that Russian strikes forced the country's nuclear plants to reduce output, Crisis.Zone reports, without naming the plants or saying how far output fell. The ministry said the return to nominal levels will be gradual, "as soon as energy workers restore the networks damaged by the shelling." The Kyiv Post's explanation, carried in the same report, is that strikes on substations and transmission lines — not on reactors — are what force the plants to throttle back, a pattern Kyiv is now framing as a European nuclear-safety issue.

The details behind that cut are stark. Energy Minister Denys Shmyhal said Russia used 289 drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles in the overnight attack of November 2–3, which grid operator Ukrenergo described as one of the most extensive on the energy system; energy facilities were hit more than 20 times in 24 hours. Three of DTEK's five thermal plants stopped generating, according to Reuters, and Shmyhal said Russia directed three drones at a substation critical to the Khmelnytskyi Nuclear Power Plant — a step he said Ukraine reported to the IAEA. Russia's Defence Ministry, by contrast, claimed it hit four power plants and two substations, according to the Institute for the Study of War — a claim that describes the same campaign from the attacker's side and does not address the civilian outages. By Wednesday morning, Ukrenergo had cancelled emergency outages in Kyiv and most regions, returning them to scheduled hourly cuts, but Kharkiv region stayed on emergency cuts and some regions faced rationing of up to 16 hours a day.

For ordinary people, the human impact is in the taps and the heating as much as the lights. Most of Kyiv went more than 24 hours without running water after the November 3 strikes cut power to water facilities — the Kyiv Independent called it the most serious water outage in Kyiv since the war began — before the city water utility said on November 4 that all pumping stations were operating normally again and network pressure had stabilised. Warming centres were set up in the hardest-hit Kyiv districts, a day after the city began connecting residential buildings to heating. On the other side of the front, Russian-installed authorities in occupied Crimea said Ukrainian strikes caused widespread emergency outages on November 4, affecting Sevastopol, Simferopol and Kerch, with a Sevastopol schedule of two hours of power followed by six without — though officials also cited an external technical incident, and the primary cause could not be independently verified.

Diplomatically, Wednesday filled in the run-up to Abu Dhabi. President Volodymyr Zelensky had a "very detailed" discussion with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, the Independent's live blog reports, covering air defence — including Patriot interceptors — and Ukraine's proposals, which he said have been given to the American side. The European Commission's Wednesday session in Brussels, EuropeSays reports, was about refining scenarios for Ukraine's post-war economic development and prosperity, following up on earlier Miami talks, with a separate security-guarantee session due Thursday. Zelensky said another Brussels meeting takes place Thursday. ISW's assessment, carried by Crisis.Zone, reads the Russian campaign as aimed at "inflicting increasing costs on Ukrainian society and coercing Ukraine into capitulating over the winter" — which is exactly what the nuclear-output cut, the water outage and the 16-hour rationing look like from the ground.

In short: no reactor was hit, but the grid around them was hit hard enough to throttle nuclear output, Kyiv's water is back after its worst outage of the war, Kharkiv is still on emergency cuts — and with Brussels meeting again Thursday and Abu Dhabi set for November 10–11, Zelensky's message to the US envoys was air defence first.

Riyadh airport kept operating on Wednesday despite the Houthi missile claim — while Tehran reads the US midterms as a vote against war

Yemen's Houthis claimed on Wednesday that a ballistic missile "directly" hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, causing what spokesman Yahya Saree called significant damage, Demócrata reports, alongside the other Wednesday operations already covered in the evening brief. The counter-evidence arrived the same evening: flight tracking reviewed by The Contrail shows the airport operating on Wednesday, with no delays flagged and about 15 departures cancelled — in line with recent days — and Saudi Arabia has not confirmed any damage. Saree again urged airlines and passengers to avoid Saudi airspace except over Mecca and Medina.

The two sides' positions are now easy to state plainly. The Houthis say the airport is a military target and unsafe, and have claimed repeated hits on it since October 7–11, when attacks killed at least 20 people by one tally. The airlines' behaviour tells a different story from either claim alone: most foreign carriers are still staying away — Etihad has now extended its Riyadh cancellations through November 19, Air France to December 12, Cathay Pacific to January 31, and British Airways' return is reportedly set for January 11 — while Saudi airlines keep flying, Saudia having moved most of its Riyadh flights to other Saudi airports through about December 1. Australia escalated its travel advice in the same window, Smartraveller warning travellers not to visit Saudi Arabia's airports in the current volatility, the Houthis having named Aramco targets as well.

For ordinary people, the practical point is that "open" and "normal" are not the same thing in Riyadh right now: the airport is operating, but largely on Saudi airlines, with most foreign routes still suspended into November, December or beyond — and every new claim, confirmed or not, gives airlines another reason to push their return dates back. In Tehran, meanwhile, parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf said Wednesday that the US midterm results reflected growing public opposition to the war against Iran, AnewZ reports — a political reading of an American election, not a change in the battlefield, where Iran's position remains that the Strait of Hormuz reopens only if Washington meets its seven conditions, a position Rezaei repeated as "not convincing" on Tuesday. No new US–Iran deal was announced Wednesday.

In short: the Houthis claimed a direct hit on Riyadh airport, the airport kept operating and Riyadh has not confirmed damage, foreign airlines keep pushing their returns back — and Tehran is spinning the midterms while holding its Hormuz conditions unchanged.

Open source

  • Tencent Hy4 — a 770-billion-parameter model that can see and use a computer, but the weights aren't out yet: Tencent switched on the full Hy4 on November 4: a 770B-parameter mixture-of-experts model (49B active per token, 1M-token context) that, unlike the text-only August preview, can interpret images, charts and documents and operate software through graphical interfaces and command lines. The catch the headlines skip: on launch day Hy4 is hosted only — through Tencent Cloud TokenHub, WorkBuddy/CodeBuddy, Yuanbao, ima and APIs on OpenRouter and others — with weights and a system card promised "in the coming days." Until they land, nobody outside Tencent can download, run or fine-tune it, so treat "open-source" here as announced, not yet delivered. Tencent's own computer-use benchmark puts Hy4 at 78.9 on OSWorld 2.1, behind Claude Opus 5.5 at 81.8 in Tencent's table — company-reported, not independently reproduced. Sources: StartupFortune, November 4, Tech Insider, November 4, dev.to explainer, November 4
