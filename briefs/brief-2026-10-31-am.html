World escalation

The energy war escalated on both sides overnight — Kyiv lost power and water, a giant Russian refinery complex caught fire

Russia and Ukraine traded energy strikes overnight, and both sides' claims point to a sharp escalation rather than a one-off exchange. According to a daily war brief compiling Ukrainian official statements, Russian forces struck Kyiv's critical infrastructure with drones overnight, hitting a facility three times and cutting electricity and water to multiple districts; Kyiv mayor Vitali Klitschko confirmed a critical infrastructure facility was hit, with outages reported in Troyeshchyna, Obolon, Pechersk and several other districts, and parts of the city also left without water. Kharkiv took Shahed-type drone hits across three districts, injuring three people and damaging ten residential buildings, according to mayor Ihor Terekhov. Ukraine's Air Force said Russia launched 134 drones, including 72 jet-powered Shahed models, hitting a combined heat and power plant in Kyiv, four electrical substations and a gas facility in Dnipropetrovsk, and another power plant in Shostka, with railway infrastructure damaged in Dnipro and Kharkiv.

Russia's Defence Ministry, for its part, said its air defences intercepted 433 Ukrainian drones overnight over 19 Russian regions, and claimed its own drone strikes hit Kyiv's CHP-6 power plant, workshops in Kharkiv's Proton industrial zone, and a cargo ship off Odesa. Moscow describes CHP-6 as supplying electricity to military logistics and weapons storage; Kyiv has consistently said Russian strikes on its energy system target civilian infrastructure ahead of winter, and Ukrainian officials had not publicly confirmed the specific Russian claims at the time of writing. Neither side's battlefield figures can be independently verified, and both regularly publish claims the other disputes — but the Kyiv outages are confirmed by the city's own mayor, which is the part that matters for people on the ground.

The other half of the night played out deep inside Russia. The Bashneft-Novoil oil refinery in Ufa, central Russia, caught fire Saturday after a reported Ukrainian drone attack, according to Russian outlet ASTRA, citing open-source analysis of eyewitness photos. ASTRA's analyst said a key catalytic reforming unit, the L-35 — which produces reformate, a high-octane gasoline component, and hydrogen needed to refine motor fuels — was likely burning. Residents reported several explosions Saturday morning, a column of smoke, and internet disruptions after an overnight drone alert, and Telegram channel Exilenova+ reported fires at two of the three refineries in Ufa's northern industrial zone. Bashneft's three adjacent Ufa refineries form one of Russia's largest refining complexes, with a combined capacity of 23.5 million tons of crude a year; Ukraine's General Staff said it hit the same L-35 unit in August 2025.

This is the second night of the resumed refinery campaign, not the first. Ukraine struck the Novokuibyshevsk refinery in the Samara region early Friday, ending a roughly two-week pause that followed heavy pressure from the Trump administration, and Russian Deputy Prime Minister Alexander Novak confirmed Friday that three oil refineries and one gas-chemical complex were attacked overnight, with two men killed in Samara. Novak warned the strikes "could certainly lead to a reduction in supply volumes" under the diesel deal struck with President Donald Trump, which saw Russia ship about 510,000 tons of diesel in October. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy told reporters Tuesday that Ukrainian forces "do not have an order" not to hit refineries, and European allies had advised Kyiv to wait until after the US midterms — advice Kyiv now appears to have set aside.

For ordinary people, the two directions of this exchange land differently. In Kyiv, it is another winter morning of no power and no water in several districts, with private energy provider DTEK saying the capital was suffering electricity shortages again on Saturday after Friday's strikes on energy infrastructure. In Russia, a hit on a catalytic reforming unit at one of the country's biggest refining complexes is the kind of damage that can tighten domestic gasoline and diesel supply — which is exactly why the diesel deal with Washington is now in tension with the refinery campaign. The US-brokered energy ceasefire that briefly held in mid-October is now firmly in the past: Zelenskyy declared that pause over on October 15, and both sides have been hitting energy targets since.

In short: overnight brought confirmed power and water outages across Kyiv districts and a fire at the Bashneft-Novoil refinery in Ufa, the second night of Ukraine's resumed refinery strikes — and with Moscow warning the strikes threaten the diesel volumes promised under its deal with Trump, the energy war is now running directly against the diplomacy meant to contain it.

Hundreds of starving Oleshky residents arrived back in Ukraine on Saturday — the rare ceasefire held long enough to get them out

Hundreds of people — and many pets — arrived back in Ukraine on Saturday afternoon after being evacuated from the frontline, Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, under a ceasefire that Pope Leo XIV called for and US First Lady Melania Trump helped to negotiate. CNN reports tearful reunions at an undisclosed border location, with evacuees wrapped in Ukrainian flags; President Volodymyr Zelenskyy announced the arrivals on Telegram, thanking Trump for the mediation and thanking "all journalists, all political and civic leaders, and all concerned people who drew global attention to the situation in Oleshky and the genuine humanitarian catastrophe" there.

The journey out was long by necessity. Access routes to Oleshky are heavily mined and littered with drone and vehicle debris, and crossing the Dnipro River directly into Ukrainian-controlled territory is not possible. After a two-day demining operation completed Thursday, residents who wanted to leave gathered Friday and travelled by bus through Crimea to Anapa in Russia's Krasnodar region, then flew to Gomel in Belarus — four planes in total, according to Ukrainian negotiator Davyd Arakhamia — before buses carried them to the Ukrainian border on Saturday. Ukraine's human rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets travelled to Oleshky to personally escort the evacuees, becoming the first high-profile Ukrainian official to openly travel to occupied areas under Russian control since the start of the war. Relatives waited at the border with aid tents, coffee stands and provisions for the pets evacuees brought with them, and the director of the Chernihiv Regional Hospital, Andriy Zhydenko, was there with medical staff.

What they are coming out of is hard to overstate. Oleshky, in the Kherson region, has been occupied since 2022 and was hit by major flooding in 2023 after the Nova Kakhovka Dam collapsed. Ukrainian authorities reported the town ran out of food and other supplies in September, with no access to electricity, gas or medicine, and reports from that time said people were surviving on grass and weeds. CNN tells the story of one reunited couple, Dmytro and Larisa, separated for 1,711 days: Larisa, whose soldier husband made her vulnerable to the occupiers, lost a third of her body weight and now weighs just over 90 pounds. Zhydenko told CNN that prolonged malnutrition will be a key concern — evacuees will need to be fed carefully, because eating too much too quickly after starving for so long can itself be dangerous — and that the hospital was receiving people with injuries, stroke survivors and those with limited mobility.

The numbers are still deliberately vague. Around 2,000 residents were estimated to remain in Oleshky from a pre-war population of about 24,000, and neither Moscow nor Kyiv has specified how many people were evacuated; Melania Trump posted Friday that the convoy could safely evacuate more than 750 people, and Ukrainian authorities imposed restrictions on disclosing the exact number or the border crossing location. Some residents chose to travel to Russian-held territory instead — Russian media showed civilians arriving at a temporary accommodation site in Simferopol, Crimea — and others chose to stay, with replenished food and medical supplies promised. The truce, which AFP describes as the longest pause in fighting anywhere on the front line since Russia invaded in February 2022, began at 3am Tuesday and is due to end Sunday evening. It has held without violations so far, according to both Ukrainian and Russian officials.

In short: the Oleshky evacuation worked — hundreds of starving residents, some separated from family for years, are back in Ukraine after a Crimea–Anapa–Belarus odyssey — but the ceasefire that made it possible expires Sunday evening, and what happens to those who stayed behind depends on whether that local truce holds or collapses back into the wider war.

Sources: CNN, AFP via Europe Says, Europe Says on the Belarus leg

Hormuz: Iran claims more tankers hit mines, the US disputes it — while Qatar pushes a deal that could bring US and Iranian officials face to face in Doha

The Strait of Hormuz produced two conflicting stories on Friday, and both matter. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said three oil tankers were halted after encountering "incidents" or hitting mines while using what Tehran calls an unauthorised southern route — two tankers stopped in the eastern and western parts of the strait, and later a supertanker carrying two million barrels of oil stopped after an explosion "after striking a naval mine," which the IRGC called "the third oil tanker to have exploded and caught fire today." That came a day after Iran's armed forces said at least four tankers had stopped in the area after hitting mines. Oman, meanwhile, said its navy rescued 25 sailors from the Panama-flagged merchant ship RITI after it was targeted and caught fire off Musandam, and UKMTO reported a separate vessel struck Friday by an unknown projectile 40 nautical miles south of Iran's Lavan Island, with the crew safe.

The US account directly contradicts Tehran's version. US Central Command chief Brad Cooper said in a video released Friday that US forces had "successfully cleared the strait's internationally recognised primary transit routes of Iranian sea mines," and that US forces remain present to keep those routes free and clear. CENTCOM rejected Iranian claims that commercial vessels were striking naval mines, and Qazinform notes the IRGC's claims have not been independently verified. The vessel counts do not line up neatly either — Ynet's reporting refers to one tanker hit and one other vessel on fire, while the IRGC's claims run to three or four — and none of the vessels in the IRGC's Friday claims was identified by name. What is independently confirmed is narrower: Oman did evacuate 25 crew from a burning vessel, UKMTO did log a projectile strike near Lavan Island, and oil markets moved — Brent crude climbed about 2% to $117.14 a barrel and WTI rose to $103.94, with UKMTO recording October as the month with the highest number of reported incidents in and around the strait since the war began on February 28.

Running alongside the shipping war is a diplomatic push with a deadline. Qatari mediators held more than 12 hours of talks in Tehran on Friday aimed at restarting direct negotiations between Washington and Tehran and preventing a renewed outbreak of war after the November 3 US midterm elections, according to Axios. The talks are expected to continue in Doha on Saturday, and the Times of Israel, citing the Qatari newspaper Al-Araby Al-Jadeed, reports an American delegation is expected to join Iranian officials for direct talks in Doha following a reported breakthrough in indirect negotiations, with an understanding said to cover preliminary measures on Iran's nuclear programme, Hormuz navigation and financial incentives for Tehran, and China and Russia reportedly expected to serve as guarantors. Axios cautions that the two sides remain far apart on preconditions: the Trump administration wants nuclear concessions before talks resume, while Tehran insists Washington must first lift its naval blockade, release frozen funds and allow oil sales. Qatar is pushing reciprocal confidence-building steps over several days — Iran letting UN inspectors visit its bombed nuclear sites, met with some sanctions relief.

Inside the White House, the split is out in the open. Axios reports Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth and Secretary of State Marco Rubio are sceptical a deal can be reached with Iran's current leadership and favour further weakening it, including additional strikes, while Vice President JD Vance and envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner favour resuming negotiations, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has urged giving sanctions pressure time to work. President Trump, speaking to reporters Friday, said "There are talks. They want to talk. They keep talking... We have time, frankly. It's a war, but it is not a typical war," and separately told a Kansas City radio station the war would be "wrapped up soon," without saying how. Regional countries including Egypt, Turkey, Iraq, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia have also urged Tehran to resume negotiations before the midterms, according to a regional source cited by Axios.

For ordinary people, the Hormuz standoff is already a wallet issue: Brent above $117 is the market pricing in the risk that the world's most important oil chokepoint stays contested, and the IEA's emergency oil and diesel releases earlier this month were a response to exactly this pressure. A Doha breakthrough would be the first direct US-Iran talks of this war; a failure, with the midterms three days away, leaves the post-election military option that both sides have been warning about squarely on the table.

In short: Iran claims three more tankers hit mines on an "unauthorised" route and the US says its cleared lanes are safe — the truth is unverified and the oil price is climbing — while Qatar's marathon Tehran talks may produce direct US-Iran talks in Doha as soon as Saturday, with Trump's own team split between negotiating and striking.

Israel's final election count confirms deadlock — 53 to 48, with a record 19 Arab-party seats and a foreign-interference probe clouding coalition talks

Israel's Central Elections Committee released the final results on Friday, and they confirm what the late count suggested: no bloc has the 61 seats needed to govern the 120-seat Knesset. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud emerged as the largest single party with 27 seats, and his bloc holds 53; Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar took 21 seats, and his opposition bloc holds 48; Arab-led parties — the Joint List and Ra'am — took a record combined 19 seats. Turnout was 77.9%, with more than 5.7 million ballots cast. Netanyahu's bloc is down sharply from the 68 seats his coalition held in the outgoing Knesset.

What happens next runs through President Isaac Herzog. The committee is expected to submit the formal results to Herzog on November 4, after final checks, and the new Knesset is due to be sworn in on November 10. Herzog will consult party representatives before assigning the mandate to the candidate he believes is most likely to assemble 61 seats — not necessarily the leader of the largest party. CNN notes Eisenkot is likely to collect more recommendations than Netanyahu, since the Arab parties are expected to back him, and the chosen candidate gets 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension, to negotiate a government; if he fails, the mandate passes to another candidate, and if that fails too, the country goes to automatic elections. Netanyahu, as caretaker prime minister, has a built-in advantage in that sequence: if Eisenkot gets the mandate first and fails, Netanyahu can simply wait for his turn.

The arithmetic leaves three plausible paths, all awkward. One is a Netanyahu-led coalition picking up right-wing parties currently aligned with the opposition, such as Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad (12 seats) — though Bennett campaigned against Netanyahu. Another is an Eisenkot government relying on Arab-party support, which would reach 67 seats combined but which several opposition parties have ruled out; Eisenkot himself has not ruled it out under conditions, including recognising Israel as a Jewish state and Hamas as an extremist group. A third is a national unity government of Netanyahu and Eisenkot together, commanding around 66 seats. Netanyahu warned again on Friday against forming a coalition with Arab parties, calling such a partnership treasonous, while Yair Golan, leader of the smaller left-wing Democrats party, called for a government that would include them.

Hanging over the talks is an allegation neither side can yet prove or dismiss. Committee chair Justice Noam Sohlberg wrote to the Shin Bet that "serious suspicions" of foreign interference in the Arab vote had reached him, while saying he could not verify them; the Shin Bet said its inquiry does not "cast aspersions" on an entire population that voted lawfully. The dispute gives each camp a reason to harden its position just as the bargaining starts — and it lands on top of a record Arab turnout that many Palestinian citizens of Israel cast explicitly hoping to end Netanyahu's rule and remove far-right figures from his outgoing coalition, including Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich and National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir.

For ordinary Israelis, the practical consequence is weeks — possibly months — of caretaker government, with Netanyahu remaining in office throughout, while coalition talks decide whether the country gets a new government or another election. The Netanyahu trial remains on pause during this period, with no session during election week.

In short: the final count is 53–48–19 and nobody won — Netanyahu stays caretaker prime minister while Herzog's consultations, starting after November 4, decide whether Eisenkot, Netanyahu, or a unity deal can find 61 seats, with a record Arab bloc as kingmaker and an unverified interference allegation poisoning the talks before they start.

  • Mika — an open-source Android agent that runs apps on a hidden background screen: Mika (wangzan101/Mika, Apache-2.0) turns a tool-calling language model into a phone agent: type one sentence and it opens apps and websites, reads the screen, and taps, types and scrolls on a hidden second display while you keep using the phone.
