Strikes keep coming on the eve of Abu Dhabi talks — and Trump says a deal is "very close"

Russia battered Ukraine's power grid again and Ukraine hit another of Russia's biggest refineries plus a missile-fuel plant, hours before US-brokered talks open in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday — and President Trump told CBS's "60 Minutes" on Sunday that the two sides are "very close" to a deal, while hinting Ukraine would end up with "a little bit different shape."

Russia's defence ministry said it targeted four electrical substations in Ukraine's Kyiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Cherkasy and Vinnytsia regions, part of its campaign to deny civilians heating, light and running water as winter approaches. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched 317 drones overnight, including 105 jet-powered ones. In Odesa, Russian forces hit a warehouse holding food supplies for kindergartens and schools, killing three people, local officials said. Zelenskyy said Monday the European Union had agreed to activate a special emergency mechanism that speeds up funding for power-grid repairs.

Ukraine's reply was long-range. Drones started a fire at the KINEF oil refinery near St. Petersburg, one of Russia's largest, more than 1,200 kilometres from the Ukrainian border, Leningrad Governor Alexander Drozdenko said. Ukraine's General Staff also said its forces struck a plant near St. Petersburg that produces solid rocket propellant for Russia's Iskander-M ballistic missiles — a direct hit on the weapons Ukraine says it cannot stop in the air without more US-made Patriot interceptors, which are in short supply. Ukraine said in early October that its long-range strikes had already neutralised more than half of Russia's oil refining capacity.

The two sides arrive at the table telling different stories about what the talks are even about. Trump said in the "60 Minutes" interview, which aired Sunday, that Moscow and Kyiv are "very close" to a deal despite the "tremendous hatred" between Putin and Zelenskyy — though, as AP notes, he has previously made statements about developments in the war that haven't materialised. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov said Monday that Trump's team has placed "collective security" — covering not just Russia and Ukraine but a wider area — at the top of the agenda. Moscow insists Europe will not take part in the three-way talks: Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Monday the meeting would focus on trilateral negotiations with the US mediating, while French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot said Sunday that France and other European representatives would be in the UAE. Secretary of State Marco Rubio is set to travel to Abu Dhabi. The last three-way round, in Geneva in February, delivered no significant progress.

For ordinary people, the human cost is measured in cold and dark: hundreds of thousands without power in Dnipropetrovsk and Kharkiv regions alone, emergency and scheduled outages across most of Ukraine, and a refinery fire 1,200 kilometres inside Russia that shows nowhere in either country's energy system is out of reach. The 48 hours before the talks brought a massive Russian strike on Ukraine's power system and Ukrainian strikes on Kirishi and a missile-fuel plant in return — the agenda for Abu Dhabi is being written by the attacks themselves.

In short: both sides hit each other's energy and military infrastructure one more time before Tuesday's Abu Dhabi talks, Trump says a deal is "very close" while hinting Ukraine will have to give up territory, and Moscow is trying to keep Europe out of the room — with the last round in Geneva having produced nothing.

Trump meets Qatar's prime minister as oil plunges on peace hopes

President Trump met Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al-Thani, at the White House on Monday as diplomatic efforts to end the eight-month Iran war ramp up — and oil fell sharply, with Brent settling at $92.57 a barrel, down 5.85% on the day, as traders bet on a diplomatic exit.

The meeting, held behind closed doors at noon ET, put Qatar — the key mediator carrying messages between Washington and Tehran — at the centre of the day. Vice President J.D. Vance, who has been speaking almost daily with mediators, also met Sheikh Mohammed separately. The Qatari leader visited Tehran last week and has been pushing a compressed two-day framework: the US ends its blockade of Iranian ports, eases some sanctions and halts bombing, and Iran stops its blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, with talks on its nuclear programme to follow. Washington has repeatedly rejected that sequencing, insisting nuclear issues come first.

Tehran, for its part, sounded more open on Monday. Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Iran was prepared to negotiate an end to what he called a prolonged state of "neither war nor peace," adding that Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei had instructed officials to end the uncertainty — while insisting Iran would not surrender to coercion. Iran's Foreign Ministry spokesman defended the two-day proposal on Monday as the most reasonable offer on the table. But Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Sunday he could not say the two sides were "on the verge of a breakthrough" and called Tehran's demands "unacceptable," "outrageous" and "ridiculous." A US official told Al Jazeera the indirect discussions were "positive and constructive" but stressed there would be no agreement unless Iran's nuclear programme is addressed.

The two sides' versions of the stall still do not agree on the cause. Araghchi said mediators had made no progress since the "shock" of the US midterm elections; Rubio said there had been little productive result so far. Behind the public statements, Qatari officials are expected to continue discussions in Tehran on Tuesday, and Araghchi said he hoped to receive Washington's final response by Tuesday. Meanwhile, the UAE revealed a major surge in oil exports despite the conflict, and the US blockade of Iranian ports continues to bite — Iraq's foreign minister said it has cost Iraq $60 billion.

For the world economy, the shipping war is the part that bites — and the part markets are now pricing as easing. Iran closed the strait — which handled roughly 20% of the world's oil and gas before the war — shortly after the US and Israel launched their offensive on February 28, and flows are running well below capacity. Brent fell 5.85% to settle at $92.57 on Monday, WTI down to around $87.40, after Brent dropped 16% last week, its biggest weekly decline since the start of the Covid pandemic. Cheaper oil, if it holds, means lower fuel prices — but only if the diplomacy actually lands.

In short: Trump met the Qatari mediator on Monday, Iran's president says Tehran is ready to negotiate with dignity, but Rubio says no breakthrough is near and the two sides still disagree on whether nuclear talks come first — while oil plunged nearly 6% on hopes the standoff ends.

Anthropic and Meta both dropped new AI models on the same day

Anthropic launched Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2 on Monday, and Meta launched Muse Ember the same day — two very different bets on where AI goes next, one aimed at the hardest technical work, the other at making the routine work cheap.

Anthropic's release is the bigger of the two. Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2 are the same underlying model, the company says, split by safeguards: Fable carries cybersecurity and biology blocks and is available to everyone on paid plans, Claude Code and the major cloud platforms, while Mythos drops some of those blocks and goes only to organisations approved through Anthropic's Cyber or Life Sciences Verification Programs. Anthropic says both are up to 30% faster than the 5.1 versions, communicate more clearly, and cost less — $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, down from $10/$50. On Anthropic's own benchmarks, Fable 5.2 scored 49.1% on the FrontierCode 2.0 agentic coding test, ahead of Opus 5.5 at 45.5%, and Artificial Analysis put it first on its Intelligence Index with a score of 65. Reuters notes the launch comes ahead of Anthropic's planned IPO — formal marketing of the shares to investors starts Tuesday, November 10, with a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT. Cognition made Fable 5.2 available in its Devin coding tools on launch day.

Meta's Muse Ember goes the other way: the smallest and cheapest model in the Muse family, built for the repetitive work AI agents do all day — tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, pulling data out of long documents. It costs $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens, with a 1-million-token context window, and is live now on the Meta Model API and in Muse Code under the name muse-ember-1.0. Meta says Ember matches Anthropic's Claude Haiku 5.5 on Artificial Analysis's index at about a third of the cost per task — roughly $0.14 per task against Haiku's higher figure. But the comparison cuts both ways: on real coding tasks in FrontierCode 2.0, Haiku still wins, 34.9% to Ember's 27.2%. Ember's weights stay private, as with earlier Muse releases.

The two launches frame the same argument differently. Anthropic is selling capability at the top end — its most powerful model yet, with a restricted twin for vetted security and biology researchers, and a price cut to go with it. Meta is selling cost at the bottom end — near-Haiku intelligence for agent grunt work at a fraction of the price, betting that volume matters more than peak smarts. Both sets of benchmark claims are company-reported or from a single index, not independent validation, and both companies are racing OpenAI and Google, which have been releasing new models at a similar pace. For anyone who uses these tools, the practical effect is the same either way: faster, cheaper AI coding help, with Anthropic's top tier aimed at the most ambitious technical work and Meta's at the high-volume repetitive kind.

In short: Anthropic launched its most capable models yet — Fable 5.2 for everyone, Mythos 5.2 restricted to vetted security and life-sciences organisations — the day before its IPO roadshow starts, while Meta launched Muse Ember, a tiny model that matches Claude Haiku on one benchmark at about a third of the cost per task.

Google's two new TPU systems take the Nvidia fight up a notch

Google added two new systems to its eighth-generation TPU lineup on Monday — TPU 8tx for secure, confidential inference and TPU 8i Max for massive context windows — its fourth TPU system launch in a single year, and the clearest sign yet that Google intends its own chips to be a real alternative to Nvidia's.

The two systems solve different problems. TPU 8tx, built on the same silicon as the training-focused TPU 8t and paired with 5th-generation AMD EPYC hosts, builds security directly into the hardware layer: data and model weights stay encrypted and isolated even while in active use, which matters for governments and heavily regulated industries that currently have to unlock data in memory to process it. Google says it delivers up to 2x lower latency than TPU 8t with a near-zero performance penalty. TPU 8i Max takes two existing TPU 8i chips and combines them into a single package, doubling compute, SRAM and HBM bandwidth — built for the memory wall that appears when dozens of AI agents work across an entire codebase or hours of video at once. Both are expected to be available in 2027, according to Bloomberg; Google gave no prices or full specs.

Caption: Google's eighth-generation TPU lineup — TPU 8t for training, TPU 8i for real-time reasoning, and the two new systems announced Monday: TPU 8tx for confidential inference and TPU 8i Max for massive context windows. It is the first year Google has shipped four TPU systems.

The two sides of this race are Google's pitch and Nvidia's dominance. Google says it now has nearly 100 TPU customers — including Apple, Meta and Oracle, according to Bloomberg — that Anthropic has committed to 3.5 GW of TPU capacity starting in 2027, and that Oracle will offer TPUs through its cloud from next year. Every TPU Google has available is already contracted to a customer, the company says. Nvidia, meanwhile, still owns the AI accelerator market, and Google's own chip chief, Amin Vahdat, acknowledged the new systems are reconfigured versions of existing designs rather than new silicon. The bet is that packaging, security and software — Google also open-sourced TorchTPU and a TPU agent suite that plugs into Claude Code and Cursor — can win customers Nvidia's GPUs can't serve as cheaply.

For ordinary people the effect is indirect but real: the chips that train and run AI models are one of the biggest costs in the AI economy, and a genuine second supplier means cheaper AI services and less dependence on a single company. It also matters for the memory crunch Adam has been watching — Google is designing around the same memory wall that has pushed DRAM prices up 10–15% this quarter.

In short: Google launched two new TPU systems on Monday — the 8tx for hardware-level confidential computing and the 8i Max for huge context windows — bringing its 2026 total to four, with nearly 100 customers including Apple, Meta and Oracle, and both systems due in 2027.

Nvidia — Commerce halted the Megaspeed probe after Nvidia's general counsel emailed investigators

The New York Times reported Monday that the Commerce Department halted its months-long investigation into Megaspeed — the Singapore data-centre firm suspected of diverting billions of dollars of Nvidia AI chips to China — after Nvidia's general counsel Tim Teter emailed the investigators in August 2025, saying senior Commerce official Jeff Kessler had given Nvidia permission to reach out. The case was then transferred to the Justice Department, which is now running a criminal inquiry. Important caveat, stressed by several outlets relaying the Times report: the reporting does not establish that Nvidia's contact caused the halt — Kessler told the Times he did not direct investigators to share information with Nvidia, and a Commerce official said Kessler instructed them to proceed "by the book." Nvidia's board investigation into CEO Jensen Huang continues; the House chairs want an answer by Wednesday, November 11, on a closed-door briefing November 18, and Nvidia reports Q3 earnings November 17. (NYT via WCCFTech · NYT via Clash Report · Superpower Daily)

Khor Mor — Baghdad to supply household gas while the field stays shut

Production at the Khor Mor gas field in Iraqi Kurdistan remained suspended on Monday while operator Dana Gas assesses damage from Sunday night's drone strike, which cut 2,500–2,800 MW — roughly 70–80% of the region's power. New today: Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi ordered the federal Oil Ministry to supply the Kurdistan Region with liquefied petroleum gas until production resumes, and residents queued at filling stations on Monday, though officials in Sulaymaniyah said household gas stocks would last more than a month. No restart date has been given; after the November 2025 strike on the field, normal output was restored within a week. No group has claimed responsibility — the 12th attack on the field since 2022. (The Insight International · Silent Dossier)

Krakow — prosecutors say no ideology link established yet

At a Monday briefing, National Prosecutor's Office spokesman Przemysław Nowak said investigators still lack evidence to tie gunman Michał Oksiński to any particular political ideology, despite Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński's earlier claim that he held radical ultranationalist views: "At the moment, we do not have elements that allow us to responsibly and categorically state that he was inspired by a particular ideology." The terrorism classification stands, and the investigation into motive, accomplices and how he obtained 11 firearms legally through a shooting club continues. Post-mortems on all nine victims began Monday. (Pollar · InfoHandle · RingkasNow)

Cyber

CISA adds four exploited flaws to KEV — including "SSHole" — with a 3-day deadline

CISA added four actively exploited vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on Monday, with an unusually short federal deadline of November 12: CVE-2026-123158 ("SSHole"), the OpenSSH authentication bypass on musl-based Linux; CVE-2026-102895, a 9.8 improper-authentication flaw in GPUStack (open-source GPU cluster manager) observed exploited against a 2.1.1 deployment; CVE-2026-116779, a 9.8 code-injection flaw in SignalWire FreeSWITCH; and CVE-2026-123218, a 9.8 incorrect-authorisation flaw in IBM Aspera Faspex allowing an authenticated user to gain full admin privileges. The catalog now stands at 1,773 entries. (CISA via CTXSec · Malice.ai)

"SSHole" is now confirmed exploited — hackers are breaking into Linux systems

BleepingComputer reported Monday that hackers are actively exploiting the "SSHole" OpenSSH flaw (CVE-2026-123158) to execute commands, steal credentials and establish persistent access on Linux systems. The flaw lets an attacker log in to accounts with no password on musl-based systems — Alpine Linux first of all — simply by entering a password longer than 256 characters. GreyNoise recorded the first successful exploitation in the wild on November 7, about 40 hours after the fix shipped, logging 16 successful attack chains from six source IPs; Previdian sensors confirmed 20 attempts from 13 IPs in nine countries. WatchTowr said exploitation is associated with botnets and cryptomining. In one case, attackers gained root, changed DNS settings, started a hidden second SSH server and installed a remote-control agent with Chinese-language comments. The fix is OpenSSH 10.6p2 (released November 5) or a distribution patch — or disable password authentication until patched. (BleepingComputer · Previdian)

Fridge-hack spread across Europe, Guardian investigation finds

A Guardian investigation published Sunday found the refrigeration-system attacks that hit French supermarkets last month also disrupted a London food wholesaler and retailers in Belgium, Estonia, Ireland, Finland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden during the previous week. French investigators are still hunting for those responsible, with no claim of responsibility; Sekoia has suggested a lone hacker may be behind it, possibly with no malware on the controllers at all. (Tech Current)

FTC v. Amazon — both sides move for summary judgment in the Prime case

Amazon and the FTC have both moved for summary judgment in the Prime "dark patterns" case — Amazon seeking dismissal, the FTC seeking liability — after nearly two years of discovery. The FTC is seeking to hold three Amazon executives personally liable for potentially over $1 billion in penalties, alleging they knew about accidental Prime enrollments. At Friday's economics hearing in the separate antitrust case, Judge Chun pressed the FTC on what consumer harm it is actually trying to prove — lower quality, higher prices — and warned it to "keep an eye on" a similar consumer case he oversees that has been repeatedly dismissed. The Prime jury trial continues in Seattle. (Typefully · Typefully)
