Evening brief — Thursday, November 5, 2026

The tankers keep getting hit while Washington argues about whether to bomb Iran again

Iran reported three more tankers damaged in the Strait of Hormuz on Thursday, and the attacks on Gulf shipping are now running at close to a record pace — at least five vessels have reported incidents since Sunday, according to the UK maritime security agency UKMTO, about the same rate as October, when 35 vessels were hit, the most of any month of the war. The UKMTO said one vessel was struck by two unidentified projectiles late on Wednesday. Iran's Revolutionary Guards, who don't usually admit to firing on merchant ships but say the transit route is unsafe without Tehran's consent, said two tankers had struck naval mines and another had caught fire on Thursday. The morning brief's tanker hit near Qatar — two projectiles about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash Shamal — was one of the week's incidents.

The politics around the war are now being fought in public. Vice President JD Vance said in a long social media post on Wednesday that the war was to blame for the Republicans' midterm defeat, and a White House adviser told Reuters that some officials inside the White House saw the post as a foreign policy break from the president and a public recognition of the war's economic cost. Iran's Parliament Speaker Mohammad Baqer Qalibaf mocked Vance on Thursday, accusing him of letting Trump double down like a "losing gambler" by planning more US strikes, and telling him: "Skip the TED talk. Why not say this to your boss?" Trump, asked about Vance's comments on Wednesday, defended launching the war as necessary to stop Iran getting a nuclear weapon — a justification he has used repeatedly.

But two sources told Reuters they believed Trump was still likely to order resumed hostilities. "He is planning to bomb Iran. Don't listen to what he's saying. He held off because of the midterms. We asked him to hold off until the midterms," one said; the other said Trump had nothing to lose politically now that the midterms were over. That sits against what Trump said Wednesday — no immediate new large-scale strikes, "locked and loaded if we need to go, but there may be no reason for it" — and against the military preparations Axios reported, with more assets being sent to the region and no final decision made. On Wednesday, Supreme Leader Ayatollah Mojtaba Khamenei said there should not be "even the slightest inclination towards submission" to the United States, which he said would remain hostile no matter which party was in power. Qatar said Tuesday that both sides were committed to mediation and reviewing proposals, but a senior Iranian official said this week the two sides remain far apart, and neither side has announced any breakthrough on the big issues: the future of the strait, the US blockade on Iranian ports, sanctions, and a resumption of nuclear talks.

For ordinary people, the clearest signal is still the oil price. Prices have plunged in recent days as traders bet that Trump, chastened by voters, may be less likely to restart the strikes he launched alongside Israel as "Operation Epic Fury" in February. They bounced back slightly on Thursday but are still down more than $20 a barrel from last week. The morning brief's numbers still stand: Brent was at $95.07 on Thursday, down almost 10% in a week, while US diesel is running about $6.27 a gallon — roughly 75% higher than a year ago — with reserves below 100 million barrels for the first time in more than 20 years.

In short: the shooting in the Gulf hasn't stopped — three more tankers Thursday, a record pace — while Washington argues in public about whether the war cost Republicans the election, and Trump, who says there may be no reason to strike again, is still being described by sources close to him as likely to do exactly that. The Qatar mediation is the only off-ramp on the table, and nobody is claiming it's close.

The Houthis' price to stop: $51 billion — and recognition as Yemen's rulers

Saudi Arabia reached out to the Houthis to ask what it would take to stop the fighting, and the answer, according to the Wall Street Journal on Thursday, was $51 billion — the sum the Houthis say covers years of Saudi strikes and a long blockade — plus formal recognition as the rulers of Yemen. People briefed on the discussions said other figures have come up since, but the message was clear: the price of an exit would be in the tens of billions of dollars. The demand landed after the Houthis attacked Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea and seized territory commanding the Bab al-Mandeb chokepoint, and as they press toward Aden, the seat of Yemen's internationally recognized government, moving to within about 50 miles of the city — closer than at any time since 2015.

The two sides don't even agree on whether the demand was made. In a post on X on Thursday after the Journal's article appeared, Rayed Krimly, Saudi Arabia's deputy minister for public diplomacy, denied that the Houthis demanded money from Riyadh, calling them militias that "have revolted against the Yemeni government, caused the starvation of the Yemeni people and plunged Yemen into conflicts and agendas that do not serve Yemen and its people." The Houthi leadership didn't respond to requests for comment. The Journal, citing people briefed on the discussions, stands by the figure — and analysts quoted in the piece say the money may be the smaller problem: paying it would set a precedent where the group can escalate militarily whenever it wants more concessions, said Maged Al-Madhaji of the Sana'a Center for Strategic Studies.

Meanwhile the shooting hasn't paused. On Thursday, the Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed a ballistic missile fired toward the kingdom's eastern region, and later said two ballistic missiles launched toward Riyadh were also intercepted and destroyed, according to Al Jazeera. That followed Wednesday's Houthi claim of a ballistic missile at Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport during Syria's President Ahmed al-Sharaa's visit — unconfirmed by Saudi authorities, with the airport kept operating — and Thursday morning's Saudi alerts warning of potential danger in Yanbu, the Red Sea oil export hub. Inside Yemen, the Journal reports a Houthi strike in Shabwah governorate killed at least 75 people and wounded more than 200, the deadliest since the civil war reignited, and the Houthis have been targeting areas that would serve as muster points for any Saudi-led ground attack.

Riyadh's options are all bad, and the Journal lays them out plainly. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman doesn't want to negotiate fully with the Houthis without gains on the ground to use as leverage, Saudi officials said, but worries that sending more Saudi troops into Yemen could be bloody and spark anger at home — and there have already been dozens of casualties the authorities haven't reported publicly. The main outside ally the kingdom once had in Yemen, the UAE, withdrew entirely from the anti-Houthi campaign last December after a falling-out with Riyadh. Saudi Arabia has instead sought help through its new Mecca Pact with Turkey and Pakistan, which treats an attack on one as an attack on all — but Turkey's parliament has yet to approve it (it passed only the first article of the ratification bill on Wednesday before adjourning), Pakistan has refused to join strikes inside Yemen, and Egypt, whose parliament approved sending military support in October, says any mission would be defensive only. The US, meanwhile, has so far declined to offer direct support against the Houthis after its own weekslong campaign against the group in 2025.

For ordinary people, the war is measured in closed airports, cancelled flights and displacement. The Houthis carried out a near-daily campaign of attacks on Saudi Arabia in October, hitting civilian airports and Aramco facilities, and there have been 26 reported incidents in the kingdom in recent days alone, according to the conflict monitor Acled. Inside Yemen, fighting since July has killed and wounded thousands and displaced more than 250,000 people, according to the UN.

In short: Saudi Arabia asked the Houthis what it would cost to stop, and the answer — $51 billion and recognition as Yemen's government — is a price Riyadh hasn't agreed to and one Saudi official publicly denies was even demanded. With no ally willing to fight on the ground and the Houthis 50 miles from Aden, the kingdom is stuck between paying, fighting a war it can't win cheaply, or waiting.

Israel: Eisenkot wins the recommendations, Herzog names his pick on Sunday

President Isaac Herzog finished consultations with every Knesset faction on Thursday evening and said he intends to assign the mandate to form a government this coming Sunday, November 8. Factions representing 67 lawmakers recommended Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot; factions representing 46 recommended Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu; and United Torah Judaism, with seven seats, declined to recommend anyone. The Joint List, with 11 seats, announced Thursday it would recommend Eisenkot, joining Ra'am's eight — though both Arab-led lists' endorsements don't commit them to joining his coalition, and Eisenkot, like Netanyahu, has ruled out governing with them.

The arithmetic is the trap. Eisenkot's 67 recommendations are not 67 votes for a government: his core bloc of four Jewish-led parties won only 48 seats, the Arab-led lists won 19, and a majority needs 61 of 120. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats in the October 27 election, with Likud the largest single party at 27. Herzog said he still needs to clarify directly with some party leaders who can actually assemble a governing majority, which is why he is taking until Sunday rather than announcing immediately — he has until November 11. The new Knesset is sworn in on November 10, and whoever gets the mandate has 28 days to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension.

Netanyahu, at risk of losing power, has seized on an investigation by election officials into possible vote tampering to cast doubt on the results, accusing Arab-led parties of stealing the vote — a charge his critics say is an attempt to undermine results he denies trying to undermine. Israel's Central Election Committee said on Wednesday that the election's integrity had not been compromised, and its head, Supreme Court Justice Noam Sohlberg, called on elected officials to protect public trust rather than shake it. "This is a real danger. One of the foundations of democracy is the peaceful transfer of power," Sohlberg told Herzog. Netanyahu's far-right allies, including National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir, have called for the immediate arrest and interrogation of Arab party leaders.

For ordinary Israelis, the stakes are a caretaker government running the country while the region stays volatile — Netanyahu remains prime minister until a new coalition is formed, or through another election if no candidate succeeds — and a president openly warning parties to do their utmost to avert another round of voting. Herzog told the factions that "any reasonable person need only look at the security and diplomatic challenges before us, at the changing region around us, and remember the issues tearing us apart from within, to understand that an immense responsibility rests upon us."

In short: Eisenkot has the recommendations, 67 to 46, and Herzog will name his pick on Sunday — but recommendations aren't a coalition, and with both men ruling out governing with the Arab parties whose backing put Eisenkot ahead, nobody has shown a path to 61 seats yet. Netanyahu is fighting the result itself, and another election is still on the table.

Ukraine: aid-centre death toll reaches 42 as Brussels talks turn to security guarantees

The death toll from Monday's Russian bombing of an aid distribution point in the village of Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region, has risen to 42, police said on Thursday, making it one of the deadliest strikes on civilians this year. Up to 33 people remained buried beneath the rubble, with rescue work continuing and bodies still being identified. On Wednesday, regional official Ivan Fedorov had said 59 people were missing. Bodycam footage from one of the police officers killed, which Reuters verified, showed the moments before a second Russian strike hit, destroying the shelter where residents were hiding after a radio warning. Ukraine's national police said the strikes included a 3-ton FAB glide bomb, carrying around 1.4 tons of explosives. Russia's Defence Ministry said the attack targeted Ukrainian military units around Komyshuvakha and two other settlements, and that up to 25 Ukrainian servicemen were killed. Both Moscow and Kyiv deny deliberately targeting civilians; the UN human rights mission in Ukraine said on Tuesday that the bombing likely violated international law.

At the same time, the diplomacy track moved to its second day in Brussels. Talks on Ukraine's postwar reconstruction and security guarantees resumed on Thursday at NATO headquarters, with delegations from Ukraine, the US, the UK, France, Germany, the EU and NATO, plus the World Bank and the US investment group BlackRock, according to the Associated Press. Ukraine's lead negotiator Rustem Umerov said the discussions covered postwar reconstruction, funding for 2027 and the security guarantees Western allies could offer, as well as a new post-war European security architecture. US envoy Jared Kushner called the talks "productive and substantive" in a post on X. The Brussels meetings come a week before negotiators from Russia, Ukraine and the US are due to meet in Abu Dhabi on November 10-11.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, speaking from an Ibero-American summit in Spain, said in an interview broadcast Thursday that Ukraine must adapt to the new realities of US politics after the Democrats won both chambers of Congress, and that solving the Patriot air-defence issue is the top priority — he is also working to keep enough F-16 fighter jets operating over the next five to six months. He said EU, NATO and US officials meeting in Brussels were making some progress on security-guarantee proposals. Overnight, Russia said it struck logistics and transport targets across Ukraine, including a Nova Poshta logistics hub in Kharkiv region that Moscow said was used to assemble drones, railway locomotives, a power station in Dnipropetrovsk region, and a cargo vessel at sea off Odesa — claims Reuters could not independently verify.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the war's daily shape is unchanged: power generation and substations keep getting hit, emergency outages keep coming, and the people queuing for aid in a frontline village were the ones who paid for Monday's strike. The Brussels talks are about what comes after — a prosperity plan for reconstruction and guarantees meant to stop the next war — but they are being held while the current one is still killing civilians at aid centres.

In short: the Komyshuvakha toll rose to 42 with dozens still buried, the UN says the bombing likely broke international law, and in Brussels the allies spent a second day sketching the post-war guarantees and reconstruction plan — with Zelenskyy now openly recalibrating for a Washington run by Democrats and Patriot missiles at the top of his list.

Courts: FTC unseals Amazon ad-price allegations

FTC v. Amazon (ads auction): The FTC and 22 state attorneys general filed an updated complaint on October 28, with the newly unredacted details announced November 5, alleging Amazon secretly raised the prices advertisers paid in its online search advertising auction — by up to 50% above auction results on ordinary days, and up to 250% on major shopping days like Prime Day and Black Friday. One large food-and-beverage advertiser faced increases of more than 100% during Prime Day 2023, and Amazon internally acknowledged the increases were "destroying campaign performance." Amazon said it strongly disagrees with the claims and that the FTC "fundamentally misunderstands how advertisers operate." The antitrust trial itself is set for March 29, 2027. Sources · Digiday via Dig.Watch

FTC v. Amazon (Prime "dark patterns"): Both sides have now moved for summary judgment — Amazon seeking dismissal, the FTC seeking liability — and at Friday's economics hearing Judge Chun pressed the FTC on what consumer harm it is actually trying to prove, noting "millions of shoppers are happy" with the service. The jury trial is underway in Seattle. Separately, Amazon agreed to pay $3 million in refunds to eligible Arizona consumers over its old Prime cancellation process, after the state AG sued in 2024; Amazon denied violating Arizona law. Sources · TheStreet
