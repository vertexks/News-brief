World escalation

Russia's election-day barrage hits Ukraine's power grid as the aid-centre toll rises to 29

Russia launched a sweeping overnight bombardment of Ukraine's energy infrastructure on Tuesday, hours after the death toll from Monday's strike on a humanitarian aid centre in Zaporizhzhia rose to 29, with 48 people still missing. The barrage — 289 attack drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles across nine regions, according to President Volodymyr Zelensky — triggered emergency power cuts across the country and landed on the day of the US midterm elections, a timing Zelensky pointedly flagged.

The details, as reported by the Associated Press and Reuters, show a two-front night. In Komyshuvakha, the Zaporizhzhia village hit Monday, rescuers kept pulling bodies from the cratered aid facility and shelter through Tuesday, with acting regional military chief Mykhailo Semikin calling it "one of the worst tragedies in terms of the number of civilian victims in our region since the start of the full-scale war." The region declared three days of mourning. In the overnight wave, a strike on a city clinical hospital in Kharkiv wounded at least 62 people by local authorities' count — Zelensky put the hospital toll at 73 — and forced the transfer of more than 200 patients; in Dnipro, one person was killed and 17 wounded, with a woman pulled alive from rubble Tuesday morning. Ukraine's largest private generator, DTEK, said three of its five thermal power plants were knocked offline, and railways were hit as well. Russia's Defence Ministry said it carried out a "massive strike" on electricity facilities supplying military factories and on port infrastructure in Odesa. In the other direction, Ukraine's drones set fire to a major oil refinery in Yaroslavl, wounding two employees, according to the regional governor; Moscow's mayor said 493 drones were fired toward the capital, and Moscow-installed officials in Crimea imposed rolling blackouts after energy attacks there.

The two sides read the night in opposite ways. Zelensky framed the timing as a message to Washington: "The Russians think America won't notice this signal of theirs," he said, urging the US to "help Ukraine defend its skies; otherwise, Russia will never stop its missile strikes." Moscow's account is purely military: its Defence Ministry described strikes on facilities supplying Ukraine's army and arms factories, with no comment on the Komyshuvakha aid centre or the Kharkiv hospital. The gap between those accounts is the story — one side presenting a deliberate signal to an election-day America, the other presenting routine strikes on war infrastructure.

For ordinary people, the impact is heat, light and hospital beds. Emergency and unscheduled outages spread from Odesa in the south to Rivne in the west, three DTEK plants are offline, and hospital patients in two cities were moved mid-treatment. The Komyshuvakha search continues under the threat of further strikes, with dozens of families still waiting to learn whether the missing are alive. The diplomatic calendar adds pressure: US envoy Steve Witkoff has said Washington will mediate working-level talks between Ukrainian and Russian delegations in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11, a week after a night like this one.

In short: at least 29 dead and 48 missing from Monday's aid-centre strike, a 365-weapon overnight barrage that knocked out power plants and hit hospitals, Kyiv reading it as an election-day signal to Washington, Moscow calling it strikes on military supply — and Abu Dhabi talks a week away.

US strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island land on the eve of the midterms — while Doha mediators report progress

The United States carried out limited strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island on Monday, the Wall Street Journal first reported, citing US officials — and three US officials confirmed the operation to CNN, describing it as defensive. The strike, on an island at the western end of the Strait of Hormuz that hosts IRGC naval and missile bases, came the same day Qatari mediators reported progress in indirect US–Iran talks, and on the eve of the US midterm elections after which President Donald Trump has been expected to decide on wider strikes.

The details, as reported by CNN, USA Today and the Journal via multiple relays, are still thin on damage. One US official said the strike came in response to Iran firing at a US aircraft; another described it as "a defensive measure for Iranians using surface-to-air missiles against US assets." The Journal reported that the US has carried out other such limited strikes in recent weeks without announcing them, to avoid signalling a return to major combat. Iran's state-affiliated Fars agency reported several explosions heard on Qeshm in the early hours of Tuesday local time, also heard on nearby Hengam Island and in Bandar Abbas. The IRGC claimed it shot down two US reconnaissance drones over Qeshm and destroyed an MQ-9 east of the strait — claims US Central Command has disputed in part and that remain unverified. Neither government has confirmed a strike on the record, and no targets, damage or casualties have been independently established.

Each side's public posture points in a different direction. Washington's line, through unnamed officials, is that this was protective, not escalatory — and one person familiar with the matter told CNN the administration deliberately kept it under wraps "in an effort not to overstate the defensive move." Tehran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, downplayed the incident as part of an "ongoing war" while reserving the right to defend itself, and parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf warned early Tuesday that any enemy miscalculation would meet an "unpredictable" military and strategic response. At the same time, the diplomacy track moved: Qatar's foreign ministry said its mediation team had returned to Doha after talks in Tehran, that "clear progress is emerging," and that both sides are reviewing the mediators' proposals; CNN cited diplomats saying responses are expected within two days. Vice President JD Vance said Monday the two sides have "made some progress" toward an off-ramp. Trump, for his part, told Fox News the war is "a very soft war," said a deal "could happen," and again raised Pickaxe Mountain, the suspected underground nuclear site, as a target he could hit "in one hour" whenever he wants.

For ordinary people, the stakes run through fuel prices. Oil rose in early Asian trading Tuesday after the strike news broke, after falling Monday on hopes for a deal — and CNN notes gasoline and diesel prices are on track to be the most expensive of any US Election Day in history. The strait, through which about 20 million barrels of oil a day passed before the war, is the reason a limited strike on one island moves pump prices on another continent. The two-day Hormuz reopening offer Tehran has floated — blockade lifted, sanctions waived, frozen assets released, then the strait reopens — remains the shape of any deal, and Washington's answer is still outstanding.

In short: a limited, unannounced US strike on Qeshm that Washington calls defensive and Tehran calls part of an ongoing war, landing hours before Americans vote, while Qatari mediators say the talks are making real progress and expect answers within two days. The strike and the talks are now racing each other.

Sources: CNN on the limited strikes, the defensive framing and the mediator timeline, USA Today on the strike and the IRGC drone claims, Seoul Economic Daily on Qatar's progress statement and the two-day plan

Israel's opposition agrees on Eisenkot — but the coalition maths still doesn't work

Israel's opposition leaders held their first joint meeting since the October 27 election on Tuesday morning in Herzliya, and came out with a single candidate and a plan: all participating parties will recommend Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot to President Isaac Herzog for the first mandate to form a government, their negotiating teams will immediately begin drafting guiding principles and coalition agreements, and they will move to replace the Knesset speaker at the first opportunity.

The details, as reported by Israel RealTime and Anadolu via DailyImroz, show agreement on the candidate but not on the coalition. The joint statement, issued under the Change and Reform parties, pledged a "National Emergency Government" and a "Zionist government" built on Eisenkot's four principles, and accused Likud and Netanyahu of a campaign to delegitimise the election results. Former prime minister Naftali Bennett said his Beyachad party "will fully mobilize to help Gadi Eisenkot form a government as soon as possible." But the arithmetic is unchanged: Netanyahu's bloc holds 53 of 120 seats, the Jewish opposition 48, the Arab-led parties 19, and 61 are needed. Yair Golan's position in favour of a government with the Arab parties remained a minority view at the meeting, Avigdor Liberman still refuses a government that includes or depends on Arab parties, and Joint List chairman Yousef Jabareen told CNN he will not join a coalition because he "cannot take responsibility for military actions against our Palestinian people." A separate wrinkle: Liberman said his party will back Yair Lapid for Knesset speaker, a move Channel 12 analyst Amit Segal read as pushing toward another election — while figures in Eisenkot's own party said Lapid will not become speaker, arguing it would kill almost all chances of forming a government.

Each side's argument is about legitimacy versus arithmetic. The opposition says it has a parliamentary majority for change and a decisive public vote, and that replacing the speaker and taking the mandate are the first steps to acting on it. Netanyahu's camp says he must lead any broad government and rejects one dependent on the Arab parties; his bloc, with 53 seats, remains the largest. Herzog begins two days of consultations with factions on Wednesday, starting with the smaller parties, and whoever he taps gets 28 days plus a possible 14-day extension.

For ordinary Israelis, the practical consequence is a caretaker government that stays in place while the deadlock plays out: Netanyahu remains prime minister until a new government is sworn in, and the two routes to 61 — a minority arrangement Liberman has ruled out, or a unity government Eisenkot has ruled out joining under Netanyahu — are both blocked by the people who would have to agree to them. Eisenkot is also due to begin receiving direct Shin Bet protection on Wednesday.

In short: the opposition now speaks with one voice behind Eisenkot and has a plan for the Knesset's first days, but 48 plus Arab recommendations it won't govern on still isn't a coalition — and Wednesday's presidential consultations are where that contradiction gets tested.
