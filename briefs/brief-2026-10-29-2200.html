World escalation

Night falls, strikes continue: Kyiv hit by ballistic missiles, 350,000 in Dnipro left without power — while Oleshky's aid delivery is set for Friday

Hours after Thursday's deadly daytime strikes, Russia hit Kyiv with ballistic missiles late Thursday night and knocked out power for around 350,000 people in and around Dnipro — even as both sides' envoys headed toward Oleshky, where the first aid delivery under the local truce is expected Friday.

Reuters reports that Russian forces attacked Kyiv with ballistic missiles late Thursday, hitting warehouses on the east side of the Dnipro River and starting a fire, according to Mayor Vitali Klitschko, with Reuters witnesses reporting explosions in the city. The Kyiv Independent, via its overnight report, puts the toll at one person killed in Kyiv, with warehouse buildings in the Darnytskyi district struck just after 10 p.m. local time. In Dnipro, strikes on energy infrastructure left at least 350,000 people without power, according to regional governor Oleksandr Hanzha, and another strike hit a student dormitory and set it ablaze. CNN adds a further Thursday detail: an employee of the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant was killed by a drone, an attack the International Atomic Energy Agency condemned.

The two sides' framing has not moved. President Volodymyr Zelensky called Thursday's daytime strikes "deliberate strikes against civilians, brazen in their cruelty," carried out "right as the humanitarian operation in Oleshky is unfolding," and warned Ukraine had intelligence about preparations for new ballistic missile strikes — a warning borne out hours later in Kyiv. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said she was "heartbroken" by the "senseless and indiscriminate" Dnipro attack and urged allies to mount pressure on Russia. Moscow, which has consistently denied deliberately targeting civilians, had made no public comment on the Dnipro strike in early reporting, and has made clear the Oleshky truce applies only to the Kherson region, not the wider war. Zelensky also said Russia has launched over 100 ballistic missiles against Kyiv and the surrounding region since October 14, and said in his nightly address that Ukraine had struck two Russian fighter-bomber planes and an Iskander launcher — a claim Moscow had not commented on.

For ordinary people, the night's strikes are the winter-war pattern in miniature: a capital of around three million under repeated ballistic-missile fire, and a major city, Dnipro, losing power for hundreds of thousands of households on the same day 14 people were killed on its railway platform. The Oleshky exception is the counterpoint. The six-day truce there, brokered by US First Lady Melania Trump with Pope Leo's involvement, has now held for 54 hours without a reported violation, according to Ukrainian ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets. Both ombudsmen — Lantratova for Russia, Lubinets for Ukraine — said Thursday that demining was complete and the convoy was ready, heading first for the district hospital. Reuters reports Lantratova travelled into Oleshky on Thursday with four Newsmax journalists, while Lubinets led a Ukrainian delegation to the area. Ukrainian officials expect aid delivery on Friday, October 30, with evacuations of residents who wish to leave reportedly planned for Saturday, October 31 — via Anapa in Russia and then Gomel in Belarus, according to documents reported by Fox News and Ukraine's ombudsman office, a route Kyiv has neither confirmed nor denied, chosen, the office said, because the front-line approach is mined and there is no river crossing.

In short: Thursday's toll across Ukraine rose to at least 20 — 14 on the Dnipro platform, five in daytime strikes in Chernihiv and Kremenchuk, and one in the late-night Kyiv missile attack — while the Oleshky truce held into its third day and its convoy is due to move Friday. The truce runs only until 10:59 p.m. local time on November 1, so Friday's delivery is the test of whether it is real.

Sources: Reuters, Kyiv Independent via Zot, CNN, Reuters via Duke FM, Guardian briefing via Europe Says

Houthis claim six strikes across Saudi Arabia — refineries, both main airports, a military HQ — but Riyadh confirms only two Riyadh intercepts

Yemen's Houthis claimed a six-target wave across Saudi Arabia on Thursday evening — three oil facilities, both of the kingdom's main commercial airports, and a military command headquarters — but the Saudi-led coalition confirmed only two ballistic missiles intercepted over Riyadh, leaving the other five claims unverified.

Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree named Ras Tanura refinery, Aramco's Abqaiq processing facility, the Rabigh refinery on the Red Sea coast, King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, and a Saudi military headquarters in the Najran region, according to Al Jazeera and Middle East Eye as carried by the House of Saud, with Xinhua carrying the same six-target claim. Saree framed the wave as retaliation for Saudi strikes on Houthi-held areas, saying operations would continue "until Saudi acts of aggression cease and the blockade is lifted." Coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki confirmed air defenses intercepted and destroyed two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh — the sole Saudi confirmation from any of the six claimed targets — and Saudi Civil Defence said shrapnel from the interception wounded one citizen and damaged one house and one vehicle. No Saudi, coalition or Aramco statement has addressed the other five targets, including Saree's claims of disrupted air traffic at Dammam and of deaths and wounded at the Najran headquarters.

Caption: A refinery complex at dusk in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, the same coastal industrial corridor as Ras Tanura — one of the three oil facilities the Houthis named on October 29. This is a stock image of the corridor, not a photo of Thursday's claimed strikes, for which no damage photos have been published; it matters because Abqaiq, also named, is the world's largest crude processing facility, and any confirmed hit there would move oil markets.

The gap between claim and confirmation is the pattern of this month, not an exception: on October 24 the Houthis claimed eight targets and the coalition confirmed two intercepts. What is new in this wave is Abqaiq, not publicly claimed as a Houthi target in 2026 before Thursday — the facility whose September 2019 attack temporarily removed roughly 5% of global daily oil supply. The wave also landed hours after flydubai extended its Saudi suspension to November 1, scrapping a plan to resume Riyadh and Dammam flights on October 30 — the two airports Saree named. No airline statement directly links the extension to the six-target announcement.

For ordinary people, the practical effects are already visible even without confirmation of the refinery claims: Saudi air travel remains disrupted, with Emirates set for Jeddah on October 30 but Riyadh and Dammam not until November 8 and Etihad suspended until at least November 5, and the threat alone keeps airlines away. Inside Yemen, the exchange continues in both directions and both sides' tallies are unverified: Yemen's government forces claimed 749 strikes on Houthi positions in 24 hours, neutralising 239 fighters, while the Houthis claimed 101 Saudi-led strikes across Sanaa and eight provinces, and earlier Thursday accused coalition strikes of killing 14 people at an Al-Jawf market and fuel station. The Institute for the Study of War separately notes the Yemeni government accused the Houthis of using chemical weapons in Taiz on October 28 — a claim it says came with no evidence and no local corroboration. The six-target wave also came three days after Saudi Arabia, Iran, Turkey and Pakistan issued a joint communiqué after talks in Tehran on October 26; the Houthis were not party to that meeting.

In short: six targets claimed, two Riyadh intercepts confirmed, one person wounded by shrapnel — and no confirmation yet for Abqaiq, Ras Tanura, Rabigh, Dammam airport or the Najran headquarters. Until Aramco, aviation authorities or satellite imagery weigh in, this is a claim about reach, not a confirmed hit on Saudi oil infrastructure. Developing.

Sources: House of Saud, citing Al Jazeera / Middle East Eye / SBS, FXStreet, citing Xinhua, ISW Iran Update, October 29

Cybersecurity

  • CISA adds GitLab's CVSS 10.0 flaw to KEV — and patching GitLab alone may not fix it: CVE-2026-97229, an integer overflow in how GitLab compiles regular expressions in CI/CD configs, allows unauthenticated remote code execution; the bug sits in Onigmo, the regex engine in Ruby. CISA added it October 29 with a federal deadline of November 1. Alpine Linux disclosed October 28 that its own server was probed October 20–21, crashing workers eight times, after it had upgraded to 19.3.4 instead of the fixed 19.3.5 — and that upgrading to 19.3.6 still left it vulnerable, because GitLab's fix is a patch to the Ruby bundled in its own Linux packages and container images, not in GitLab's code alone. Check your install type, not just your GitLab version. Source: PBXScience
  • CISA adds exploited Brotli decoder flaw to KEV: CVE-2026-111497 (Node2Shell) — a Brotli decoder flaw reaching well beyond Node.js into products that embed it — can remotely crash systems, expose sensitive memory or potentially execute code. This is a distinct, more specific KEV entry than the general Node2Shell warning in the evening brief. Source: IANS
  • Reminder, not new: Federal deadlines land soon for the flaws in the evening brief — Linux kernel "Fragnesia" CVE-2026-46300 by October 31, Cisco ASA/FTD CVE-2026-76418 by November 1, VMware ESX CVE-2026-41709 by November 11.
