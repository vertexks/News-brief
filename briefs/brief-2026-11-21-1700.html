Houthis now 60 km from Aden as government forces claim counter-gains

Houthi fighters have advanced to within about 60 kilometres of Aden, the seat of Yemen's internationally recognised government, after capturing two districts in neighbouring Lahj province, military and government sources told AFP. The fighters are positioned at Al-Kharaz camp in Al-Madaribah district, one of the two districts — Al-Maqatirah and Al-Madaribah — reported captured. France 24 and Al Jazeera carried the same account, and the Institute for the Study of War's November 21 update records government forces fighting Houthi fighters in northern and central Lahij to stop the advance toward Aden.

The advance is the latest step in a ground campaign that has been moving fast since the civil war reignited this summer. The government has already pulled its last forces back from the Bab el-Mandeb Strait area to defend Aden, and the Houthis have taken over evacuated positions along more than 150 km of Red Sea coastline, from Dhubab toward Khor Amira. According to sources cited by the Institute for the Study of War, the Houthis are now building fortifications — tunnels and berms — along Bab al-Mandeb to develop a long-term ability to threaten civilian shipping in the strait at close range, exploiting the vacuum left by the government's withdrawal from all positions within 65 km of the strait. Inside Aden, the government has been digging trenches, building earth barricades and setting up checkpoints, and Yemen's parliament has unanimously approved a bill designating the Houthis a terrorist organisation.

The picture is not one-sided, though the claims cut both ways and neither side's battlefield reporting can be independently verified. An independent Yemeni journalist reported on Saturday that government-aligned forces assaulted and recaptured several Houthi-held positions near Am Sarih, north of Jabal Kharaz mountain in central Lahij, and retook Jabal Habanan, about 15 km north of Am Sarih — but the Institute for the Study of War says it cannot verify any of those claims. Yemen's army said on Monday it had carried out 661 attacks on Houthi positions across several fronts, killing or wounding 173 fighters, as part of its "Dawn of Yemen" offensive launched October 5. The Houthis, for their part, say they are only hitting military and strategic targets; Riyadh calls them a terrorist militia deliberately targeting civilians.

For people in Aden, the war is now a question of distance measured in tens of kilometres, with missiles already falling on the city and the forces meant to defend it split between rival factions — the Saudi-backed government and the UAE-backed Southern Transitional Council, which has not joined the latest fighting. More than 320,000 people have been displaced since August 1, and the World Health Organization counted 11,755 casualties in the Yemen fighting as of November 8. If the Houthis take Aden, the recognised government loses its last major stronghold in the south; if they fortify Bab al-Mandeb, the threat to Red Sea shipping — already severe — gains a permanent land base.

In short: the Houthis have closed to about 60 km of Aden after taking two Lahj districts, and are digging in along Bab al-Mandeb to threaten shipping — while the government claims counter-attacks that cannot be verified, and Aden's defenders remain divided.

Sources: Nation Path India (AFP), Institute for the Study of War — Iran Update, November 21, 2026, Al Jazeera

Trump told Europe to stop funding Ukraine in an angry call after the Putin summit

President Trump rang into a meeting of European leaders in Berlin on Thursday and told them to stop pouring billions into Ukraine, according to the Telegraph, which reported the call on Friday. Two sources described his remarks as a "tirade," saying he challenged European plans to cover Ukraine's estimated €70 billion financing gap for 2027 and argued the money could instead benefit their own "beautiful countries," as the Washington Post also reported. Trump called in from Air Force One, on Jared Kushner's phone, after his trilateral summit with Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, and told the Europeans that Putin was tired of the war and ready to accept terms he previously would have rejected.

The call landed badly in Berlin, where leaders had gathered with President Zelenskyy to discuss emergency finance for Kyiv. British Prime Minister Andy Burnham and his European counterparts pushed back and reaffirmed that their support for Ukraine was unwavering. A third source offered a softer reading, saying Trump was arguing that a peace deal would save everyone money rather than ordering an end to aid — and the White House disputed the Telegraph's account, saying Trump had emphasised the war's enormous human and financial cost and that everyone should be striving for peace because it would save lives and money. Either way, no European leader publicly changed course, and the meeting went ahead with its funding plans.

The timing undercut Trump's own case. On the same day he told Europe that Putin was ready for peace, Putin publicly rejected renewed negotiations over a limited ceasefire on energy infrastructure and Black Sea shipping, repeated his demand that Ukraine surrender the remaining Donbas territory it controls, and rejected any European peacekeeping force in Ukraine after a settlement. Hours later, Russia launched its biggest barrage of the war on Kyiv. Trump's optimism after Shenzhen — he rated the summit "a 12" out of 10 — now sits against two nights of mass strikes, a NATO airspace violation in Poland, and a European funding debate he tried to shut down from 30,000 feet.

For Ukrainians, the stakes are immediate and financial as well as military: the €70 billion gap for 2027 is what keeps the state running and the army supplied, and the European Commission's plan to borrow up to €45 billion on capital markets to lend to Kyiv still needs approval from the European Parliament and member states. For Europeans, the call raised the question of whether Washington is still a reliable partner in funding the war at all.

In short: Trump used his post-summit call to Berlin to tell Europe to stop funding Ukraine and focus on his peace plan; the White House says he was only arguing peace would save money, the Europeans pushed back and carried on — and Putin's own words and missiles the same day contradicted the premise that he is ready to deal.

Sources: Europe Says (Telegraph), The Scottish Sun (Telegraph), Matzav, BSI News

Washington's Iran decision: Netanyahu pushes a 4–7 day campaign, Trump says not before January

President Trump is weighing another round of extensive military strikes on Iran, the New York Times reported on Wednesday, citing US and Israeli officials briefed on the discussions — but he has not decided, and he has told reporters he will not decide until after the new year. Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is pressing hard for the United States to join a four-to-seven-day air campaign, according to the Times, backed by Israeli intelligence warnings that Iran has rebuilt the missile and drone arsenals it lost earlier in the war and is advancing chemical and biological weapons programmes as an alternative to a nuclear weapon. Mossad director Roman Gofman travelled to Washington on November 13 to press the case with senior administration officials including CIA Director John Ratcliffe, Axios reported, and the Times says Trump recently told Vice President JD Vance to stop pursuing a negotiated settlement, favouring sustained economic pressure instead.

Trump's own public words point the other way on timing. Aboard Air Force One on Thursday he said, "I am not rushing into any decision [on Iran]. We will see what happens after the beginning of the year," and told Israel's Channel 12 the same. In a Fox News interview in Shenzhen on Wednesday he raised the chemical and biological warning himself, saying there were indications Iran was "looking at gases, looking at bio, because we've shut them out with the nuclear." The New York Post reports hawks inside and outside the administration are pressing him to resume the campaign that has been paused since the summer, and the Financial Times reports the same pressure from within the administration — while Israel's ambassador to the US, Michael Leiter, told the Post that Israel believes ousting the Tehran regime "is now the only long- or even medium-term answer" and "will not hesitate to go it alone."

The pressure runs in both directions. US Central Command has warned officials about the strain the prolonged buildup is placing on American forces — "running out of time, burning money and readiness," one source told the Financial Times — and the Pentagon puts the war's cost at more than $48 billion. Gulf states have also pushed for a delay: Just The News and Fox News report Trump postponed a planned attack after appeals from the Emir of Qatar, Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman and the UAE president, who argued for a short hold while negotiators worked toward a deal with no Iranian nuclear weapon. On Friday, Trump accused Iran of refusing to negotiate a deal acceptable to Washington, and repeated his vow that "Iran will never have a nuclear weapon ever."

The backdrop is the shipping war in the Strait of Hormuz, where two more tankers were hit on Saturday (covered in this morning's brief). The New York Post reports that since the US war with Iran began in late February, more than 120 commercial vessels have been attacked in and around the strait, killing at least 24 seafarers, according to the International Maritime Organization. More than a dozen Iranian oil tankers have gathered near Kharg Island as Tehran weighs ways to move crude despite the US naval blockade. For everyone else, the cost shows up in fuel prices and shipping insurance: Brent crude is trading around $90–92 a barrel, and every new strike keeps the risk premium in place.

In short: Israel is pushing Trump to join a four-to-seven-day air campaign against Iran, Trump says he won't decide until after the new year, and the debate is now a three-way pull — Israeli pressure, CENTCOM warning about strain, and Gulf states asking for time to negotiate — while the tanker war in the strait keeps escalating.

Sources: The Boston Report (NYT), News One USA (NYT), American Tribune, New York Post

Ukraine — new details, no new barrage yet

Zelenskyy confirms Russia used cluster warheads in Friday's record barrage — President Zelenskyy confirmed on Friday that Russia deployed missiles carrying cluster warheads during the November 19–20 attack on Ukraine's energy infrastructure, describing the sequencing as "particularly vile": successive salvos at the same targets with 20-to-30-minute intervals, timed to catch firefighters and repair crews in the open. The barrage — more than 400 drones and over 110 missiles, 27 of them ballistic — killed at least eight people and knocked out power and water across Kyiv and ten other regions. Source: Europe Says

Ukrainian strikes kill three in Russia's Belgorod region; Crimea loses power — Ukrainian drone strikes killed three people in Russia's Belgorod region, Al Jazeera reports, and Ukrainian strikes on energy infrastructure caused power outages in parts of Russian-occupied Crimea, where temporary outage schedules are in force. Russia's Defence Ministry said it hit two power stations in Ukraine's Kyiv and Zhytomyr regions. Zelenskyy said the overnight attacks damaged power generation facilities in nine Ukrainian regions. Source: Al Jazeera

Cybersecurity

FBI's national crime database breached — again — and Trump downplays it — The FBI is investigating an apparent breach of its National Crime Information Center (NCIC), the database federal, state and local law enforcement use to share information on warrants and background checks, CNN first reported on Friday, citing two people briefed on the matter. It is the second such breach in recent months. How the intruders got in, what they accessed and who is behind it are not public, and the FBI has not commented. President Trump, asked about it in a Fox News phone interview on Saturday, said "it's not good — I don't like that," and used the question to revisit his grievances about the FBI's 2016 Russia investigation. CNN notes the breach comes amid a series of high-profile outages affecting banks, the National Security Agency and the FAA's air traffic control system. Source: CNN

Microsoft Entra Connect Health flaw was exploited to read tenant data — Microsoft is notifying organisations that attackers exploited a vulnerability in the Entra Connect Health API to get read-only access to tenant-level metadata: email addresses of technical contacts and administrator accounts, tenant IDs and names, licence tiers and directory sync status. Microsoft found the flaw on November 9 and deployed a server-side fix on November 19. No passwords, tokens or valid keys were exposed, Microsoft says. It has not said who exploited the flaw or how many tenants were hit. Single-source (threat-watch aggregator). Source: Threat Watch

Prism Medical Products breach hit 1.32M+ people — Prism Medical Products, a North Carolina home medical supplies company owned by Henry Schein, says an unauthorized third party accessed parts of its environment and obtained files after suspicious activity spotted October 19. The files may include medical, health insurance, payment and financial account data and government ID numbers such as Social Security numbers. Its filing with the Oregon attorney general puts the number affected at more than 1.32 million. Prism has not said how the attackers got in. Source: Threat Watch

Java4Shell: no change. Still no confirmed in-the-wild exploitation and still not in CISA KEV; GreyNoise is seeing only trigger-shaped probing requests.
