Evening Brief — Wednesday, November 18, 2026

Ukraine's drones have nearly halved Russia's oil refining — and the grid war is now raising nuclear fears

Ukrainian drone attacks have slashed Russian oil refineries' combined crude processing by 45% since late October, according to industry estimates reported exclusively by Reuters on Wednesday — the clearest measure yet of how far Kyiv's long-range campaign has bitten. At the same time, Russia kept hitting Ukrainian cities, killing four people in Odesa, and nuclear safety regulators from 38 countries warned that the grid war is raising the risk of a nuclear accident this winter.

Reuters reports, citing two industry sources, that Russian refineries' crude processing has fallen to around 400,000 metric tons a day — about 3 million barrels a day — from more than 700,000 tons a day at the end of October. The attacks have now disrupted or halted operations at 15 of Russia's 35 major refineries, up from 9 in the middle of last week, including plants in Ryazan, Yaroslavl, Kirishi and the Volga region. The combined nominal capacity of the refineries affected since late October stands at around 500,000 tons a day, roughly 54% of Russia's total primary refining capacity. Russia is already facing domestic fuel shortages in some regions and restricted export capacity, Reuters reports, and reduced refining could tighten global fuel markets and hamper Moscow's ability to deliver on a deal to export diesel to the United States. The sources cautioned that because the reduction was gradual, average November processing is still above 500,000 tons a day — about 5% to 10% below September and October levels.

The refining squeeze follows nearly three weeks of near-daily Ukrainian drone strikes, capped by what Russia's Defence Ministry said on Monday was the biggest overnight drone attack on Russia since the full-scale invasion began — 1,441 drones claimed intercepted, a record. That barrage included the two-night operation that collapsed the road arch of the Kerch Bridge to Crimea, reported in yesterday's briefs. President Zelenskyy has claimed Ukraine has disabled more than 55% of Russian refining capacity; the industry sources cited by Reuters put the processing cut at 45%, a lower but independently sourced figure. Trump announced an "energy ceasefire" on October 11 that neither Moscow nor Kyiv acknowledged agreeing to; Ukrainian strikes eased for roughly two weeks, then intensified again as Russia ramped up its own drone and missile strikes.

Russia's answer on Wednesday was aimed at Ukrainian cities and the grid. Russian forces struck the centre of Odesa, the Black Sea port, in three waves, killing four people and injuring 13, according to regional officials cited by Reuters. Windows of residential buildings, cafes and shops were broken, a car was set on fire, and a residential building and an educational institution were damaged. President Zelenskyy said Shahed drones struck the centre of the city. The strikes came a day after Russia hit Kyiv's North and South bridges with seven ballistic missiles and as Ukraine's Energy Ministry counts more than 1,000 Russian strikes on energy facilities since September, leaving many Kyiv homes with power for only around four hours a day.

The grid campaign has now drawn a formal warning from nuclear regulators. Three international associations — HERCA, WENRA and NORA, representing regulators from 38 countries including all EU members, the United States, Britain, Canada and Japan — warned on Monday, in a joint statement reported by Reuters, that Russia's intensifying attacks on Ukraine's electricity infrastructure are increasing the risk of a nuclear accident this winter. Their concern is that reactors need reliable electricity to keep their fuel cool even when shut down, and prolonged outages force plants onto emergency generators. Per Strand, who heads HERCA and NORA, told Reuters that "the highest risk will be this winter for a nuclear accident," and described a nationwide blackout affecting all nine of Ukraine's operating reactors at once as the worst-case scenario. Ukraine's three working nuclear plants are expected to supply about 80% of the country's electricity this winter, according to Reuters — before the war, nuclear was just over half. The International Atomic Energy Agency said on Saturday that grid instability from military activity had forced several operating reactors to cut output in the preceding week, and that a drone had been detected about 350 metres from the South Ukraine plant. At Zaporizhzhia, Europe's largest plant, which sits under Russian occupation and has generated no electricity since 2022, the facility lost all external power for more than 12 hours on November 8 — its 27th complete loss of off-site power since the invasion — and has spent a cumulative two months without external electricity over the past year, according to Bellona's analysis.

What each side says: Ukraine frames its long-range strikes as degrading the fuel and money that power Russia's war machine, and Zelenskyy has presented the refining campaign as a deliberate strategy. Russia presents its own strikes as hitting energy infrastructure that supports Ukraine's war effort, claims to intercept the overwhelming majority of Ukrainian drones, and says the Kerch Bridge will be rebuilt "even better than before" — though one analyst told reporters it "won't be repaired during this war." The nuclear regulators, for their part, urged Moscow to halt strikes on nuclear facilities and the infrastructure that supplies them, and urged European governments to coordinate emergency preparations.

For ordinary people, the squeeze is now visible on both sides. In Russia, fuel shortages are already appearing in some regions and some areas have reintroduced rationing, according to earlier reporting this month; in Ukraine, civilians in Odesa are being killed in city-centre strikes while Kyiv heads into freezing weather on a few hours of power a day. The nuclear warning adds a new dimension: a grid failure at the wrong moment would not just mean cold homes, but a potential radiological emergency that, as the regulators note, would not respect borders.

In short: Kyiv's drone campaign has now cut Russian oil refining nearly in half by one independent measure, while Russia's retaliation keeps hitting Ukrainian cities and the power grid — and the people who regulate nuclear safety in 38 countries say this winter carries the highest accident risk of the war so far. Watch whether Russia's refining keeps falling, whether the grid holds as temperatures drop, and what, if anything, Thursday's Shenzhen trilateral produces on Ukraine.

Gulf: condemnations pile up, but oil keeps falling anyway

"Kuwait says Iran attacked it with drones overnight, and Iran says it hit a US base in Kuwait" — Rest of the World Report

No new strikes were reported in the Gulf today, but the diplomatic fallout from overnight's attacks on Kuwait and Bahrain kept growing. Kuwait's Foreign Ministry condemned the drone attack as a "flagrant violation of its sovereignty," Bahrain called Iran's attacks on its territory criminal, and the Gulf Cooperation Council, Qatar and the UAE condemned the strikes on both countries. GCC Secretary-General Jasem Mohamed Albudaiwi held Iran responsible for the escalation. The Arab League asked the UN Security Council to act. Iran's Revolutionary Guard warned that any use of regional military bases for hostile operations against Iran "will not go unanswered," and Iranian Army chief Maj. Gen. Amir Hatami said his forces were monitoring enemy movements after a third US aircraft carrier arrived in the region. Meanwhile the oil market shrugged: Brent crude traded around $92.50 a barrel on Wednesday, down roughly 0.5–0.7% on the day and about 20% lower for the month, as a 5.3-million-barrel build in US crude inventories and the International Energy Agency's finding that global supply has surpassed demand outweighed the fighting. One analysis notes Brent has fallen about 16% over the month despite the attacks on Kuwait, Bahrain and tankers in the strait.

Shenzhen: Xi and Putin held their own talks first — and Trump gets a one-on-one with Putin on Thursday

"Xi, Trump and Putin gather in Shenzhen for first trilateral summit" — Kilambi News

Ahead of Thursday's first-ever trilateral, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin held a bilateral meeting in Shenzhen on Wednesday lasting more than two hours — their fifth meeting this year — and both used their APEC remarks to criticise Western trade protectionism and sanctions, without naming the US directly. The Associated Press reported on Wednesday that Trump will also meet Putin separately on Thursday, in addition to the three-way session — their first face-to-face meeting since the 2025 Alaska summit, which failed to produce a deal on Ukraine. Thursday's schedule, per published reports: an APEC leaders' family photograph at 9:30 a.m. local time, a Trump–Xi bilateral at 12:45 p.m., and the closed-door trilateral at 2:30 p.m. local time, with nuclear arms, Ukraine and Iran on the table. The trilateral's nuclear stakes are sharpened by the expiry of the New START treaty in February 2026, which ended the last formal limits on US and Russian strategic arsenals. Analysts still expect no substantive breakthroughs, and whether a separate Trump–Putin meeting takes place is disputed between reports — Axios says yes, citing a US official; the Kremlin has not confirmed it.

Yemen: 15 killed in a coalition strike on a Hodeidah factory, and shippers eye a return to Suez

"Red Sea Shipping Disruption: Conditional Route Return Amid Yemen Escalation" — CrisisBrief

Houthi health officials reported 15 people killed and 13 wounded in a coalition strike on a foam factory in Hodeidah, according to a new situation analysis — a casualty claim that has not been independently verified. The strike came during the coalition's campaign against Houthi positions as the group advances toward Aden, reported in this morning's brief. Separately, Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd have announced a conditional return of three of their Gemini services to the Suez route from December 2026 or January 2027, the first sign that major carriers may start testing Red Sea transits again despite the Houthi advance on the Bab al-Mandeb approaches — though actual sailings, rather than the announcement, will show whether route avoidance is really easing.

Java4Shell: exploit code is now circulating, still no confirmed attacks

"Nov 18 Advisory: OpenJDK Remote Code Execution (Java4Shell) [CVE-2026-114938]" — Censys

The Java4Shell flaw (CVE-2026-114938) reported in this morning's brief has moved a step closer to real-world exploitation. Censys updated its advisory on Wednesday to note that exploit code is now circulating: public repositories claim, without independent verification, working exploit chains against lab applications, and VulnCheck says a full exploit is available to its customers. The researchers who found the flaw say they are holding back app-specific exploits to give people time to update. As of today there is still no confirmed in-the-wild exploitation and the flaw is not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The researchers demonstrated pre-authentication remote code execution against default installs of Keycloak, Atlassian Jira, Atlassian Confluence, JFrog Artifactory, Jenkins and PingFederate, and authenticated code execution on Kafka, Solr, Elasticsearch, Gerrit and Spinnaker. Oracle rates it 8.1; the researchers rate it 9.2. If you run Java 17, 21, 25 or 27 on x86-64 servers with AVX-512 VBMI processors, install your vendor's November update — or restart affected JVMs with the -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics flag until a fixed build is in place.

Sources: Censys · Malware News · ThreatAft

CISA added four exploited flaws to its catalog today — including the Apache Axis zero-day

"CISA KEV Additions by Date" — East Bay Cyber

CISA added four vulnerabilities to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on Wednesday, November 18: CVE-2024-10441 (Synology DiskStation Manager), CVE-2026-40217 (BerriAI LiteLLM — unprotected alternate channel), CVE-2026-90817 (Vanderbilt REDCap — code injection), and CVE-2026-90436 (Apache Axis — unsafe reflection). The last one is the zero-day behind the N-able N-central compromise reported in last night's brief as single-source and unconfirmed — its KEV listing now confirms it is being exploited in the wild, with 21 servers reported compromised. The catalog now stands at 1,786 entries, with 38 added in the last 30 days. Federal agencies must patch the new entries by their CISA deadlines; everyone else should treat KEV additions as patch-now.

Netanyahu trial: defense drops its objection — prosecutors can now cross-examine Mandelblit

"Netanyahu trial prosecutors to cross-examine Mandelblit after defense drops objection" — JBizNews

Prosecutors in Benjamin Netanyahu's criminal trial will be allowed to cross-examine former attorney-general Avichai Mandelblit after defense lawyers withdrew their objection on Wednesday, easing the restrictions that prompted an unusual prosecution petition to the High Court of Justice this week. The Jerusalem District Court said the arrangement will apply going forward to witnesses called by the defense, whether or not they had originally appeared on the prosecution's witness list — expressly including Mandelblit, whose testimony has focused on his authorization of the investigations into Netanyahu and the indictment. The dispute before the High Court is not fully over: defense lawyers asked for the petition to be struck out and Sunday's hearing canceled, while prosecutors sought clarification of the law, and Supreme Court President Isaac Amit directed the state to say by noon on Thursday whether it is maintaining its petition. Mandelblit's questioning by the defense has not finished — his testimony was interrupted after additional material about his investigation approvals was disclosed to the defense.
