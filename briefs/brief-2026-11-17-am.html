World escalation

Bridges on both sides: Ukraine collapses the Kerch road span as Russia hits Kyiv's crossings

Ukraine severely damaged the bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea overnight, collapsing a section of its road span, in what President Volodymyr Zelensky called an "unprecedented" operation — while Russia answered by firing seven ballistic missiles at two of Kyiv's main bridges across the Dnipro, killing at least two people in the capital and knocking out power, water and heating. It was the second straight night of record-scale drone warfare on both sides, and it landed on the morning of the Ramstein defence meeting in Brussels and two days before Thursday's Trump–Putin–Xi summit in Shenzhen.

The details, as reported by the Press Association, Reuters and Ukrainian officials: Ukraine's General Staff said an arch and a span of the Kerch Bridge's road section collapsed, severing the road link, while the railway line was still standing but its arch was damaged. Zelensky said the attack was the culmination of weeks of preparatory strikes in seven stages — knocking out air defences, radars and electronic-warfare equipment first — before domestically built long-range drones hit the road section and Neptune missiles and other drones targeted the rail section, with Flamingo precision-guided missiles delivering the final blow. Russia's Investigative Committee said the bridge was attacked by drones and sustained damage; Crimea's Moscow-installed governor, Sergei Aksyonov, said traffic was halted but rail traffic was restored by Tuesday afternoon, without mentioning damage. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said there was "no doubt that the bridge will be restored and will be even better than it was before." Russia's Defence Ministry said its air defences downed 1,441 Ukrainian drones overnight — a day after claiming 1,336, the previous record — and Moscow's mayor said around 800 drones flew toward the capital region, 321 shot down as they approached. Independent outlet Astra reported a huge fire at the Gazprom Neftekhim Salavat petrochemical complex in Bashkortostan, nearly 1,300 km from the border.

In Kyiv, Zelensky said four ballistic missiles hit the Southern Bridge and three hit the Northern Bridge. Both were closed to traffic, the metro system was partially damaged, and fires broke out in four districts. Ukraine's State Emergency Service reported two deaths in Kyiv's Obolonskyi district. Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov said overnight strikes left consumers without power in eight Ukrainian regions; Zelensky put the figure at significant outages in three regions. Russian strikes also hit Zaporizhzhia, killing two people and wounding 18, and industrial facilities in three districts of the region. The Russian Defence Ministry confirmed strikes on Ukrainian transport, energy, military and port infrastructure.

The two sides are telling very different stories about what this means. Kyiv frames the Kerch strike as a masterstroke against the symbol of Russia's 2014 annexation — the General Staff's statement was blunt: "The symbol of the occupation has cracked." UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting, meeting allies at NATO headquarters in Brussels, called it "another example of Ukrainian ingenuity and Ukraine's absolute determination to defend itself." Moscow, for its part, treats the bridge as repairable and the Kyiv strikes as routine — its Defence Ministry described the overnight barrage as a strike on military-industrial, energy, transport and port infrastructure, and Peskov promised a better bridge. Zelensky, meanwhile, said Russia has battered Ukrainian infrastructure, especially the power grid, every day, and that "we have repeatedly offered Russia steps toward de-escalation — and every proposal has been met with loud rejection." He also said on Monday that Russia is receiving dozens of ballistic missiles from North Korea every month and urged allies to give Ukraine comparable weapons for "missile parity." On diplomacy, the negotiating teams of Ukraine, the US and European partners held a working video call on Monday about the Abu Dhabi talks and next steps; Rustem Umerov, head of Ukraine's Foreign Intelligence Service, said "we aligned our positions and our next joint steps." The US was represented by envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and the commander of US forces in Europe, Alexus Grynkewich.

For ordinary people, this is now a war on the routes they use every day. In Kyiv, two of the eight bridges across the Dnipro are shut, buses and trolleybuses have been diverted, the metro is partly damaged, and parts of the city are without water, power and heating as winter closes in. In Crimea, the road link that moves military and civilian supplies to the peninsula is severed, at least for now. And in Russia, the drone war is reaching deeper — Bashkortostan is nearly 1,300 km from Ukraine's border. The immediate test is today in Brussels: Germany's Defence Minister Boris Pistorius announced a $300 million contribution to NATO's PURL programme for US weapons for Ukraine — its fifth package — focused on PAC-2 and PAC-3 Patriot missiles, with Germany and Norway sponsoring the package. Patriot missiles are the only weapon reliable enough to stop Russian ballistic missiles, but there is a global shortage, made worse since the US launched its war against Iran. Germany is also working to integrate its IRIS-T missile with Norwegian NASAMS systems and will deliver additional radars, drone ammunition and fighter jets. NATO allies will also discuss Washington's review of US troop levels in Europe with Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby, who is attending the Ramstein meeting; more clarity on any drawdown is expected within weeks.

In short: both sides struck the other's bridges overnight — Ukraine collapsed part of the Kerch road span after weeks of preparation, Russia hit Kyiv's North and South bridges with seven ballistic missiles and cut power in eight regions — in the biggest drone exchange of the war so far. Watch today's Ramstein meeting for Patriot missiles, and Thursday's Shenzhen trilateral, where Trump says he will raise Ukraine with Xi and Putin even though US officials say it is not on the agenda.

Two more tankers hit in Hormuz as the US and Iran trade blows — and oil falls anyway

The Gulf war's sharpest escalation in days came on Sunday night and Monday, with two more tankers confirmed hit in the Strait of Hormuz, a US strike on Iranian missile launchers reported but not confirmed by Washington, a drone alarm at Iraq's biggest gas field, and the Houthis closing in on Aden — yet oil prices fell, not rose, as traders bet on a global supply surplus.

The details, as reported by Reuters and Al Jazeera: the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency confirmed two tanker attacks in the strait on Monday. In the latest, a tanker was disabled and an engine-room fire broke out after an attack seven nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman; earlier, another tanker was struck by an unidentified projectile while travelling out of the strait, also causing an engine-room fire. No casualties were reported in either. Maritime monitor Vanguard identified the first vessel as the Panama-flagged On Phoenix, hit about three nautical miles off Oman. Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy said two oil tankers struck naval mines and caught fire while trying to cross the southern part of the strait through a route Tehran considers unauthorised, and Iran's regular navy said another tanker caught fire and was immobilised after hitting a mine east of the strait — bringing the Guard's claimed tally to six tankers on fire since Sunday. None of the Iranian mine claims has been independently verified, and Iranian authorities have not identified the vessels. The Guard also said it seized a US MQ-170 drone near Qeshm Island after an electronic-warfare operation; that claim is also unverified. Euronews reported that the US military launched airstrikes on Iranian missile launchers at Bandar Lengeh and Sirik in southern Iran on Sunday night, after Iranian forces attacked a US base in Bahrain and fired on tankers — but US Central Command has not confirmed the strikes, and other outlets note the report cites no confirming source. Iran did carry out a drone attack on a radar at the US Al Riffa air base in Bahrain on Sunday, its second in three days; Bahrain and Washington say every drone was shot down, with no injuries or damage. The USS Theodore Roosevelt arrived in the region over the weekend, joining the USS George Washington and USS George H.W. Bush — three US carrier strike groups in the Middle East for the first time since the Iraq war.

The fighting is also spreading inland. In Iraq's Kurdistan region, warning sirens sounded on Monday after a drone flew over the Khor Mor gas field, Reuters reported, citing two security sources. There were no immediate reports of a strike or damage — but production at Khor Mor had already been halted the previous week after a suspected drone attack sparked a fire and knocked out 2.8 gigawatts of electricity generation, according to Dana Gas, one of the field's operators. The field supplies most of the natural gas for power generation in Iraqi Kurdistan, and Kurdish officials have blamed Iranian-backed armed groups for repeated attacks on it. No group was identified as responsible for Monday's flyover. In Yemen, the Houthis have advanced to about 60 km from Aden after government-aligned forces withdrew from positions overlooking the Bab al-Mandeb Strait to reinforce the city's defence; the Houthis now control the area overlooking the strategic waterway. The International Organization for Migration said at least 348,000 people have been displaced in fighting between Saudi-backed government forces and the Houthis since August 1, including 18,780 between November 8 and 14. Saudi Arabia replaced the commander of its coalition forces in Yemen on Monday, appointing General Fayyad al-Ruwaili in place of Lieutenant General Fahd bin Hamad al-Salman. The Houthis claimed Saudi forces launched 88 airstrikes and missile attacks in 24 hours against Sanaa and several governorates; those claims have not been independently verified.

The two sides' positions are hardening in opposite directions. Tehran is floating a two-day plan to reopen Hormuz — Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, in Muscat on Tuesday after an Economic Cooperation Organisation meeting in Islamabad, said the organisation welcomed the proposal — but it is conditional on Washington accepting Iran's terms, and Tehran has ruled out curbs on uranium enrichment and IAEA access to damaged nuclear sites. Iran also warned vessels against entering areas it considers unauthorised and said ships must coordinate with its armed forces. Washington, for its part, has not confirmed the reported Sunday-night strikes, and Vice President JD Vance said there was no disagreement between him and President Trump over Iran, acknowledging the political cost of higher energy prices but saying the conflict would not become a "forever war." Trump has said further strikes on Iran are "quite unlikely" and that the military part of the war is "effectively over" — even as the fighting widens.

For ordinary people, the disconnect between the battlefield and the market is the story. Brent crude fell to about $91.66–$91.80 a barrel on Tuesday, down roughly 0.8%, with WTI around $85, despite the fresh tanker attacks — the IEA said on Friday that the global oil market will return to a surplus in the fourth quarter, with supply running about 1.7 million barrels a day above demand, and Middle East exports recovering faster than demand. Traffic through the strait is still only about 49% of its pre-war level, according to ANZ bank estimates, and marine underwriters have repriced transit exposure to near-prohibitive levels. The human cost is concentrated in Yemen, where more than a third of a million people have been displaced since August, and in Iraqi Kurdistan, where the Khor Mor shutdown has already cut power generation. Iran is also facing warnings of winter gas shortages, with its government forecasting a peak-demand deficit of about 430 million cubic metres per day.

In short: two tankers were confirmed hit in Hormuz on Monday, Iran claims six on fire since Sunday (unverified), the US is reported to have struck missile launchers at Bandar Lengeh and Sirik (unconfirmed by CENTCOM), a drone buzzed Iraq's Khor Mor gas field, and the Houthis are closing on Aden — but oil fell anyway on surplus forecasts. Watch Araghchi's talks in Muscat today, and whether CENTCOM confirms the Sunday-night strikes.

Tech products

Nvidia's first earnings without Huang land tonight — with the stock down 30% in a month

Nvidia reports third-quarter results after the market closes today, its first earnings call since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO on November 12 — and the numbers will land with the stock at its lowest in months and the whole AI rally on trial. Interim CEO Colette Kress, who is also still CFO, leads the call at 5 p.m. ET, with results expected at about 4:20 p.m. ET.

The details, as reported by USA Today, CoinDesk and market data: consensus is revenue of about $108.7–$109.1 billion and adjusted earnings of about $2.47 a share, against Nvidia's own guidance of $108 billion, plus or minus 2%. The quarter ended October 25, before Huang resigned, so the results will show what Nvidia delivered under him; the fourth-quarter outlook from Kress will be the first look at what management expects after the handover, and options imply a swing of roughly 7–9% either way. The stock closed Monday at $156.14, down 4.12% on the day and its lowest close since July 1, 2025, after falling about 12% last week. It was trading around $155.26 in Tuesday morning trade, down about 27.5% over the past month and roughly 30% below its October record high of $243.37, with a market capitalisation of about $3.92 trillion. The Nasdaq fell 2.49% on Monday into a correction, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index lost 5.3%. Reuters, citing anonymous sources, reported that Nvidia's board is not considering bringing Huang back, despite a "Stand with Jensen" petition that has passed 180,000 signatures, including thousands of employees and shareholders. Huang is due to give a closed-door congressional interview before Thanksgiving and testify under oath to the Senate Banking Committee on December 3. Anthropic's IPO, with trading set to start Thursday on Nasdaq at a targeted valuation of about $1.4 trillion, is the other big test of the AI market this week.

The arguments around the company split two ways. The bull case, such as it is, is that the business itself is still growing fast — consensus revenue is up about 91% year on year — and that demand for AI hardware has not gone away. The bear case is about leadership and trust: Huang stepped down amid allegations involving an extramarital relationship and an investigation into whether he had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation, and investors are asking what Nvidia is without the founder who built it over 33 years. Some big shareholders, including Altimeter's Brad Gerstner, have urged a slow, deliberate CEO search and keeping a possible Huang return on the table; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has called for a broader leadership shake-up and an outsider. Reuters also reported on Tuesday that Nvidia has warned customers about legal exposure once the US Remote Access Security Act took effect and stopped supplying buyers who sublease more than half their GPUs to Chinese firms.

For ordinary people, the stakes are mostly in retirement accounts and the broader market: Nvidia is the world's third-largest company and the anchor of the AI rally, so a bad print tonight could drag the Nasdaq further into its correction. Separately, Nvidia announced its Vera CPU rack-scale platform for high-performance computing at the SC26 supercomputing conference in Chicago on Tuesday — 192 of its first fully custom data-centre CPUs, interconnected by Quantum-X800 InfiniBand networking, with more than 200 TB/s of aggregate peak memory bandwidth for climate modelling, molecular dynamics and agentic science. Racks are expected from Dell, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro from early 2027. ASUS and Penguin Solutions also announced Vera Rubin NVL72 rack systems shipping now, with 72 Rubin GPUs and 36 Vera CPUs per rack.

In short: tonight's earnings are really two tests — the quarter Huang delivered, and the outlook Kress gives for the company without him. Watch the 4:20 p.m. ET release and the 5 p.m. call, especially the fourth-quarter guidance, and whether the board says anything new about the CEO search.

Cybersecurity

  • Critical Apache HTTP/2 flaw CVE-2026-119618: unauthenticated remote code execution, patch now: Apache fixed a critical race condition in mod_http2 in version 2.4.71, released November 11. The flaw lets an unauthenticated remote attacker corrupt server memory and potentially execute code with the privileges of the Apache process — but only on servers with HTTP/2 enabled; HTTP/1.1-only servers are not exposed. Versions 2.4.55 through 2.4.70 are affected, as is standalone mod_h2 2.0.8 through 2.0.42. Red Hat scores it 9.8; CISA scores it 8.1. A public proof-of-concept showing heap corruption appeared on November 12, but no public RCE exploit and no confirmed in-the-wild exploitation, and it is not in CISA's KEV catalog. Notably, the flaw was found by Anthropic's OSS Scanner — the free LLM vulnerability scanner for open-source projects — alongside Google Cloud Vulnerability Research. Why it matters: Apache runs a huge share of the web, and HTTP/2 is on by default in many setups; upgrade to 2.4.71 or your distribution's fixed package. (Wordfence · CIRCL · Sysdig)
  • CISA KEV: no new additions. The catalog still stands at 1,781 entries (version 2026.11.13). The most recent additions were November 13 (Check Point CVE-2026-123039 and UnrealIRCd CVE-2026-114713, whose federal deadlines passed yesterday). A federal deadline for Apple CVE-2024-27834 hits tomorrow, November 18, and SonicWall SonicOS CVE-2026-19868 is due November 20. (Daily CISO · ImmunitySec)
  • UK NCSC launched a Cyber Adversary Simulation scheme on November 11, alongside its 2026 Annual Review, "State of the Cyber Nation," published November 3. Worth knowing if you work in UK cyber; not a vulnerability. (Sweat Digital)

Major court cases

  • Knesset speaker vote today: Kahana set to replace Ohana: Israel's Knesset is voting at 4 p.m. local time on Tuesday to elect a successor to Speaker Amir Ohana, after 67 lawmakers signed a request to put the election on the agenda. The opposition bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot has nominated Matan Kahana, a lawmaker from Eisenkot's Yashar party and former religious affairs minister; Likud nominated Ofir Katz, backed by Shas and United Torah Judaism. Kahana was expected to win with support from the Arab parties — the Joint List signalled its backing — completing the opposition's control of key parliamentary positions before government formation. The vote matters because the speaker chairs debates and decides what reaches the floor. (Asia Today · News in Israel · Jewish Rhode Island)
  • Apple to face trial in France over planned obsolescence: Apple will be tried in France in June 2027 to answer accusations that it obstructed iPhone repairs "outside its authorized channels," the association Halte à l'Obsolescence Programmée (HOP) announced on Monday. HOP filed the complaint four years ago; it calls out "serialization" — parts paired to specific devices. Apple stores currently charge over €400 to replace a screen on the latest-generation iPhone. Why it matters: a conviction could force Apple to loosen repair restrictions in France and set a precedent across the EU. (Le Monde)
  • Russell Brand: no fresh developments overnight. The Ministry of Justice investigation into his prison message — read out by his wife on YouTube on Sunday — continues; Downing Street called it "clearly appalling" on Monday. No appeal has been filed; the windows run to December 3 for conviction and December 7 for sentence.
