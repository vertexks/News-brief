World escalation

A night of drones on both sides — and a public fight over Starlink

Russia fired 153 Shahed-type drones at Ukraine overnight into Sunday, including 92 faster jet-powered variants, along with low-cost Banderol cruise missiles, according to Ukraine's Air Force as reported by the Kyiv Independent. Ukraine said it neutralized 119 of the 153 drones, with debris and impacts recorded at 15 locations. The human toll is still being reconciled across reports: the Kyiv Independent-based tally puts 8 people killed and 51 wounded across Ukraine over the past day, while other Sunday reports cite at least 4 killed and 32 injured. A drone hit a residential building in Vinnytsia, injuring six; in Synelnykove, Dnipropetrovsk region, a strike on an apartment building killed four people, including victims who later died in hospital, and injured at least 13, including an 11-year-old girl. In Kharkiv, Russian forces first struck a home-improvement hypermarket, then hit the emergency crews who arrived — a double-tap strike that injured first responders and destroyed a fire engine tanker.

Russia reported its own casualties from Ukrainian strikes. Belgorod's governor said an overnight Ukrainian drone attack on the city killed two people and injured 13, damaging 30 apartment blocks, and another person was killed near Shebekino. In Bryansk region, a drone attack on a minibus belonging to an agricultural enterprise killed two and seriously injured two more. Russia's Defence Ministry said its forces struck Ukrainian logistics centres, port infrastructure and a dry-cargo vessel it claimed was carrying supplies for Ukraine's armed forces near Chornomorsk, and claimed 532 Ukrainian drones were intercepted over 25 Russian regions overnight. Reuters noted it could not independently verify the ministry's claims, and the same caveat applies to both sides' tallies.

The two sides also fought publicly over the satellite link Ukrainian forces depend on. SpaceX CEO Elon Musk wrote on X that Ukraine's military violated Starlink's terms of service by using the civilian service for offensive drone strikes to "kill people," and said that "loophole was closed last week" — a statement that, if it refers to the speed restrictions Ukrainian officials complained about for weeks, would explain them. Ukrainian presidential adviser Serhiy Beskrestnov pushed back, saying commercial Starlink terminals are technically incapable of supporting long-range strikes because of active speed restrictions. Meanwhile Kyiv says Starlink/Starshield access has been restored to full speeds, improving drone command-and-control — a claim that sits uneasily next to Musk's post, and neither side has fully explained the sequence.

President Zelensky used the overnight toll to press his case for air defence. In a Sunday post he said Russia launched around 1,600 guided aerial bombs, 48 missiles and nearly 2,300 attack drones over the past week, hitting a thermal power plant, substations, coal mines, grain storage, shopping malls and a postal logistics hub across nine regions. "This is how Russia responds to diplomacy and deisolation," he wrote, urging European partners to unblock already-approved Patriot interceptor deliveries and keep supporting Ukrainian combat aviation against jet-powered Shaheds.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the pattern is now the war itself: apartment blocks, shops and warehouses hit nightly, emergency crews targeted while responding, and winter approaching with the power grid already under strain. The Oleshky local ceasefire — the one agreed pause in weeks — is still set for Tuesday, October 27, but nothing in the overnight exchange suggests the wider war is slowing for it.

In short: both sides traded heavy drone strikes overnight with civilians killed on both sides of the border, the casualty counts differ by outlet and remain unverified, and the Starlink dispute has moved from quiet restrictions into an open argument between Musk and Kyiv over what the system may be used for.

Riyadh airport reopens its first terminals as the coalition says it stopped 10 missiles

King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh reopened Terminals 1 and 2 on Sunday, the first sign of normal operations returning after two weeks in which the airport was repeatedly targeted — including the October 10 strike that killed 13 people and wounded more than 300. The Houthis immediately called the reopened airport a "military target" and warned it is not safe for airlines, employees or travellers, renewing the evacuation warning they have issued since the airport campaign began.

The reopening came under fire. The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a ballistic missile and a drone over Riyadh overnight, destroyed a Houthi cruise missile aimed at Khamis Mushait on Sunday morning, and shortly before noon said it had intercepted and destroyed ten Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at the Southern Region. No casualties were linked to the latest interceptions in coalition statements, and there was no independent confirmation of damage from the Houthi side's claims. The Houthis had claimed seven operations on Saturday, including strikes on Riyadh and Dammam airports, Yanbu Aramco facilities, the Rabigh refinery and the East-West pipeline — claims Saudi authorities have not confirmed for the oil sites.

The two narratives have barely moved all month, and that is the story: the coalition reports interceptions and counts no hits; the Houthis claim hits on airports and energy sites and count no need for Saudi confirmation. What changed Sunday is the airport itself — Riyadh is testing whether partial reopening is sustainable while missiles are still being fired at it, and the Houthis are testing whether they can keep that bet from paying off. Egypt's parliament is due to resume its session on Saudi defence authorisation on Monday, October 26, a vote that could formalise more regional backing for the coalition.

For travellers and for the region's economy, the stakes are concrete: Riyadh's airport is the kingdom's main gateway, its closure has already forced airlines to suspend or reroute Gulf services for weeks, and every intercepted missile over the capital keeps the risk premium on Gulf aviation and shipping where it is. If Terminals 1 and 2 stay open through the week, that is a genuine de-escalation signal; if a missile gets through, the closure returns overnight.

In short: Riyadh reopened part of its airport under fire, the coalition says it stopped 11 missiles and drones in a single day, the Houthis say the airport remains a target — and the question of whether civilian aviation can operate through a missile campaign is now being answered in real time.

Iran / US: post-midterm strike planning reported, no decision confirmed

CNN and the New York Times have reported, over October 21–25, that US officials have discussed a short but intense campaign against Iran after the November 3 midterms — roughly three days of strikes on nuclear facilities, energy infrastructure and missile and drone arsenals, coordinated with Israel — while President Trump has publicly pledged not to attack Iran before the midterms and has not confirmed any final decision. Trump said Saturday the US could take further military action against Iran or reach a deal, without specifying timing. Treat this as reported planning, not a decision: the White House has said it does not comment on military plans "that may or may not exist," and Trump has repeatedly vetoed proposals for a return to major combat this year. Sources: Suara Garut / CNN summary, StateLine Daily / NYT summary, USA Today

Hormuz shipping costs: tracker puts freight plus war-risk surcharges above $40 a barrel

A daily Hormuz tracker refreshed October 25 assesses the threat index as critical and puts combined freight and war-risk insurance surcharges above $40 per barrel on Gulf routes, with operators shifting to ship-to-ship transfers off Oman to bypass the strait. October remains the worst month on record for shipping attacks in the corridor (28 attacks, per the Washington Institute). This is a tracker estimate, not an official figure, and the underlying mine claims for two supertankers remain unverified by any maritime authority. Source: Region Alert

Cybersecurity

  • Keystone wallet breach: customer names and shipping addresses exposed. Hardware wallet maker Keystone said unauthorized access to its website exposed customer contact and shipping information. The company says private keys, recovery phrases and payment details were not compromised — so the risk here is phishing and physical-targeting scams using the leaked contact details, not drained wallets. Keystone urged customers to ignore unexpected messages claiming to be from the company and never enter recovery phrases into unfamiliar devices. Source: Abijita
  • CVE-2026-116068: Dokploy cross-organization authorization bypass, CVSS 8.8. Dokploy through version 0.30.10 lets owners or admins of any organization control other tenants' applications by supplying a foreign application ID. Disclosed October 25; no public exploit confirmed and not in CISA's KEV catalog yet, but multi-tenant Dokploy hosts should patch promptly. Source: TheHackerWire
