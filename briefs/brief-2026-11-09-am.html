Ukraine hits Russia's second-biggest refinery as Moscow blacks out 12 regions — on the eve of Abu Dhabi talks

Ukrainian drones set fire to Russia's second-largest oil refinery overnight, and Russian missiles and drones knocked out power across 12 Ukrainian regions, in a final round of energy strikes the day before US-mediated talks open in Abu Dhabi. Both sides arrived at the negotiating table having just demonstrated they can still hit each other's energy infrastructure at will.

The Ukrainian strike hit the KINEF refinery at Kirishi, in Russia's Leningrad region about 800 kilometres from the Ukrainian border. The regional governor, Alexander Drozdenko, confirmed drones struck the town's industrial zone and a fire broke out at the refinery, which he described as Russia's second-largest, processing around 400,000 barrels a day — about 20 million tonnes a year, more than 6% of Russia's refined output. Independent Russian outlet ASTRA confirmed from local video that the fire was at the refinery itself, and NASA's FIRMS satellite system registered multiple heat hotspots at the site, the Kyiv Independent reported. Drozdenko said air defences downed 113 Ukrainian drones over the region during the attack — his count rose from 81 while the operation was still under way, according to TASS — and a woman was killed in the town of Vsevolozhsk when drone debris struck the roof of her apartment building. An apartment in Kirishi was also damaged and a family evacuated. The fire was contained, the governor said, but the extent of damage to the plant's processing units has not been independently confirmed, and the operator has not commented. The refinery has been hit before — in May, strikes damaged three of its four crude distillation units, and again on August 30.

Russia's reply was a large overnight barrage of its own. President Zelenskyy said it involved more than 30 ballistic missiles and over 170 drones, and Ukraine's Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov said consumers were left without electricity in 12 regions: Vinnytsia, Dnipropetrovsk, Donetsk, Zhytomyr, Zaporizhzhia, Kyiv, Odesa, Poltava, Rivne, Kharkiv, Khmelnytskyi and Chernihiv. Nekrasov said the situation was most difficult in the Dnipropetrovsk and Kyiv regions. Ukraine's energy ministry accused Russia of deliberately targeting the energy and oil and gas sectors 128 times over the past week, with 219 incidents of damage or destruction recorded. Russia's defence ministry said it had struck three electricity substations, and claimed to have hit a cargo ship in the Black Sea it said was carrying weapons to Ukraine — a claim with no independent confirmation. Meanwhile the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant, which lost all external power on Sunday for the 27th time in the war, is back on the grid after about ten hours on diesel generators — but through the same single 330-kV line that keeps failing, with its main 750-kV line still down since October 23, according to the IAEA.

The two sides tell the story of this energy war very differently. Kyiv says it never formally agreed to the energy ceasefire President Trump announced unilaterally in October, and senior Ukrainian officials told the Kyiv Independent it was never a deal — though Zelenskyy has said Ukraine would halt refinery strikes if Russia stopped hitting energy targets. Moscow, for its part, presents its strikes as retaliation and has accused Kyiv of contradicting US peace efforts. Trump has blamed Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries for contributing to higher diesel and gasoline prices in the United States, although he has also acknowledged that his decision to attack Iran alongside Israel played a major role in the increase — and rising fuel costs contributed to Republican losses in last week's midterm elections.

The talks themselves open Tuesday, November 10, in Abu Dhabi, running two days. Reuters reports that Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner will lead the US side, France's foreign minister said Europeans will be represented, and Russia's delegation is reported to be led by presidential aide Vladimir Medinsky. Ukraine's priorities, according to the Associated Press, are halting strikes on energy facilities and critical infrastructure, resuming grain shipments through the Black Sea, and expanding prisoner exchanges. It is the fourth round sponsored by the Trump administration, after the last round in Geneva in February, and previous rounds have made no tangible progress toward ending the war.

For ordinary people, the human cost is measured in cold and dark: hundreds of thousands without power in Dnipropetrovsk and Kharkiv regions alone, emergency and scheduled outages across most of Ukraine, and a refinery fire 800 kilometres inside Russia that shows nowhere in either country's energy system is out of reach. The 48 hours before the talks brought a massive Russian strike on Ukraine's power system and Ukrainian strikes on Kirishi and Rostov in return — the agenda for Abu Dhabi is being written by the attacks themselves.

In short: Ukraine set Russia's second-largest refinery on fire overnight while Russian strikes blacked out 12 Ukrainian regions, and both sides head into Tuesday's Abu Dhabi talks having shown they can still hit each other's energy infrastructure — with Kyiv saying it never agreed to an energy ceasefire in the first place.

Iran talks are frozen, both sides say — but the backchannel is still moving

A day after President Trump told CNN that fresh US strikes on Iran were "highly unlikely" if talks keep progressing, both Washington and Tehran are now saying the talks themselves have stalled — while quietly keeping the backchannel alive through Qatar.

Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met Qatari mediators in New York on Monday to discuss Tehran's proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. He told Iranian state media the Qataris would carry the ideas discussed to the US side and that he hoped to receive Washington's final response by Tuesday, then said he would fly back to Tehran within hours: "Whenever the Qataris have a response, they know how to get it to us." The Iranian plan, announced Friday at the UN General Assembly, calls on the US to release frozen Iranian funds, lift sanctions and end its naval blockade of Iranian ports — steps Araghchi said could be completed within four or five days, allowing the strait to reopen and negotiations on a final agreement, including on Iran's nuclear programme, to begin by the seventh day. Trump rejected the proposal on Saturday, calling it "unacceptable."

Washington's public answer came from Secretary of State Marco Rubio, who said on Sunday he could not say the two sides were "on the verge of a breakthrough" and called Tehran's demands "unacceptable," "outrageous" and "ridiculous." A US official told Al Jazeera the indirect discussions were "positive and constructive" but stressed there would be no agreement unless Iran's nuclear programme is addressed, adding that Trump is willing to relieve sanctions and release frozen funds in exchange for progress on nuclear issues. The official said Iran has indicated flexibility on the nuclear questions, but the sides remain apart on timing and who moves first — and expressed scepticism of Tehran, saying it broke the memorandum of understanding signed in June by firing on commercial vessels. Tehran denies that and says Washington broke its commitments by failing to halt Israel's attacks on Lebanon and by seeking alternate shipping routes through the strait.

The two sides' versions of the stall do not agree on the cause. Araghchi said on Sunday that mediators had made no progress since the "shock" of the US midterm elections and that it would take time for the atmosphere to become conducive to visits resuming. Rubio, speaking before flying to Paris for the G7 foreign ministers' meeting, dismissed suggestions a deal is imminent while acknowledging that multiple third-party nations are mediating. Behind the public statements, Vice President J.D. Vance has been speaking almost daily with mediators, including Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al-Thani and Pakistan's military chief Asim Munir, and Qatari officials are expected to continue discussions in Tehran on Tuesday. Trump is meeting the Qatari prime minister at the White House on Monday, and told reporters Monday: "We're going to win. It's going to go pretty quickly," adding that gas prices would fall once the US won the war. In Tehran, Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei struck a defiant tone, saying the days of foreign domination were over, while Iran's armed forces chief warned of a "decisive and devastating response" to any threat.

For the world economy, the shipping war is the part that bites. Iran closed the strait — which handled roughly 20% of the world's oil and gas before the war — shortly after the US and Israel launched their offensive on February 28, and flows are running well below capacity. Crude exports from major Middle East producers rebounded in September to 12.8 million barrels a day, their highest since the war began, according to Kpler data cited by Reuters — but still far below February's 18.8 million. Brent fell 1.7% to $96.67 a barrel on Monday morning, with WTI at $89.87, after Brent dropped 16% last week, its biggest weekly decline since the start of the Covid pandemic. The US has also imposed a blockade on Iranian ports and threatened secondary sanctions against airports and companies doing business with Iranian airlines; the UAE, Iraq and Oman have already cancelled related flights. Iran's navy claimed on Sunday that a fourth tanker had struck a mine in the strait — a claim Reuters could not confirm, and one that conflicts with the Revolutionary Guards' own count of three.

In short: both sides now say the Iran talks are stalled — Araghchi blames the post-midterm freeze, Rubio calls Tehran's demands ridiculous — but the Qatari backchannel is still running, with a US answer expected by Tuesday, Qatari officials heading to Tehran, and Trump meeting the Qatari prime minister today. Oil fell again on Monday as traders bet the standoff holds.

Krakow gunman dies in hospital before he can be questioned

The 24-year-old suspected of killing six people in Krakow's Main Market Square on Saturday — and, investigators believe, his parents and sister earlier the same day — died in hospital early on Monday, taking whatever motive he had with him. He had never been questioned, and now never will be.

The man, identified by Polish media as Michał O., died at approximately 4.30am on Monday, according to Krzysztof Klęczar, the governor of the Małopolska region. He had undergone at least three operations after being critically wounded in a shootout with police on Saturday, and prosecutors had been unable to question him because of his condition. Eryk Stasielak, a prosecutor at Poland's National Prosecutor's Office, said proceedings directly concerning the deceased suspect would be discontinued, but the wider inquiry — including whether anyone else knew about his plans — will continue. Post-mortem examinations of all nine victims began on Monday and could take about two days.

The dead are now counted as ten including the gunman: six in the Main Market Square — a 51-year-old Austrian woman, a 32-year-old British man, three Polish women aged 47, 53 and 61, and a Polish man of about 50 — and three at the family home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, about 150 kilometres north-east of Krakow: his 53-year-old father, 52-year-old mother and younger sister, all with gunshot wounds. Fifteen other people were wounded, including two police officers — one stable, one serious but stable. The officer who arrested the attacker was woken from an induced coma on Sunday.

What investigators do know is piecemeal. Police recovered 11 firearms from locations connected to the shootings, including seven at the family property — a total matching the number of weapons covered by permits held by the suspect and his father. Michał O. held a valid firearms licence, had no previous criminal convictions and was not previously known to police, and some weapons had been positioned within reach of the driver's seat of his Subaru. Officers found no letter or other document on the suspect or inside his car explaining his actions, though they are analysing his phone, electronic storage devices and handwritten notes. Interior ministry spokeswoman Karolina Gałecka told Polish radio on Monday that specialists were still examining emblems and patches found on the suspect's clothing.

The two sides of the motive question are, for now, one official reading and no answer. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said the suspect held radical ultranationalist views and appeared fascinated by far-right ideologies, and Prime Minister Donald Tusk called the shooting an act of terrorism. The National Prosecutor's Office formally classified the attack as a suspected terrorist offence on Sunday. But the authorities have not established whether the gunman's political beliefs directly motivated the killings, no evidence of membership of an organised extremist group has been publicly disclosed, and Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek said investigators had obtained evidence that could help explain the events but declined to disclose its contents. Le Monde reports that disinformation about the attacker's political affiliations spread from Saturday night into Sunday.

For Krakow, the human picture is mourning, not answers. Poland declared two days of national mourning on November 8–9, running through November 11 in Krakow, and residents and visitors have placed flowers and candles near the square, which reopened to the public on Sunday evening. The investigation now becomes a reconstruction — the full sequence, the source of the weapons, the meaning of the emblems, and whether anyone else took part in planning — carried out without the one person who could have explained it.

In short: the Krakow gunman died in hospital at 4.30am on Monday, never having been questioned, so the investigation into nine murders — six in the square, three at his family home — now has to be rebuilt from forensics, phones and witnesses, with a suspected far-right motive still unproven and no note or letter left behind.

Russell Brand jailed for 10 and a half years for rape and sexual assault

Russell Brand has been sentenced to 10 years and six months in prison, after a jury convicted him last week of raping one woman and sexually assaulting three more. The 51-year-old comedian was sentenced at London's Southwark Crown Court on Monday morning, and showed no emotion as the sentence was read out.

Judge Joel Bennathan handed down nine years for the count of rape and 18 months for the three sexual assault charges — nine, 18 and eight months respectively — running consecutively, for a total of 10 years and six months. Brand must serve two-thirds of the sentence before he is eligible for parole, according to the Guardian — meaning at least seven years behind bars. The judge told him he was a sexual predator who believed that "fame meant you could act how you liked, when you liked, unhindered by the rules by which decent people live," and that he had shown a "casual, arrogant disregard of any woman's right to say no to you." He added: "Whatever women wear, wherever they go, whatever they drink, they retain the right to say no and demand justice in the criminal courts when sexual predators like you, Mr Brand, act without their consent." As Brand left the dock he mouthed "I love you" to his wife, Laura Brand, and told her "we'll be ok" as he was taken away.

The conviction came on Thursday, November 5, after a trial lasting around four weeks that began on October 12. The jury convicted Brand on one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault, and acquitted him on two other rape charges and one count of attempted indecent assault. The offences the jury found him guilty of were committed against four women between 2004 and 2009, at locations including a radio station, the set of a DVD recording and Brand's home; the wider allegations heard at trial spanned 1999 to 2009 and involved six women. Brand pleaded not guilty throughout, testified that he never had non-consensual sex and that he believed "consent is fundamental, essential, necessary, and in many ways, more important than sex itself," and said he had "misused" his fame to have sexual encounters with women. His defence argued the 2023 documentary that brought the allegations to light had sparked a wave of false accusations, and that "the well of justice had been corrupted and contaminated."

The two sides of the case could hardly be further apart — and the jury sided with the prosecution. Prosecutors told the court Brand was a "predator" who used his fame to sleep with women and didn't care if they consented or not. Detective Superintendent Andy Furphy, of the Metropolitan Police, said after the verdict that the outcome was "a testament to the extraordinary courage, resilience and determination of the women who came forward," and added: "Brand used his influence, status and fame to exploit and sexually offend against women. As his profile grew, so too did his sense of entitlement. I hope this conviction sends a clear message that no one is above the law, no matter how famous or powerful they may be." Victim impact statements were read in court before sentencing on Monday. The case began with a September 2023 joint investigation by Channel 4's *Dispatches*, the Sunday Times and the Times; police gathered evidence from more than 100 witnesses, took over 400 witness statements and reviewed more than 1,400 documents.

For the women involved, and for the wider question of what fame protects, the sentence is the end of one case but not of the police work: the Metropolitan Police said its investigation into rape allegations made by two more women remains ongoing, including one involving a woman who was 16 at the time, and files have been submitted to the Crown Prosecution Service. Brand was remanded into custody after the verdict and has been held at HMP Wandsworth.

In short: Russell Brand, 51, was jailed for 10 and a half years on Monday after being convicted of one rape and three sexual assaults against four women between 2004 and 2009 — the judge called him a sexual predator who believed fame put him above the rules, and he must serve at least seven years before parole. Police are still investigating allegations from two more women.

Houthi/Saudi — Monday alert in Riyadh, no new strikes reported

Saudi civil defence raised an alert in Riyadh on Monday morning and declared the danger passed minutes later, a day after Houthi missiles damaged the Aramco Tower in the capital's financial district. BBC Verify has now verified video showing windows blown out across several floors of the tower — more damage than the Energy Ministry's account of "minor damage" from falling debris suggested — though there were no casualties. The Saudi-led coalition said it destroyed ballistic-missile launchers in Saada and Sanaa that it said were used in Sunday's attacks, after intercepting three ballistic missiles and two cruise missiles. Khor Mor gas field drone attack blacks out most of Iraqi Kurdistan

A drone struck a liquefied petroleum gas storage tank at the Khor Mor gas field in Iraq's Kurdistan region late on Sunday night, setting off a fire, halting all production and knocking out 2,500–2,800 megawatts of generation — roughly 70–80% of the region's electricity. Gas supply was cut to power plants serving Erbil, Sulaimaniyah, Bazian, Chamchamal and Duhok, a population of five to six million, with hospitals and water stations given priority and supply projected at five to six hours a day in places. Operator Dana Gas confirmed on Monday that the fire was out but production remains suspended pending a damage assessment, with no restart timetable. Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi said an initial assessment found no casualties and ordered a joint investigation. No group has claimed responsibility — the 12th attack on the field since 2022 — though Kurdish officials have previously blamed Iran-aligned Iraqi armed groups for earlier strikes on the region's energy sites, and Iraqi security sources said the drone approached from the direction of Tuz Khurmatu, outside Kurdistan's control. The field, run by the Pearl Petroleum consortium, supplies more than 80% of Kurdistan's electricity. Developing — attribution and restart date both open. (Business Times via Reuters · Silent Dossier · Political.org)

Israel — Eisenkot reaches out to Netanyahu's allies; Knesset sworn in Tuesday

Gadi Eisenkot has begun his 28-day mandate by reaching out to Benjamin Netanyahu's allies — appealing to Likud and to Religious Zionism to join a national emergency government under him as prime minister — while his own bloc holds only 48 of the 120 Knesset seats and needs 61. Avigdor Liberman is willing to compromise on conscription to build a 61-seat government without Arab parties, and Eisenkot himself is said to prefer a 48-seat minority government with Arab parties supporting from outside, with the ultra-Orthodox possibly abstaining. His four conditions for partners stand: recognition of Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, commitment to the Declaration of Independence, universal national or civil service, and designating Hamas a terrorist organisation. The new Knesset is sworn in on Tuesday, November 10. (Lanka Leader/Reuters · Particle)

Chainalysis breach was far worse than first disclosed — credentials and live tokens for all users exposed

Chainalysis has confirmed that names, usernames, password hashes and live authentication tokens for all of its users were exposed to a malicious actor — a much wider disclosure than its October 30 statement, which said it had found no evidence that credentials or session tokens had been touched. The company added the sentence to its incident notice late on Wednesday, November 4, and told some customers an "on-path attack" had exposed a subset of passwords. The attack used what the company calls "offensive agentic techniques." As of its November 7 update, Reactor, KYT and Address Screening are back online with tighter controls — customers must submit trusted IPs for an allowlist, reset passwords and rotate API keys — while Data Solutions and Chain (Beta) are still offline. No user count has been given, and the company has not answered questions about why the wider exposure was disclosed days after it was discovered. For anyone in crypto compliance, this is the firm police and banks call when crypto goes missing — and its own login and screening tools were the ones hit. (Tech Insider · Netics Labs)

No new CISA KEV additions; "sshole" OpenSSH flaw now has sensor exploitation evidence

CISA has added nothing new to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog since the SonicWall SonicOS flaw (CVE-2026-19868) on November 6 — the catalog stands at 1,769 entries, federal deadline November 20. TheHackerNews' November 9 write-up adds that SonicWall also patched other high-severity SonicOS flaws in the same round, including CVE-2026-107155 (8.8) and CVE-2026-82308 (7.5). Separately, the "sshole" OpenSSH flaw (CVE-2026-123158) now has direct sensor-exploitation evidence from GreyNoise dated November 7, after the first real exploit attempts on November 5 — still no confirmed production victims.
