Morning brief — Friday, November 6, 2026

Trump says he'll decide "soon" whether to resume all-out war on Iran — as Qatar pushes a two-day deal and he phones the Emir

President Donald Trump said Thursday he will soon decide whether to order a new round of major strikes on Iran, after meeting his top national security advisers on the issue for the first time since the midterm elections. "Now we will make a decision," he told reporters in the Oval Office, adding: "I would rather not destroy the rest of the country if possible, but maybe I will have to. You'll know soon enough." The meeting was part of his weekly intelligence briefing, according to two US officials cited by Axios, and came after White House meetings on a possible resumption of military operations on Wednesday and Thursday.

Trump is weighing three options, Axios reports: launching new strikes, continuing to strangle Iran economically through sanctions and the naval blockade, or cutting a deal that includes significant concessions to the Iranians. He framed the choice around the nuclear question — whether to go further with strikes to ensure Iran's nuclear program is dead and "it will never happen again" — while stressing US forces are ready: "If we have to do the rest of the Iran thing, we're very much locked and loaded." US Central Command is finalizing preparations for a possible resumption of major combat operations, a US official said, though it has not received a new directive from Washington. A third carrier strike group, the USS Theodore Roosevelt, is heading to the region but stops in Singapore on Friday and is not expected to reach Central Command's area before mid-November.

Diplomacy is running in parallel, and it moved fast on Thursday. Qatari mediators are discussing a draft two-day confidence-building proposal with both sides: on day one, the US would lift its naval blockade and release some of Iran's frozen funds, and Iran would invite International Atomic Energy Agency inspectors to the bombed nuclear sites; on day two, the Strait of Hormuz would reopen, and nuclear talks would resume, focused on Iran's stockpile of highly enriched uranium. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi held multiple calls Thursday with mediators from Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, the UAE, Oman and Egypt, and a senior Iranian official said Tehran sent a nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatar on Thursday. On Friday morning, Trump called Qatar's Emir Sheikh Tamim bin Hamad Al Thani — a call Trump initiated, according to Qatar's state news agency — to discuss de-escalation efforts; the Emir stressed continued dialogue, no further escalation, and protection of navigation in the strait and energy supplies. CBS News confirmed the call.

The two sides are still far apart, and each is publicly setting conditions the other has rejected. Iran's Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei met Russian President Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin on Thursday, delivered a message from Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, and said afterward that Iran announced seven conditions to the Americans — including lifting sanctions, releasing frozen assets, compensation for war damage and ending the blockade — and that "until the Americans fulfill our seven conditions, neither the Strait of Hormuz will be opened nor will there be any negotiations." Putin said he is ready to try to help end the war. Washington has rejected several of the conditions and insists Iran's nuclear program be permanently dismantled. Vice President JD Vance has emerged as a leading advocate of diplomacy over renewed strikes, and has publicly blamed the war as a primary cause of the Republicans' midterm defeats; Trump has acknowledged higher gasoline prices tied to the conflict contributed to those losses. Iranian military officials have warned any new US attack would draw retaliation against US targets without geographic limits.

Meanwhile the shipping war goes on. Iran reported three more tankers damaged on Thursday, and the UK maritime agency UKMTO says incidents are running close to October's record pace of 35 vessels. One new detail: the Greek-operated, Marshall Islands-flagged tanker Velos Sapphire, struck by two unidentified projectiles about 50 nautical miles north of Qatar late Wednesday, has one seafarer missing — maritime security sources say the crew member was killed, though the International Maritime Organization has not confirmed a fatality. Iran has also reshuffled its blacklist of vessels accused of breaching its transit rules, leaving 239 ships on it, and the Joint Maritime Information Centre recorded four attacks in the strait in a 96-hour window from November 1–3, keeping its threat rating at severe. The IMO counts 129 confirmed maritime incidents in the region since the conflict began, with 24 seafarer deaths.

For ordinary people, the stakes are visible at the pump and in the markets. Brent crude traded around $99.90–$100.30 a barrel on Friday morning, up about 1.5–2% on the fresh tanker attacks, but still heading for a roughly 12% weekly decline after Wednesday's near-10% crash on hopes of a deal. Oil shipments through the strait are running below 60% of capacity, and US diesel reserves are below 100 million barrels for the first time in more than 20 years, with diesel around $6.27 a gallon — about 75% higher than a year ago. Gulf states are watching anxiously: the war is in its ninth month, and whether Trump picks strikes, pressure or a deal will shape fuel prices, shipping and the region's security for months.

Qatar's two-day proposal — blockade lifted and frozen funds released, then Hormuz reopened — is being discussed, Iran has sent its own nuclear proposal, and Trump spoke to the Emir on Friday. But Iran says no talks until its seven conditions are met, and the tanker attacks and the missing seafarer on the Velos Sapphire show the shooting hasn't stopped while the diplomacy plays out.

Ukraine: Russia launches 227 drones overnight as both sides keep hitting each other's energy

Russia launched 227 drones at Ukraine overnight into Friday, Ukraine's Air Force reported — Shaheds, jet-powered Shaheds, Gerbera decoys and Parodiya decoys — and air defences had shot down or neutralized 196 of them by 8 a.m. local time, including 70 jet-powered models, with the attack still ongoing at that point. Drones struck 14 locations and debris fell in eight more. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy put the figure at 210 strike drones, about 140 of them Shaheds, and said: "Russia continues to attack our energy sector, critical infrastructure, and civilian facilities."

The strikes hit the power grid hardest. An energy facility was struck in Bila Tserkva, in the Kyiv region, and Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov said new outages hit seven regions overnight and Friday morning — Vinnytsia, Dnipropetrovsk, Zhytomyr, Zaporizhzhia, Kyiv, Kharkiv and Chernihiv — with the capital region worst affected, where repair crews had to partially shut down the local grid because of heavy damage. In the Kharkiv region, railway infrastructure was attacked and traction substations hit, leaving thousands of families without electricity. A Russian drone attack damaged port infrastructure in Odesa and wounded at least two people, damaging two high-rise residential buildings; in Kherson, one person was killed and eight injured over the past day. Russia's Defence Ministry said it intercepted 142 Ukrainian drones between Thursday night and Friday morning.

Ukraine hit back at Russian energy and military targets the same night. Ukraine's General Staff said its forces struck two ammunition depots in occupied Zaporizhzhia — near Melitopol and Khliborobne — a drone command post near Chasiv Yar and a fuel depot near Luhanske in occupied Donetsk. Monitoring channels reported Ukrainian drones also struck the Afipsky oil refinery in Russia's Krasnodar region overnight, with drone debris falling on an industrial facility in the town of Afipsky and sparking a fire; the exiled outlet Astra identified the target from eyewitness footage of smoke. The refinery processes 9.1 million tons of crude a year — around 3% of Russia's national total — produces gasoline and Euro-5 diesel, and was placed under EU sanctions in April. And on Thursday night Ukraine launched its fourth drone attack in a week on the Black Sea port of Tuapse, starting a fire at the seaport terminal.

Each side says the other's strikes prove its own case. Kyiv argues the nightly barrages on power plants, railways and ports — 78 strikes on energy facilities since around November 2, per DTEK — show Russia is trying to freeze Ukraine into submission as winter approaches, and Zelenskyy is pressing for more sanctions and synchronized pressure on Moscow. Moscow, which has not commented on the overnight barrage in the reporting so far, has framed its campaign as retaliation for Ukrainian refinery strikes, and Putin has vowed retaliation "twice over" for them. Ukraine, for its part, says hitting refineries and depots is meant to choke the oil revenue funding the war.

For ordinary Ukrainians, this is now a nightly routine with a winter edge: rolling blackouts across seven regions, emergency grid shutdowns in the Kyiv area, trains disrupted in Kharkiv, and apartment blocks burning in Odesa. No power plant in the country has gone undamaged, Zelenskyy has said, and the grid is being hit faster than crews can repair it. For Russians, the Afipsky and Tuapse strikes are part of a campaign that has repeatedly reached refineries deep inside Russia, squeezing fuel supply and export earnings.

In short: Russia fired 227 drones overnight, knocking out power in seven Ukrainian regions including Kyiv, while Ukraine struck the Afipsky refinery, hit Tuapse port for the fourth time in a week, and destroyed ammunition and fuel depots in occupied territory. Both sides are now openly trading energy-infrastructure strikes, and the grid — not the front line — is where the war is being felt most by civilians.

Apple: the first touchscreen Macs go on sale today — five new products at once

Apple's first touchscreen Mac finally hits stores today. The 14-inch and 16-inch MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display go on sale Friday, November 6, alongside three other new products: the OLED iPad mini, the M6 iMac and an M6 version of the regular 14-inch MacBook Pro. Apple announced all five across three days last month, wrapping up with its "Pro and Tell" event in Los Angeles on October 28, and preorders that began that day are now arriving.

The touchscreen MacBook Pro is the one stealing the spotlight. After years of Apple executives dismissing the idea, the new MacBook Pro is the first Mac with a touch display, and the first with a tandem OLED panel — which Apple calls PROLED XDR. The 14.2-inch and 16.3-inch screens go up to 2,000 nits of peak brightness for both HDR and SDR content, with a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio. Touch is built into the panel rather than added as a separate layer, and Apple has kept it restrained: it works alongside the keyboard and trackpad rather than turning the Mac into an iPad. The laptops are also slimmer and lighter — the 14-inch drops from about 3.5 to 3.0 pounds, the 16-inch from 4.7 to 4.0 — bring Dynamic Island to the Mac, and mark the return of the glowing Apple logo. The 14-inch starts at $2,899 with M5 Pro ($4,499 with M5 Max); the 16-inch starts at $3,399. The first reviews, published Thursday, praise the display — Notebookcheck measured about 2,085 nits of peak HDR brightness on its 14-inch review unit — but the price is the argument.

The other three products fill out the range. The 14-inch MacBook Pro with the new M6 chip starts at $1,999 with 16GB of RAM and 1TB of storage, delivers up to 30% faster graphics than its predecessor, drives two external 4K displays at up to 165Hz, and adds Apple's in-house N1 wireless chip for Wi-Fi 7, Bluetooth 6 and Thread — but it has no touchscreen and no OLED panel. The OLED iPad mini gets its biggest redesign in years: a larger 8.5-inch OLED panel with an optional nano-texture finish, upgraded speakers and the A20 Pro chip in a 5.4mm-thin body with IP67 water and dust resistance, for $699 — $150 more than the previous model. The 24-inch M6 iMac skips M5 entirely, jumping from M4 to M6 with up to a 12-core CPU and GPU, Wi-Fi 7, optional 2.5 Gigabit Ethernet and support for two external 6K displays, starting at $1,499 with no price rise.

Reviewers are split in a useful way. The case for the touchscreen models is the display itself — tandem OLED, 2,000 nits, touch built in, a lighter machine — and the case against is the $400 premium over the equivalent non-touch M5 Pro model, which buys a better screen and touch but not a newer chip: the OLED models still run M5 Pro and M5 Max, while the cheaper M6 MacBook Pro has the newer processor. The consensus emerging from the first reviews is that the M6 14-inch at $1,999 is the safer buy for most people, and the PROLED models are for video, AI and 3D work where the display earns its keep. Apple is rolling the new machines out in 30 countries and regions, with deliveries and retail availability from today.

For buyers, the practical picture is simple: if you want touch on a Mac for the first time ever, it's $2,899 and up starting today; if you want the newest chip at the lowest price, it's the M6 14-inch at $1,999; and the iPad mini and iMac both got their biggest upgrades in years, with the mini up $150 and the iMac flat at $1,499.

In short: Apple's five-product launch day is today — the first touchscreen MacBook Pro from $2,899, the M6 MacBook Pro from $1,999, the OLED iPad mini at $699 and the M6 iMac at $1,499. The touchscreen models win on display and lose on value: the $400 premium buys OLED and touch, not a newer chip, so the M6 model is the pick for most buyers.

Houthi / Saudi: drone aimed at Mecca region intercepted Friday morning

The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed a Houthi drone heading for Saudi Arabia's Mecca region on Friday morning, coalition spokesman Turki al-Malki said in a social media post, and a missile aimed at western Saudi Arabia was brought down earlier. The coalition also intercepted two ballistic missiles fired toward Najran on Friday, with debris falling and damage being assessed. On Thursday it destroyed a ballistic missile aimed at the eastern region and two aimed at Riyadh. The Houthis denied targeting Mecca or the surrounding region, saying their operations target Saudi oil facilities and military bases only — the coalition has previously accused them of launching drones toward Mecca and Medina. The Houthis also claimed a drone attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam on Thursday evening, saying it achieved its objective; the coalition has not confirmed that claim. Inside Yemen, fighting persists around Taiz, where the governor said the city is under heavy shelling with residential neighbourhoods hit, medical supplies, water and fuel cut off, and a notable escalation around Jabal Saber and Jabal Habashi. Israel: Herzog still hasn't picked — decision Sunday, Eisenkot expected to get the nod

President Isaac Herzog still has not assigned the mandate to form Israel's next government, and will announce his choice on Sunday, November 8. After consultations ended Thursday with 67 lawmakers recommending Yashar! leader Gadi Eisenkot and 46 backing Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Herzog said the tally did not settle who could actually assemble a viable coalition, given mutual vetoes between and within blocs, and that he would hold further personal talks with party leaders first. Reuters reports Eisenkot is expected to receive the mandate, but it is unclear whether he can build a majority: his 67 recommendations include the Arab-led Ra'am (8) and Joint List (11), whose backing is not the same as joining a coalition, and he has ruled out governing with either Arab party. Netanyahu's bloc holds 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset; the new Knesset is sworn in Tuesday, November 10. Sources · Interbellum News · Demócrata

Poland: nearly half of Poles say Tusk should resign after the tribunal takeover

The first poll since Prime Minister Donald Tusk's takeover of the Constitutional Tribunal finds 47.4% of Poles say he should no longer serve as prime minister, against 38.3% who want him to stay and 14.3% undecided, in a Wprost survey reported Friday. The takeover — judges elected by the current parliamentary majority suspending the court's president and vice president, lowering the quorum and assuming control, with riot police seizing the building — has split the country: a separate United Surveys poll published Tuesday found the largest group, 38.6%, believes neither side is right, with Nawrocki backed by 28.7% and Tusk's government by 26.6%. Meanwhile the government is pressing ahead: the head of the Prime Minister's Chancellery, Jan Grabiec, confirmed the administration will soon table a broad slate of legislative measures to the court, seeking to fast-track matters officials say have been stalled for years. President Karol Nawrocki has called the takeover a "crime against the Constitution"; the two leaders still have no meeting date, and European Parliament President Roberta Metsola is due in Warsaw in the coming days. Cyber: WordPress deadline is today — and the attacks are already at 240,000+

WordPress CVE-2026-119977 — federal deadline TODAY, November 6. The race-condition flaw in WordPress Core (versions 4.7.0–7.1.3, CVSS 8.1) lets unauthenticated attackers run code during a core upgrade, and CISA added it to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 3 with a November 6 deadline for federal agencies. Wordfence published fresh attack data on November 5: its firewall has now blocked more than 240,000 exploit attempts, peaking on October 30 with over 74,000 attempts in a day. The fix is WordPress 7.1.4 (with backported fixes for older branches) — update now if you run a site. Sources · 0day News

Binance Recovery Update #9: more withdrawals reopened, and the proof-of-reserves is now live. Binance reopened withdrawals for another 58 token-network pairs from 04:00 UTC on Friday, November 6 — the fourth batch since the October 29 theft — bringing the published total to 259 pairs across 151 tokens. Native Cardano, Litecoin, Polkadot, Stellar and NEAR withdrawals are still only available as BNB Smart Chain tokens, not on their native networks. And the 44-token zero-knowledge proof-of-reserves snapshot from November 4 is now live, PANews reported Friday morning — users can verify for themselves that balances are backed at least 1:1, so the 98%-replenished figure is no longer just Binance's word. The remaining ~2% of the ~$6.3B stolen is still being bought back through market and OTC channels. Sources · PANews · DailyCoin

No new CISA KEV additions reported overnight. The catalog stood at 1,768 entries as of November 5; nothing new surfaced in this window.

Ransomware claims, all unverified tracker reports: the N0n group published a large data archive from mobile ad network Chartboost (publisher account balances for 185,545 accounts, 1.1 billion rows of advertiser/publisher performance data, 293GB of marketplace transactions, 35 million device events) and claimed Saudi retailer Centrepoint (3,094,863 customer loyalty records with names, emails, phones and birth dates), Italian pharma company Dompé Farmaceutici's US ophthalmology claims warehouse, Scry Analytics, Hallmark Cards (221,978 customer chat sessions) and Cogent Biosciences. Akira listed Hudson & Hudson SignProducts; Qilin listed Rotstein & Shiffman. None of these claims has been independently confirmed. Sources · QPulse · HookPhish
