World escalation

The truce that held — and the refusal that ended it

The war's longest local ceasefire expired Sunday night, and the reason it will not continue is now on the record: a senior Ukrainian official told AFP that Kyiv's Foreign Ministry offered to extend the six-day Oleshky truce and Russia refused. The pause around the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, in effect from October 27 to November 1, ended with no reported violations — but with no successor arrangement, and with Kherson residents bracing for fighting to resume.

The details, as reported by AFP and carried by The Leaders Online and France 24, show a final day that mixed relief with dread. Ukraine's military evacuated 167 more people Sunday from threatened areas of southern Kherson region before the deadline, and human rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets confirmed afterward that "the ceasefire, which was in effect from October 27 to November 1, has ended," calling it "an important step in our joint humanitarian efforts." Kherson governor Oleksandr Prokudin urged residents to stay out of the Dnipro "red zones" and to respond immediately to attack warnings. One Kherson resident told AFP the truce had been a "breath of fresh air"; a Ukrainian officer, speaking anonymously, warned the fighting could be "worse than before," saying Russian forces had used the calm to amass troops, move ammunition and reconnoitre Ukrainian positions with surveillance drones. Minutes after the truce ended, Ukraine's air force said its Air Alarm app had probably been subjected to hostile interference after false radiation-threat alerts appeared — spokesman Yuriy Ignat confirmed to AFP that the app had been hacked, and Ukraine's strategic-communications centre blamed Russia, though that attribution is Kyiv's claim, not independently verified.

The two sides are now talking past each other about what comes next. Kyiv's position, as Zelenskyy put it in a Fox News interview Sunday, is transactional: Ukraine would stop hitting Russian oil refineries — as President Donald Trump has demanded — if Washington supplied powerful Patriot interceptor missiles, or if Russia stopped hitting Ukraine's energy system. Moscow, for its part, has praised only the Oleshky operation itself — Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Russia had a positive view of how it was conducted — while Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov insisted initial peace talks should be trilateral, involving only Moscow, Kyiv and Washington, excluding European nations. AFP reports US-mediated talks between Russia and Ukraine are set to resume this month, having been effectively frozen since the United States and Israel launched joint strikes on Iran in February. Pope Leo, who had pressed for the Oleshky truce, said he hoped the cooperation would "strengthen the desire for dialogue and lead to new concrete peace initiatives."

For ordinary people, the end of the truce is the return of danger to a place that had six quiet days — and, beyond Kherson, the continuation of the energy war that left hundreds of thousands of Ukrainian homes without power Sunday after Russian strikes on infrastructure in four regions, with Kyiv under emergency outages for much of the day. Zelenskyy has said Russia is trying to take maximum advantage of Ukraine's shortage of ballistic-missile interceptors, which is why the Patriot offer matters: it ties the refinery-strike question directly to the air-defence gap ordinary Ukrainians feel every night.

In short: the Oleshky truce ended Sunday having held without violations, but Russia refused Kyiv's offer to extend it, leaving the mechanism Kyiv wants to keep alive with no second use agreed — while Zelenskyy offered a refinery-strike halt in exchange for Patriot missiles, trilateral talks are due to resume this month, and Kherson residents are back under attack warnings.

Doha talks grind on — while the waterway they are about keeps getting hit

Indirect talks in Doha between Iranian negotiators and Qatari mediators ran through a third day Sunday with still no agreement on reopening the Strait of Hormuz — and the gap between diplomacy and events on the water widened again, with a UAE-owned tug reported partially sinking after a projectile strike and another tanker hit overnight in the northern Gulf.

The details, as reported by Reuters, AP and the maritime press, show a negotiation that has narrowed to one question: what Washington does next. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said Sunday that Tehran was prepared for both defence and diplomacy, that consultations with mediators were continuing, and that the outcome depended on Washington's responses to Iran's questions — including the release of more than $6 billion in frozen Iranian assets and the lifting of sanctions on Iranian oil exports. Araghchi reiterated that there are no direct US–Iran talks at this stage and no final outcome has been reached, while calling a letter from US Vice President JD Vance a "positive step." US Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Saturday that President Donald Trump had not made a final decision on further strikes but preferred a diplomatic solution, and that talks with Tehran had "actually never stopped." The Guardian reports Gulf officials are "braced for the worst" ahead of Tuesday's US midterm elections, after which the US moratorium on strikes against Iran is set to expire.

Meanwhile, the strikes did not pause for the talks. UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) reported that an Emirati-owned offshore tug, identified by security consultancy Vanguard Tech as the Selat Storm, was hit by a projectile just after 1500 UTC Sunday northwest of the Musandam Peninsula, with a passing vessel relaying its mayday call and the tug reported "partially sinking"; the crew's status was not reported. Overnight Sunday, UKMTO reported a tanker hit by two projectiles about 70 nautical miles east of Ras Al Khafji, Saudi Arabia, with all crew accounted for — at least the fifth energy vessel hit by suspected Iranian strikes since Thursday, according to the Maritime Executive. Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Navy claimed two oil tankers, including a supertanker, exploded after hitting mines on an unauthorised route — a claim carried by Iranian state media and several outlets but not independently verified, and it is unclear whether it describes the same incidents UKMTO reported. The Institute for the Study of War assesses that Iran likely struck a vessel in the strait on November 1 as part of its effort to disrupt civilian shipping and pressure Washington, and notes Iran remains the only threat to civilian shipping there.

For ordinary people, the stakes are the same ones that have driven this whole standoff: energy prices and winter supply. The Straits daily brief put Brent at $113.43 on Sunday, with shipping through the strait running far below pre-war levels, and every new strike — even one on a small tug, even one with no confirmed casualties — feeds the war-risk premiums and freight costs that eventually reach fuel pumps and heating bills. The Al Mayeda case, confirmed by owner Nakilat on Sunday and covered in the evening brief, is the sharpest version of that worry: a Qatari gas carrier hit reportedly hours after Tehran had cleared its passage, which is why shippers now doubt whether any clearance, or any deal, actually makes the waterway safe.

In short: Doha's third day ended with Araghchi saying Iran is ready for diplomacy or defence and Rubio saying Trump has made no final decision — while a tug was left partially sinking, another tanker was hit overnight, and an unverified Iranian mine claim muddied the picture further, all days before the US midterm deadline that Gulf states are bracing for.
