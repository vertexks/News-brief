Morning brief — Thursday, November 19, 2026

Trump, Xi and Putin hold first-ever three-way summit — and leave with a declaration, not a deal

The leaders of the United States, China and Russia sat down together for the first time ever on Thursday in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the APEC summit. The meeting lasted about 90 minutes, started nearly two hours late, and ended with a four-point joint statement the three leaders called a "peace declaration" — but no agreement on Ukraine, Iran, or nuclear weapons.

The declaration, published by China's Xinhua news agency and the Kremlin, commits the three countries to four things: reforming the United Nations so it can "respond more effectively to global challenges"; increasing the production and supply of refined petroleum products; limiting the International Criminal Court's reach, saying the court and "other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction"; and holding a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing proliferation, reaffirming that "a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought." Strikingly, the document does not mention Ukraine or Iran at all, sets no timelines, and creates no new treaty — the last US-Russia arms pact, New START, expired in February and remains lapsed.

The one concrete result of the day came before the three-way session, in Trump's bilateral meeting with Xi: the US and China agreed to extend their trade truce, according to the Globe and Mail, CNBC and Bloomberg. Neither side said how long the extension runs or on what terms, and China's own readout mentioned no extension details. The truce agreed in Busan in October 2025 had been due to run until January 10, 2027, after tariffs between the two countries peaked at 145 percent on the American side and 125 percent on the Chinese side. Trump left Shenzhen for Washington soon after the talks ended, without speaking to reporters.

The two sides of the room read the day very differently. Supporters of the format called it historic in itself: Henry Huiyao Wang, a former senior adviser to China's government, told USA Today that "the summit is more than symbolic" and that "face-to-face dialogue of this kind can help calm down a lot of the world's conflicts." Skeptics said the substance was thin and the direction troubling: Jonathan Eyal of the Royal United Services Institute said "just about the only substantial outcome of the Shenzhen summit was the rejection of international law and institutions such as the International Court of Justice," and called it "a summit supposedly designed to deal with global questions, but one which ended up with an outright rejection of globalization." Ukraine's President Zelenskyy had warned in October that he did not want the meeting to turn into "'Yalta 2, 3, 4,' with a redistribution of influence" — a reference to the 1945 conference that carved up post-war Europe — and Putin, for his part, said there was nothing "wrong with Yalta." Xi described nuclear weapons as "the sword of Damocles hanging over humanity," according to Chinese state media.

For ordinary people, the immediate effects are limited but real. The pledge to boost refined-oil supplies, if it amounts to anything, could eventually ease fuel prices — but there is no mechanism or deadline attached, so nothing changes at the pump today. The trade-truce extension matters more directly: without it, US-China tariffs could have snapped back toward the triple-digit levels of last year, which would have raised prices on electronics, clothing and countless other goods. And for Ukrainians, the summit's silence on their war is the story: the three most powerful leaders in the world met, talked about Ukraine behind closed doors, and said nothing about it in public.

In short: a historic photo-op with a real document attached, but the document is promises, not deals — no Ukraine plan, no Iran plan, no nuclear treaty, and a trade-truce extension with no published terms. The test now is whether the promised nuclear-risk dialogue ever gets a date.

*Sources: USA Today, The Times, WorldPulseWire*

Kerch Bridge hit for the third time this week as Russia pounds Kyiv in daylight

Ukraine struck Russia's Kerch Bridge — the only direct link between Russia and occupied Crimea — for the third time this week early on Thursday, halting rail traffic just as Vladimir Putin was about to sit down with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping in Shenzhen. Hours later, Russian drones were hitting Kyiv in broad daylight, including a drone that crashed near the Dutch Embassy and one that struck a three-storey building.

The bridge attack came days after a section of the road bridge collapsed following strikes on Monday and Tuesday. CNN reports that rail traffic was halted on Thursday morning and resumed in the afternoon, according to Russia's transport ministry, which said there were no casualties. Russian Deputy Prime Minister Marat Khusnullin told state media that individual sections of the bridge could reopen "within one or two months," and the Kremlin said it would be rebuilt "better than before." President Zelenskyy has described the bridge strikes — carried out, he says, exclusively with Ukraine-made weapons — as the seventh stage of an "unprecedented operation in response to Russian strikes on our infrastructure." Speaking to reporters in Shenzhen after the trilateral meeting, Putin threatened Ukraine with retaliation and claimed, despite ample evidence to the contrary, that the bridge serves "purely peaceful purposes."

Meanwhile Kyiv came under a renewed drone assault on Thursday afternoon, the eighth air-raid alert since overnight. The Kyiv Independent reports a Russian drone crashed near the Dutch Embassy, damaging cars and a security post and shattering windows, and a drone struck a three-storey building in daylight as Russia extends its barrages from overnight into daytime hours, often using newer jet-powered Shahed-type drones that leave residents little warning. In the Kyiv region, a woman was killed when a house in the Bucha district was partially destroyed, and four people were injured in the Vyshhorod district, including a child; 29 homes were damaged there. Overnight, Russia also hit two power substations in Odesa, a fuel storage facility in Chornomorsk, a cargo ship at the port of Odesa, and a substation in Sumy, according to Russia's defence ministry.

The two sides are fighting over the same logic from opposite ends. Kyiv says it is hitting the bridge and Russian refineries because Russia keeps hitting Ukrainian power plants and cities — the bridge is a military supply artery to Crimea, and Ukraine says it will keep striking Russian infrastructure until Russia stops striking Ukrainian infrastructure. Moscow calls the bridge civilian and threatens retaliation, while its own nightly campaign against Ukraine's energy grid continues; the UK government, meanwhile, announced on Wednesday it will gift Ukraine more than 1,000 long-range strike drones, some able to fly over 1,000 km carrying heavy warheads. The Coalition of the Willing — Ukraine's key allies — also met in Berlin on Thursday, with US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner attending.

For people on the ground, the bridge strikes and the Kyiv attacks are two halves of the same winter war. In Crimea, Russian-installed authorities already imposed a state of emergency over the summer because of power outages and fuel shortages, and losing the bridge's rail link — even temporarily — squeezes the peninsula's supplies further. In Kyiv, the power crisis keeps deepening: many homes are getting only around four hours of electricity a day, and the daytime drone attacks mean there is no longer a safe part of the day.

In short: Ukraine took out a chunk of Russia's most symbolic supply line for the third time in a week, Russia answered by hitting Kyiv in daylight, and both sides are now trading infrastructure strikes with winter closing in. Watch whether the bridge's rail link holds and whether the Coalition of the Willing meeting produces new air-defence pledges.

Iran hits Kuwait and Bahrain again, another tanker struck — and a US carrier leaves the Gulf

Iran widened its attacks across the Gulf on Wednesday, firing drones at a US airbase in Kuwait and launching fresh strikes on Bahrain, a day after American jets hit an Iranian missile site on Qeshm Island. At the same time, another tanker was hit in the Strait of Hormuz, and the US Navy pulled one of its three aircraft carriers out of the region.

Iran's army said its drones targeted Patriot missile systems and launch platforms at Ali Al-Salem Air Base in Kuwait, according to state broadcaster IRIB, and both Kuwait and Bahrain said they intercepted the attacks. Neither country reported damage or casualties. It was Iran's first attack on Kuwait since September, and Bahrain has now faced four Iranian attacks in six days. The strikes came a day after the US hit an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island, which a US official described as a "self-defence" strike. Iran says its attacks are retaliation for that strike; the US has not announced further strikes.

On the water, the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre said an unknown projectile struck a tanker's engine room about six nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, at 16:45 UTC on Wednesday, and that all crew were safe and accounted for as of early Thursday. That follows Tuesday evening's hit on the Navig8 Messi, an ADNOC Logistics tanker struck 13 nautical miles off Oman, leaving one crew member missing and 11 injured, and the Panama-flagged LPG tanker Tara Gas, struck twice in two days with one Indian seafarer missing. Iran's army claims three tankers hit naval mines and caught fire — a claim Reuters and other outlets could not confirm — while UKMTO describes the strikes as unknown projectiles. The mechanism is unresolved, but the pattern is not: about 18 confirmed attacks on commercial vessels in and around the strait this month alone.

The two sides' accounts diverge sharply. Tehran says it is responding to American aggression and warns ships to coordinate passage with Iranian authorities, effectively claiming control of the strait; Washington says it is escorting ships through in defiance of that claim and calls the Qeshm strike self-defence. Gulf states are caught in the middle: Kuwait's foreign ministry called the attacks a "flagrant violation of sovereignty," the GCC chief held Iran responsible, and the Arab League asked the UN Security Council to act. Meanwhile a Navy official told CBS News the USS George Washington has left the Middle East for the 7th Fleet, leaving the USS George H.W. Bush and USS Theodore Roosevelt in the region.

The human and economic impact is spreading. The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook called the Hormuz crisis the "largest threat" to energy security in history — the strait carried about a quarter of global oil flows before the war. Oil prices actually fell on Wednesday — Brent to $91.95 and WTI to $83.26, its lowest since August 26 — after a big US crude stockpile build, but they remain hostage to the next incident. Gulf countries are facing mounting pressure on oil exports, and flight cancellations in Saudi Arabia continue as the Houthis keep firing: the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a Houthi cruise missile headed for southern Saudi Arabia, while Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed cruise missiles hit King Khalid International Airport and a Riyadh financial centre.

In short: the tit-for-tat is now a daily cycle — US strike, Iranian retaliation on Gulf allies, another tanker hit — and with one American carrier gone, the question is whether the remaining two are enough to keep the strait open, or whether Iran's mine and drone campaign will keep choking the world's most important oil chokepoint.

*Sources: NY Post, Dubai Standard, Mappr*

Anthropic shares debut on Nasdaq today in the largest IPO ever

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share, above its $70–78 range, and starts trading today on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. The offering was upsized to 980 million shares, raising about $81.3 billion — the largest IPO in history, eclipsing SpaceX. The company is valued at roughly $1.5 trillion fully diluted, with Amazon set to hold about 14.7% and Google 13.7% after the offering. The filing shows a revenue run rate of about $81 billion as of October 31, but also roughly $900 billion in multi-year compute and infrastructure commitments. First trade comes after the 9:30 a.m. ET open, once Nasdaq's opening auction clears.

Nvidia rebounds after five down days as Washington probes pile up

Nvidia shares were up about 3.7% in early trading Thursday, to around $156.50, after closing Wednesday at $150.96 — down 2.89% and about 38% below the October record. The rebound comes as scrutiny intensifies: the SEC is examining whether Nvidia misled investors in its disclosures, according to a Reuters report citing the Wall Street Journal, and House Energy and Commerce Committee chair Brett Guthrie has demanded interim CEO Colette Kress brief the panel by December 2 on export controls and safeguards. Separately, Semafor reports a faction of Nvidia's board is still pushing to bring back ousted CEO Jensen Huang, despite chairman Harvey Jones saying reinstatement is not under consideration — and Nvidia said on its earnings call it will not immediately file its 10-Q quarterly report, breaking with usual practice. Huang is due before three House committees on December 1.

GTA 6 is out today — and it's a 99 on Metacritic

Grand Theft Auto VI launched today, November 19, on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S, with a 99 Metascore — tying The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time as the highest-rated game in Metacritic history. The PS5 version sits at 99 from 150 critic reviews, 100% positive; OpenCritic shows a 99 top-critic average with 99% recommending. The game costs $79.99 standard / $99.99 Ultimate Edition, and Sensor Tower estimates over 17 million copies sold already for more than $1.65 billion. Take-Two shares rose 6.5% on Wednesday. The launch hasn't been flawless: players are reporting glitches, crashes and a softlock in an early mission, and there's still no PC version announced. The game also leads The Game Awards 2026 nominations with eight, including Game of the Year.

Google reverses course and will let experienced Android users sideload apps

Google is revising its Android sideloading policy to let "experienced users" bypass verification restrictions and accept the risks of installing unverified apps. The change follows developer pushback against limits on user choice and developer freedom. Google says it is building a new advanced flow for the opt-in. No date was given for when it rolls out.

Java4Shell probing has begun — 187 trigger-shaped requests, no intrusions yet

Attackers have started probing for Java4Shell (CVE-2026-114938), the OpenJDK Base64 memory-corruption flaw, but no successful intrusion has been reported. GreyNoise counted 187 trigger-shaped requests from five IP addresses since November 17, aimed at paths like /login.action and /actuator/env, with no successful intrusions as of Thursday morning. Cloudflare said one IP probed a target with a "java4shell" user agent, then sent the exploit trigger about 17 hours later. Cisco, Elastic, Red Hat and Siemens have listed affected products; Oracle's fixed builds shipped November 17. The flaw is still not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Separately, Rapid7 has published proof-of-concept code for NGINX2Root (CVE-2026-123007), the decade-old HTTP/2 flaw fixed in NGINX 1.31.9 — and Synology says patches for affected DiskStation Manager versions are due November 20.

*Sources: CyberSixt, CyberSixt briefing*

Netanyahu trial: state must say by noon today whether it keeps its High Court petition

The prosecution must tell Israel's High Court by noon on Thursday whether it is maintaining its petition to cross-examine former attorney-general Avichai Mandelblit, after the defence dropped its objection on Wednesday. The District Court has already ruled prosecutors can cross-examine Mandelblit and other defence-called witnesses going forward. If the state drops the petition, Sunday's High Court hearing is likely cancelled. Attorney-General Gali Baharav-Miara is due before the trial panel on November 25.

*Source: JBizNews*

Saudi Arabia replaced its Yemen coalition commander as Houthis advance on Aden (catch-up)

King Salman replaced the commander of the Saudi-led coalition in Yemen on Monday, appointing Chief of Staff General Fayyad al-Ruwaili and reassigning Lieutenant General Fahd bin Hamad al-Salman as an adviser to the defence minister. The change came as Yemeni government forces withdrew from areas near the Bab el-Mandeb strait to concentrate on defending Aden, with Houthi forces reported about 100 km west of the city at Khor Omairh and around 25 km from government lines at Qawah. Fighting continued on Wednesday around Tur al-Bahah in Lahij and in Taiz, where authorities accuse the Houthis of blocking medicine shipments. No fighting inside Aden itself has been reported.

*Sources: Al Jazeera, The Library Estate*
