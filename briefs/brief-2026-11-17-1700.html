World escalation

Allies split over Ukraine's deep strikes as Germany funds more Patriots

The 37th Ramstein meeting in Brussels on Tuesday delivered new air-defence money for Ukraine — and the sharpest public disagreement yet between Washington and its European allies over how Ukraine fights the war. Germany and Norway will jointly finance another PURL package of US weapons for Ukraine, with Germany putting in $300 million, its fifth package, focused on PAC-2 and PAC-3 Patriot missiles. But the meeting's dominant argument was over Ukraine's long-range strikes on Russian territory, with Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby calling them ineffective and Germany's Boris Pistorius and the UK's Wes Streeting publicly pushing back.

The details, as reported by Reuters, Devdiscourse and Ukrainian outlets: Pistorius announced the package ahead of the meeting, saying the focus is air defence, "especially PAC-2 and PAC-3 Patriot missiles," plus IRIS-T systems, radars, F-16 ammunition and drones. He also said Germany is working to integrate its IRIS-T missile with Norwegian NASAMS systems. "Supporting Ukraine is not a charity event… it's about the security of Europe, and it's about stopping Vladimir Putin and putting him in his place," Pistorius said, and he appealed to allies such as Greece that still hold large Patriot stocks. President Volodymyr Zelensky used the meeting to propose a swap: partners would hand over available Patriot and IRIS-T interceptors from their own stocks now, in exchange for newer missiles from Ukraine's existing orders, to close the gap while deliveries lag. Sweden's Defence Minister Pol Johnson said Ukraine needs another 18–24 months to build its own anti-ballistic capability and is preparing to test its FREYJA system this winter. Ukrainian outlets also reported a letter on Gripen fighter jets signed by Ukraine, Sweden and the UK, though that has not been confirmed by the governments involved. Allies also discussed Washington's review of US troop levels in Europe with Colby; Pistorius said to expect changes but nothing concrete yet, with the review's result due before Christmas. Germany hosts about 35,000 US troops, the largest American presence in Europe, and European military sources have speculated Washington could move a brigade — roughly 5,000 troops — from Germany either to Asia or closer to NATO's eastern flank in Poland.

The two sides are arguing about what actually wins this war. Colby, opening the session, praised Ukraine's battlefield gains — he said Ukraine has for a second consecutive month liberated more territory than Russia has captured, and that Russian forces have suffered their heaviest monthly losses of the full-scale war — but judged Ukrainian long-range strikes on Russian civilian infrastructure, including Wildberries warehouses, ineffective for ending the war or slowing the Russian advance, and said US support should focus on holding the front line. He stressed his warnings do not mean Ukraine should fight with its hands tied. Pistorius and Streeting rejected that criticism, arguing that strikes on strategic targets have weakened Russia's war machine. Zelensky's position is that air defence is now Ukraine's chief priority: Patriot stocks are depleted, deliveries are slow, and Russia's ballistic missiles are the one weapon Ukraine cannot reliably stop any other way — which is why he is offering to trade future orders for interceptors sitting in allied depots today.

For ordinary people, the argument lands in the middle of the hardest winter of the war. Russia has hit Ukrainian energy and transport infrastructure daily for weeks, and the overnight strikes on Kyiv's two main bridges (covered in this morning's brief) left parts of the capital without power, water and heating. Kyiv partly reopened both bridges on Tuesday — trams and trolleybuses are back on regular routes, and the Southern Bridge is open to limited one-way traffic — but the Northern Bridge is still closed and the metro's Green Line is still split between the riverbanks. Sweden's warning that Ukraine is 18–24 months from its own anti-ballistic capability is a measure of how long Kyiv will depend on allies for exactly the missiles Colby's critics are arguing about.

In short: Ramstein produced $300 million more for Patriot missiles from Germany and Norway, but exposed a real split — Washington questioning Ukraine's deep strikes on Russian civilian targets, Berlin and London defending them, and Zelensky offering to swap future missile orders for interceptors now. Watch whether any ally actually hands over stockpiled Patriots, and what Colby's troop review says before Christmas.

Fourth tanker burns in Hormuz as Muscat talks end with no deal — and fresh strikes are reported on Qeshm Island

The Gulf war kept escalating on Tuesday even as the diplomats met. A fourth tanker caught fire in or near the Strait of Hormuz in less than 24 hours, Iran said it struck a US radar in Bahrain for the third time in under a week, and Iranian media reported US strikes on Qeshm Island — while talks between Iran's foreign minister and his Omani counterpart in Muscat ended with no agreement and no announcement about negotiations with Washington.

The details, as reported by Terra World News, Traders Union, the Financial Times and others: Iran's navy said a tanker caught fire after hitting a mine on Tuesday, the fourth such incident since Sunday, saying the vessel had been sailing through a restricted zone without coordination and with its navigation systems off. It did not identify the ship, its flag or the crew's condition, and the claim has not been independently verified. Separately, UK Maritime Trade Operations said the UAE-owned LPG tanker Tara Gas was struck twice off the Omani coast on Monday night, the second strike causing a fire on board, with heat-map data showing an explosion near the vessel at about 7 a.m. UK time on Tuesday. UKMTO says at least nine vessels have been attacked in and around the strait in the past 10 days, most near the Omani coast. Iran also said it launched a drone strike against a US air-defence radar system in Bahrain early on Tuesday; Bahraini authorities say they intercepted several Iranian drones targeting the kingdom, home to the US Navy's Fifth Fleet. The Revolutionary Guard said the attack was a response to what it called a violation of Iranian airspace by US drones. Then, on Tuesday afternoon, Iran's semi-official Mehr news agency reported US strikes on several locations on Qeshm Island in the strait, and Israel's Channel 12, citing unofficial sources linked to Iran, reported possible US strikes on Qeshm in response to the Bahrain attack. The IRGC separately claimed it shot down an MQ-1 drone south of Qeshm. None of the Qeshm claims has been confirmed by Washington.

On diplomacy, the gap is widening rather than closing. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi met Oman's Badr Albusaidi in Muscat on Tuesday; Oman's Foreign Ministry said they discussed protecting "maritime corridors" and reviving dialogue, but its statement named neither the Strait of Hormuz nor the United States, and announced no agreement, no visit and no next meeting. A Qatari delegation also met Iranian negotiators on Tuesday in an undisclosed location, according to Traders Union. Pakistan's foreign minister said on Monday that Washington has still not replied to Iran's proposal on the strait, and Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, wrote on Tuesday that Iran will now treat any dam, port or power plant near a US or Israeli base as "dual-use" — civilian and military at once. Tehran has ruled out curbs on uranium enrichment and IAEA access to damaged nuclear sites, while Trump has said further strikes on Iran are "quite unlikely" and the military part of the war is "effectively over," and Vice President JD Vance has said it will not become a "forever war." Trump has said Iran will come up when he meets Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on Thursday.

In Yemen, the Houthis are now about 60 km from Aden, holding positions at the Al-Kharaz camp in Al-Mudharaba after government forces withdrew from Bab el-Mandeb, according to sources cited by AFP. Yemeni military sources told Reuters on Monday that Houthi fighters had reached Khor Omairh, about 100 km west of Aden, and were 25 km from government lines at Qawah. The toll from a Houthi missile attack on a checkpoint in Lahij on Sunday rose to eight dead and 12 wounded. Saudi Arabia replaced its coalition commander on Monday, moving Lieutenant General Fahd bin Hamad Al-Salman to an advisory role and giving Chief of the General Staff Fayyad Al-Ruwaili command of the joint forces — a change two Western diplomats linked directly to the battlefield setbacks. Senior Houthi official Hussein al-Ezzi said the movement does not intend to advance further toward Aden. The Houthis also claimed on Sunday to have hit King Khalid International Airport and a financial centre in Riyadh with two ballistic missiles; the coalition said it intercepted two Houthi drones over the Mecca and Riyadh regions on Monday.

For ordinary people, the disconnect between the battlefield and the market is still the story. Brent crude fell to about $91.66 a barrel early on Tuesday on the IEA's forecast of a 1.7-million-barrel-a-day global surplus this quarter, then bounced back toward $93.50 as reports of the Qeshm strikes hit, according to XTB — the market is swinging on each new incident. Traffic through the strait is still only about 49% of its pre-war level, and the human cost is concentrated in Yemen, where more than 348,000 people have been displaced since August 1.

In short: a fourth tanker burned in Hormuz in under 24 hours, Iran hit at Bahrain again and US strikes on Qeshm Island were reported but not confirmed, Muscat produced no deal, and the Houthis sit about 60 km from Aden after Saudi Arabia changed its coalition commander. Watch whether Washington confirms the Qeshm strikes, whether the tanker attacks continue, and Thursday's Shenzhen trilateral, where Trump says he will raise Iran.

Tech products

Nvidia's first report without Huang beats everything — and the board says he's not coming back

Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion on Tuesday, up 103% from a year earlier and well above the $109.1 billion Wall Street expected, in its first earnings since co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO on November 12 — and the board used the earnings call to close the door on his return. Adjusted earnings were $2.68 a share against a $2.47 consensus, and the company guided fourth-quarter revenue to $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, above the roughly $125 billion analysts expected. Shares, which closed the regular session at $155.46, down 0.4% on the day, rose roughly 3–4% in after-hours trading.

The details, as reported by MLQ.ai, CNBC via MLQ, Reuters and the Motley Fool: data centre revenue rose 110% to $107.6 billion — hyperscale sales of $60.1 billion, up 71%, and AI clouds, industrial and enterprise sales of $47.5 billion, up 195%. GAAP net income was $65.3 billion, GAAP diluted EPS $2.70, and gross margin was 74.8%. Chair Harvey Jones opened the 5 p.m. ET call by saying Huang resigned on November 12 "after disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, standards, and cultural norms," and that "the Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Hours earlier, Huang had said he would be honoured to return if asked. Jones said the board has retained Heidrick & Struggles for the CEO search and is considering internal and external candidates: "We want to install the CEO, the permanent CEO, as quickly as possible. But we won't expedite the CEO before finding exactly the right person." Interim CEO Colette Kress raised the fiscal 2028 revenue growth outlook to about 75%, up from the 70% forecast in August, and said it is "business as usual," with customers, suppliers and partners "100% behind us." She also said Nvidia is no longer shipping Hopper products to China, and the fourth-quarter outlook assumes no data centre compute revenue from China.

The arguments split two ways. The bull case is the numbers themselves: revenue more than doubled, the beat was on every headline metric, Vera Rubin is shipping and Nvidia called it the fastest product ramp in its history, and Kress raised the long-term growth outlook. Dan Ives of Yorkville Ives & Co. said, "Strong guide and many did not expect that." The bear case, as the Motley Fool put it, is that the guidance is softer than it looks — the fourth quarter is a 14-week quarter, so spread across 14 weeks it implies weekly revenue up only about 4% from the third quarter — and gross margin is guided down to 71–72%. Growth is also tying up cash: accounts receivable reached $81.0 billion, or 64 days of sales, up from 60 days, and the Fool noted receivables rose about $18 billion in the quarter, nearly matching the revenue increase. And the leadership question, while answered for now, is not settled: the board's investigation into the reports that Huang was targeted by a foreign intelligence operation continues, and the board said it would take no questions on the circumstances of the resignation.

For ordinary people, the stakes are mostly in retirement accounts and the broader market: Nvidia is the world's third-largest company and the anchor of the AI rally, and the stock had fallen about 30% in a month to its lowest since July 1, 2025, before this report. The after-hours bounce is relief, not resolution — the stock is still down roughly 27% over the past month and about 36% below its October record of $243.37. The next tests are whether a permanent CEO is named, what the board's investigation finds, and whether receivables keep growing as fast as revenue.

In short: Nvidia beat on revenue, earnings and guidance in its first report without Huang, raised its fiscal 2028 growth outlook to ~75%, and the board said plainly it does not intend to reinstate him — and the market rewarded it with a 3–4% after-hours rise. Watch the CEO search, the investigation's findings, and Wednesday's regular-session open for whether the bounce holds.

Cybersecurity

  • CISA added Puppet Enterprise RCE CVE-2026-115731 to KEV today: CISA added the flaw to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on November 17, with a federal remediation deadline of December 1. It is a deserialization bug (CVSS 9.4) in Puppet Enterprise's report-processing code: any machine with a CA-signed agent certificate can send a crafted report and run code on the Puppet Server. Perforce fixed it on November 3 in Puppet Enterprise 2023.8.13, 2025.12.1 and 2026.0.1, along with a second critical bug, CVE-2026-115732, a certificate-name authorization bypass that was not added to KEV. Open-source Puppet has no fix. CISA has not said who is exploiting it. The KEV catalog now stands at 1,782 entries. (Corgea · Undercode News · Carthage Electronics)
  • Apache HTTP/2 flaw CVE-2026-119618 is now reportedly being exploited: Singapore's Cyber Security Agency told organisations on November 17 to patch, saying the flaw in mod_http2 is "reportedly being actively exploited, and a proof-of-concept exploit is publicly available." That updates this morning's brief, which said there was no known in-the-wild exploitation. The flaw was fixed in Apache 2.4.71 on November 11 and affects versions 2.4.55–2.4.70 with HTTP/2 enabled. It was still not listed in CISA's KEV catalog as of 12:25 UTC on Tuesday. (RecaTools)
  • KEV deadlines: the federal deadlines for Check Point CVE-2026-123039 and UnrealIRCd CVE-2026-114713 passed yesterday (November 16) — both are now overdue. Apple CVE-2024-27834 is due tomorrow, November 18, and SonicWall SonicOS CVE-2026-19868 is due November 20. (Carthage Electronics)

Major court cases

Israel's opposition takes the speaker's chair — and Netanyahu's grip slips

Israel's parliament voted on Tuesday to install Matan Kahana of Gadi Eisenkot's Yashar party as Knesset speaker, replacing Amir Ohana of Benjamin Netanyahu's Likud, in the opposition bloc's first major move since the October 27 election stripped Netanyahu of his majority. Kahana won 66 votes to 52 for Likud's candidate, coalition whip Ofir Katz, after a stormy seven-hour debate — and the vote hands the bloc seeking to replace Netanyahu control of the legislative agenda before a new government is even formed.

The details, as reported by Reuters, CNN and AP: lawmakers voted separately on each candidate rather than in a head-to-head ballot. Kahana was backed by the Jewish-led opposition parties and by the Arab-led parties — the opposition holds 67 seats in the 120-member chamber, including 48 across four Jewish parties and a record 19 for the two Arab parties, the Joint List and Raam. In his first comments as speaker, Kahana struck a conciliatory note: "The Knesset of Israel is the home of all the citizens of the state of Israel, and I intend to be the speaker of everyone." He also said he would abide by the decisions of the Supreme Court. The Eisenkot bloc has already appointed interim chairs for key Knesset committees, including finance and defence, and Avigdor Liberman, now chairing the Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, has said he will summon all the security chiefs for briefings. Special committees have been set up to repeal a bill that changed how judges are selected and legislation that limits the attorney general, and to undo a media regulation overhaul; the bloc has also pledged to establish a state commission of inquiry into the failures behind the October 7, 2023 Hamas attack, which Netanyahu has refused to investigate.

The two sides read the vote very differently. Eisenkot called Kahana's appointment "a proper democratic move which reflects the absolute majority who voted for change," and the bloc's priorities — term limits for the prime minister, a state inquiry into October 7, and rolling back the 2023 judicial overhaul that sparked nationwide protests — are now on the table. Likud condemned the outcome as "a low point in the history of the Knesset," saying Kahana was elected with "the votes of terror supporters," and during the debate members of Netanyahu's bloc shouted "shame" at each Arab lawmaker who voted for Kahana. Netanyahu has spent the campaign attacking Israel's Arab-led parties as an existential threat, and has baselessly alleged voter fraud by Arabs; election officials say they have seen no evidence of fraud that could have influenced the outcome. Analysts quoted by AP caution that time is short: the speaker can make the legislative process much faster or slower, but bills must be introduced, considered in committee and voted on several times before passing, and Eisenkot has only about four and a half to five more weeks to form a government. The obstacles are real — Liberman has vetoed any government that relies on Arab parties' support and is pushing talks with the ultra-Orthodox parties instead, former prime minister Naftali Bennett reportedly favours a unity government with Likud, and Netanyahu has conditioned any talks on remaining prime minister, which Eisenkot has rejected. If no one can form a government, another election will be held next year.

For ordinary people, the vote is about who controls the rules of Israeli democracy for the next few years: whether the judicial overhaul laws are repealed, whether there is finally an independent inquiry into October 7, and whether the Knesset's agenda is set by Netanyahu's caretaker government or by the bloc that beat him at the ballot box. The scars of October 7 were on display in the chamber itself — Likud member Talik Gvili, whose son was a police officer killed by Palestinian militants on October 7, interrupted Joint List member Ahmad Tibi mid-speech, shouting "Who killed my son?" and Tibi replied from the dais: "The one who committed the murder is Hamas. The one responsible is your prime minister."

In short: Kahana's 66–52 win gives the opposition control of the Knesset's agenda and committees, with judicial-overhaul repeal and an October 7 inquiry as its first targets — but Eisenkot still has to form a government within about five weeks, and his own bloc is split over how. Watch the committee moves this week and whether the ultra-Orthodox parties open talks with Eisenkot.
