World escalation

Ukraine's biggest drone wave yet hits toward Moscow as Russia strikes Kharkiv — and a fragile truce holds in the south

Overnight, Ukraine sent one of the largest drone waves of the war toward the Russian capital, while Russia kept hitting Ukrainian cities — and in the south, a rare US-brokered truce is holding just long enough for an aid mission to move.

Reuters reports that Russia downed most of 613 drones heading toward Moscow overnight, according to Moscow mayor Sergei Sobyanin, who reported the figure on Telegram. In Ukraine, Russia attacked Kharkiv, the country's second-biggest city, damaging an educational institution, mayor Ihor Terekhov said. Times Now's overnight coverage adds that the Kharkiv strikes hit at least two districts — Saltivskyi, where the educational institution was struck, and Kholodnohirskyi, where a separate attack sparked a fire — and that Russian forces used both jet-powered and conventional attack drones. The scale of the Moscow-bound wave stands out: it follows a week in which Ukrainian officials tallied 787 Russian drones and 26 civilians killed, and it comes days after Ukraine resumed strikes on Russian energy targets.

The two sides tell the story very differently. Moscow frames its air defenses as having blunted the attack, with Sobyanin's account emphasizing how many drones were downed; Kyiv's account, via Terekhov, emphasizes the civilian infrastructure hit in Kharkiv. Neither side's overnight claims have been independently verified, and casualty figures from Kharkiv were not yet confirmed in early reporting.

Meanwhile, the war's other overnight story is a truce that is actually holding. Reuters reports that neither Ukraine nor Russia has reported any violations so far of the US-brokered six-day ceasefire that took effect early Tuesday around the Russian-controlled town of Oleshky, in the Kherson region, to allow evacuations and aid deliveries. Russia's human rights commissioner Yana Lantratova was en route to Oleshky on Thursday with four journalists from the US outlet Newsmax, while her Ukrainian counterpart Dmytro Lubinets was also heading to the area. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said, via Interfax, that demining is under way and that Moscow hopes residents will be able to receive aid "today or tomorrow" and that those wishing to leave will be able to do so. The truce was brokered by US First Lady Melania Trump with the involvement of Pope Leo, and applies to the town and surrounding areas, including the wider Kherson region, which has suffered years of near-daily drone and artillery attacks. Residents there have faced regular shelling and severe shortages of food and medicine, with Moscow and Kyiv blaming each other for the situation — which is why the mission matters more than the headlines: if aid actually gets in Thursday or Friday, it will be the first relief for people trapped in the town in months.

For ordinary people, the night captures the war's split reality: in Moscow and Kharkiv, air-raid routines and damaged schools; in Oleshky, a six-day window — running to November 1 — in which civilians may finally get food, medicine, or a way out. What to watch: whether the Oleshky evacuations actually begin Friday as planned, and whether the record-size Moscow drone wave draws a Russian retaliation barrage.

In short: Ukraine launched a record-scale drone wave at Moscow, Russia hit Kharkiv, and a rare truce around Oleshky is holding — with an aid mission expected Thursday or Friday.

Iran claims three tankers hit mines in Hormuz as US warns allies of post-midterm strikes — and a back-channel letter surfaces

The Strait of Hormuz standoff sharpened overnight on two fronts at once: a disputed claim of new tanker sinkings, and fresh signs Washington is preparing allies — and Tehran — for what comes after next Tuesday's US midterms.

Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said Thursday that three oil tankers exploded and caught fire after striking sea mines while using what Tehran calls an unauthorized southern route through the Strait of Hormuz over the past 24 hours, according to Iranian state media carried by Reuters and others. The IRGC said the vessels had switched off their radar and navigation systems, ignored its warnings, and were abandoned along the strait's southern coast. It did not name the ships, their flags, or their crews. Separately, the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre (UKMTO) confirmed a vessel burning about 9 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman, at around 0300 UTC — but identified neither the cause nor the crew's condition, and no connection between that sighting and the three tankers has been established.

Washington disputes the mine story outright. US Central Command said earlier this week that Iranian mine claims are "false," that US forces have cleared the internationally recognized main transit routes, and that thousands of vessels are passing safely. IRGC spokesman Hossein Mohebi challenged Washington on Thursday to provide independent evidence for its mine-clearance claims. The two accounts can't both be right about the main shipping lanes — and the gap between them is exactly what keeps war-risk insurance premiums at record levels and many tankers rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope.

At the same time, the diplomatic track is moving in parallel with the military one. Two diplomats briefed on US warnings told reporters that Washington has urged Middle East allies to prepare for possible renewed strikes on Iran after the November 3 midterms, with a campaign that could last several days, and to brace for Iranian retaliation against energy facilities. Separately, the Wall Street Journal first reported — via Axios and other outlets — that Vice President JD Vance sent a letter to Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, saying any talks to avert a US strike must address Iran's nuclear program. It is described as the highest-level US contact with Tehran in months. President Trump publicly offered a different explanation on Wednesday, saying the letter was about getting medical supplies into Iran. Tehran, for its part, wants the US naval blockade lifted, frozen money released, and oil sales cleared before further engagement — while Washington insists nuclear talks come first and sanctions relief follows progress. Iran's foreign minister said Thursday that Tehran is still giving diplomacy a chance under a seven-day stepwise de-escalation plan, while Iranian security officials warned of broader retaliation if attacked. Iran also dismissed a Qatari peace draft as "unacceptable ideas," according to Thursday analysis.

For ordinary people, the stakes are the gas pump and the grocery bill: Brent crude slipped to about $114.10 a barrel and WTI to about $101.45 in early Thursday trading as diplomacy headlines briefly outweighed the tanker claims — still far above pre-crisis levels, and still hostage to whether the strait stays open. OPEC+ meets November 1, three days from now.

In short: Iran claims three tankers hit mines in Hormuz — unverified and disputed by CENTCOM — while the US warns allies to prepare for possible post-midterm strikes and a Vance letter tests whether nuclear talks can head them off. Developing.

Sources: Reuters via Hatha Alyoum, UKMTO via House of Saud, Eastora News, ENM News / WSJ, Particle, Discovery Alert

Yemen: 749 government strikes in 24 hours, 101 Saudi strikes claimed, and 14 reported killed at a market

Fighting inside Yemen surged to a new intensity overnight, even as Houthi missiles kept flying at Saudi territory.

Government forces said they carried out 749 attacks on Houthi command centres, fortifications, vehicles and supply lines over the past 24 hours, claiming 239 Houthi fighters neutralized and 117 military vehicles destroyed or damaged, according to a statement from spokesperson Majid al-Nuzaili carried by Xinhua and IANS. Government forces said they continued to consolidate gains in the southwestern province of Taiz. The Houthis, meanwhile, said Saudi forces carried out 101 air and missile strikes on Sanaa and eight provinces over the same 24 hours, claiming dozens dead or wounded, including women and children. Neither set of figures has been independently verified — access for independent journalists in Yemen remains severely restricted.

The civilian toll is contested in the same way. Houthi-run Al-Masirah television reported that at least 14 people were killed Wednesday in attacks on a public market and the nearby Al-Tayseer fuel station in Al-Hazm, the capital of northern Al-Jawf province, with 10 to 15 injured including women and children. Saudi authorities had not immediately responded to the allegation when initial reports were published. The same figure — 14 killed, 10 wounded — appeared in Wednesday evening reporting of a Saudi strike on Jawf/Al Hazm, but the two reports describe different target types, and the claims remain unverified.

On the Saudi side, the coalition said Thursday it intercepted a ballistic missile fired toward the kingdom's Eastern Province, spokesman Turki al-Malki posted on X — a day after a Taif intercept. The Houthis said this week they had targeted the Ras Tanura refinery in the oil-rich Eastern Province. And fresh explosions were heard near Aden International Airport on Wednesday, shortly after Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree issued what he called a "final" evacuation warning, alleging — without evidence — that a Saudi military cargo aircraft was present transporting weapons. Hundreds of passengers, including patients, left the airport following the warnings; casualties and damage from the latest blasts were not immediately confirmed.

For ordinary Yemenis, this is the world's worst humanitarian crisis getting worse: humanitarian agencies warn escalations of this scale severely disrupt aid delivery, and the airport at Aden is the south's humanitarian lifeline. For everyone else, the fighting is one more reason oil stays near $114 and Red Sea shipping stays cautious.

In short: Yemen's ground war escalated sharply — 749 government strikes claimed against 101 Saudi strikes claimed — with 14 reported killed at an Al-Jawf market and fuel station, a Houthi missile intercepted over Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province, and fresh blasts at Aden airport. Figures from both sides are unverified.

Israel's election settled nothing: Netanyahu ahead at 53 seats, no bloc near a majority

Israel's election produced a clear winner of the night and no winner of the government. With nearly all ordinary ballots counted Thursday morning, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc of right-wing, far-right and ultra-Orthodox parties held 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, according to Reuters and Israel's Central Elections Committee figures carried by multiple outlets, while Gadi Eisenkot's opposition bloc trailed on 48. Arab-led parties — the Joint List and Ra'am — surged to a combined 19 seats, nearly double their 10 in the outgoing Knesset and described by Israeli outlets as a historic high. Likud remained the largest single party at 26 seats, followed by Eisenkot's Yashar at 21.

The arithmetic is the story: 61 seats are needed for a majority, and neither bloc reaches it. As the Council on Foreign Relations' Elliott Abrams wrote from Jerusalem on Thursday morning, Netanyahu's result is "both a defeat and a victory" — his outgoing coalition held 68 seats at its peak, so this is a real reduction, yet he is positioned to continue as prime minister because the president must ask the politician best situated to form a government to try, and that has to be Netanyahu. The question, Abrams notes, is where Netanyahu finds eight more seats: the opposition has promised not to serve in a new Netanyahu government, and Eisenkot has ruled out governing with Arab parties' support. If Netanyahu fails, President Isaac Herzog turns to Eisenkot — who faces the same wall from 48. The remaining path runs through broken campaign promises: a grand coalition, defectors, or a rotation deal — including the theory that Netanyahu, who just turned 77, might step aside in two years to seek the presidency when Herzog's term ends.

The count itself is not quite finished: roughly 430,000–480,000 double-envelope ballots, including soldiers' and reservists' votes, were still being counted overnight into Thursday, with final certified results expected around midday Thursday and the statutory publication deadline November 4. Turnout was 77.7%, the highest in over 20 years, with Arab turnout approaching 80%.

Markets have already voted on the uncertainty: the shekel weakened about 2% to 3.1185 to the dollar on Wednesday — its worst day in 16 months — and Tel Aviv's benchmark index fell around 3%, reversing gains made when high turnout had raised hopes of a change of government. Capital Economics' William Jackson said the next government "will probably be fragile," and failure by either leader to form one would likely mean another election, with Netanyahu remaining as caretaker in the meantime. For the region, CFR notes the foreign-policy stakes: on Gaza, Lebanon, Syria and Iran there is broad Israeli consensus, but an Eisenkot government would be less likely to attack Iran soon simply because it would be new — while Netanyahu will likely soon ask President Trump about a renewed assault on Iran's nuclear sites.

In short: Netanyahu's bloc leads 53–48 with Arab parties holding a record 19, but nobody has the 61 seats needed to govern — so coalition bargaining, and possibly another election, comes next. Final certified results are expected Thursday.

Apple opened pre-orders for five new products, including its first touchscreen MacBooks and a new OLED iPad mini

Apple opened pre-orders Wednesday for five new products, MacRumors reports: the 14-inch MacBook Pro with M6 ($1,999), the M6 iMac ($1,499), the new iPad mini with an 8.5-inch OLED display and A20 Pro chip ($699), and the first touchscreen MacBook Pros — 14-inch from $2,899 and 16-inch from $3,399, with M5 Pro/Max and a PROLED tandem OLED touch display. Alphabet shares fell ~11% after its Q3 cash burn — but Google's TPU chip sales are now a real business

Alphabet shares fell nearly 11% on Thursday after the company reported another quarter of cash burn and said AI infrastructure spending would rise sharply next year, Reuters reports. The flip side, laid out by finance chief Anat Ashkenazi on the post-earnings call: Google's homegrown TPU chips, once reserved for internal use, are now sold to outside customers and made a "meaningful" contribution as Google Cloud revenue more than doubled in the September quarter (+103% to $30.7B). The catch is cash flow — unlike search or cloud, selling chip systems means spending on hardware well before customers pay, so inventory of unsold TPU systems is now showing up on the balance sheet. TPU system sales are expected to rise further this quarter and ramp significantly in 2027. Why it matters: Google is now a genuine Nvidia challenger, not just a customer — and investors are pricing in how expensive that ambition is. Source: Reuters

Nvidia signs study to put data centers on retired German nuclear sites

Nvidia has signed a preliminary agreement with German nuclear operator PreussenElektra to study whether retired nuclear plant sites could be repurposed as large data centers, Softonic reports — using the land, grid links and cooling water already built around the plants, not restarting any reactors. Germany shut its last operating reactors in April 2023 and has about 30 plants in closure or dismantling, eight of them PreussenElektra's. Still only a feasibility study, not a construction plan, and public support will hinge on renewables and environmental protections. Source: Softonic (single source)

Open source

JetBrains' ThinkRail tops GitHub trending — an open-source IDE built around the pi coding agent

ThinkRail, JetBrains' open-source IDE for "vibe coding" with the pi coding agent, was first on GitHub's daily trending list on October 28 with 4,585 stars, 1,075 added that day, according to Digital Soul Hub. It's Apache-2.0, written in TypeScript, and runs pi in-process — pi owns models, skills and session state while ThinkRail provides the editor, files, terminals, git and agent workspaces. No accounts of its own; sign in with JetBrains AI or any provider pi supports. Why it matters: it's JetBrains' answer to the agent-IDE wave, and it topped trending the day after GitHub Universe. Source: Digital Soul Hub

DeepTutor nears 49,000 stars as the fastest-moving open-source project of the day

DeepTutor, the self-hosted AI tutoring app from Hong Kong University's data-intelligence lab, added 2,371 stars in the last day to reach 48,899, the biggest jump of any AI repo on the day, per Silicon Noon's Open Source Radar. Point it at your own PDFs or course folders and it builds a learning path, generates graded quizzes, and tracks what you've actually mastered. Apache 2.0, runs on your own API key or a local model — so student data doesn't have to go to a vendor. Source: Silicon Noon

Cybersecurity

PHP2Shell federal patch deadline is today — and exploitation is spreading to firewall management interfaces

Today, October 29, is the federal deadline CISA set for patching the critical PHP flaws CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 (PHP2Shell, CVSS 9.2), added to the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 26. Palo Alto Networks now reports exploitation against internet-exposed firewall management interfaces, according to SecureResearch's October 29 daily brief, and Censys counted 32.5 million vulnerable PHP services as of October 27 against only about 11.2 million updated. Fixes are PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35 — updating Drupal or app code alone doesn't fix the runtime. Why it matters: this is the week's biggest patching fire, and the deadline is today, not next week. Sources: SecureResearch, The Drop Times

Volt Typhoon's covert "Silent Train" network disrupted

Lumen Black Lotus Labs attributes a now-disrupted network called "Silent Train" to the China-nexus group Volt Typhoon, according to SecureResearch's October 29 brief. The network was used in four zero-day exploitation campaigns, and sources told Nextgov that Energy Department facilities and utilities were affected. Why it matters: Volt Typhoon is the group US officials have tied to pre-positioning in critical infrastructure — a disrupted covert network is a rare visible win against it. Source: SecureResearch

iRhythm breach now put at 8.2 million people in HHS filing

An HHS filing puts the iRhythm medical-device breach at 8.2 million people, SecurityWeek reports via SecureResearch — a huge jump from the 360,000+ figure reported when the breach first surfaced. The breach hit third-party-hosted apps in June, with a ransom demand June 9. Why it matters: it's now one of the largest healthcare breaches of the year, and the scale revision shows how early breach estimates can understate the damage. Source: SecureResearch

Cisco SD-WAN authentication bypass exploited by a named APT

Cisco Talos detailed the UAT-11473 APT exploiting a Cisco SD-WAN authentication bypass, per SecureResearch's October 29 brief, and attackers are also scanning for a newly patched Oracle E-Business Suite remote code execution flaw. Edge devices remain the week's theme: if you run Cisco SD-WAN, check Talos's write-up. Source: SecureResearch

Russell Brand trial: wife Laura Brand gives evidence as cross-examination continues

Laura Brand gave evidence Thursday in her husband's trial at Southwark Crown Court, The Times reports — describing him as "caring," "amazing" and "attentive," saying "I love him very much," and telling the court the pair got back together in 2015 and married in 2017.
