World escalation

Houthis open a second front in Aden as Saudi strikes kill up to 16 in Yemen

The Houthis struck Yemen's interim capital Aden on Saturday while also firing at targets across Saudi Arabia, opening a second front against the Saudi-backed government on its own territory. Yemen's Interior Ministry said two missiles hit the coast guard headquarters inside the ministry's compound and a third struck a coast guard building in the Tawahi district, according to the state-run Saba news agency. Separately, Yemen's Transport Ministry said two ballistic missiles hit Aden International Airport, calling it the "fourth such attack this month" and warning of "a threat to the safety of civil aviation." No casualties were reported in Aden, and an Anadolu Agency correspondent heard three explosions across the city.

The two sides describe the Aden strikes differently, and the gap matters. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree said his forces targeted "Saudi enemy concentrations" at the al-Nasr camp and a naval base in Aden, naming a Saudi brigadier general he said oversaw them. Saree did not mention the airport; the government did not mention al-Nasr camp. Whether they are describing the same impacts under different names or separate strikes is unclear. What is clear is the intent: the Houthis are framing Aden not as Yemen's capital under attack but as a Saudi forward base, while the government treats the coast guard — a force Saudi Arabia has funded and equipped to patrol the Red Sea approach — as its own.

On the Saudi side, the coalition confirmed intercepting three ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh, Yanbu and Taif, plus a drone in the Eastern Province, and debris fell in Mecca, Jeddah, Taif and Rabigh. Saree claimed far more — eight sites including the Yanbu Aramco facility, the Rabigh refinery, the East-West pipeline, Dammam and Najran airports and Khamis Mushait air base — but Saudi authorities confirmed none of the oil-site hits. The pattern of the last two weeks held: Houthi claims run well ahead of what Riyadh confirms.

The human cost on the other side of the exchange came from Houthi-run Al-Masirah, relayed by AFP: Saudi strikes killed 11 in Sanaa — four in a house and six women and a child at a cement factory's workers' housing — plus five more in a family home in Hodeida, for a reported total of 16. Saudi Arabia has not commented on civilian casualties in Yemen, and independent verification inside Houthi-held territory is effectively impossible, so those figures are one side's account, not confirmed tolls. The Aden strikes follow a pattern this month: the presidential palace on October 15 (5 killed, 16 wounded) and a military coordination site near the airport on October 21, now the coast guard command — each attack picking off a different node of the Saudi-backed government's infrastructure.

Caption: Aden harbour from the water, with the Tawahi district on the volcanic headland — the neighbourhood where a coast guard building was struck on October 24. Aden has served as Yemen's interim capital since the Houthis seized Sanaa in 2014, and its port and coast guard anchor the Saudi-backed government's presence in the south.

For ordinary people, the stakes are aviation and everyday safety: a fourth missile attack this month on Aden's only functioning commercial airport, debris falling across Saudi Arabia's western seaboard including Mecca and Jeddah, and another round of civilian deaths in Sanaa and Hodeida. The GCC secretary-general called the attacks on Saudi Arabia "heinous," but there has been no comparable UN Security Council statement on the Aden strikes.

In short: the Houthis are now fighting on two fronts in a single day — Saudi cities from the south and the Saudi-backed government in Aden from the north — and the war's civilian toll is rising on both sides of the exchange, with the claims on each side still unverified where it matters most.

Sources: House of Saud, Defense and Tech, WION

Oleshky ceasefire firms up for October 27 — and Starlink is back, Ukrainian officials say

The local ceasefire both sides agreed to on Friday now has a converging start date, and a second long-running friction point appears to have eased. Kyiv and Moscow both say their forces will stop firing around the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky from October 27 — Russia's General Gerasimov put it at 4 a.m. Moscow time, Ukraine's ombudsman Dmytro Lubinets at 3 a.m. Kyiv time, the same moment — to let aid in and civilians out. The White House statement still says October 29, a discrepancy that has not been resolved, but the two armies that would actually have to hold fire now agree on the 27th. Ukraine's top negotiator says the ceasefire will cover the wider Kherson region and neighbouring areas needed for the operation, and last several days.

The deal was brokered with US First Lady Melania Trump as what her office called the "exclusive intermediary," and the Kremlin credited her with a "significant contribution." Civilian, non-military officials from both countries are to visit residents at home to assess needs, and those who want to leave will be taken to Russia or to Ukraine, their choice. Around 1,700 civilians remain in Oleshky, down from 24,000 before the war, and conditions are dire: mined roads, limited electricity, and, according to a Nobel Peace Prize-winning Ukrainian activist cited by CNN, people eating grass, with the last official food delivery in May. Pope Leo also raised the town with both Putin and Zelensky in the past week.

Separately, the Institute for the Study of War's October 24 assessment says Ukrainian officials are confirming the resumption of unrestricted access to Starlink and Starshield — the satellite system Ukrainian forces rely on for communications and missile warning — after weeks of speed restrictions that Kyiv said disrupted military operations. That is a sharp change from Friday, when Ukrainian officials said the curbs were still in place despite President Trump's claim they had been lifted. No official explanation for the restrictions, or their lifting, has been given.

On the wider war, the diplomatic track also moved: Zelensky said negotiators from Ukraine, Russia and the US agreed to technical-level talks in the first half of November in Abu Dhabi, though a US official later said Abu Dhabi or Switzerland within a couple of weeks. Zelensky said he hopes a leaders' meeting follows, but cautioned that much depends on Russia — a similar meeting expected this month never happened, and Moscow still demands all of Donbas, including territory it does not control, which Kyiv rejects.

For ordinary Ukrainians, the Oleshky deal is narrow — one town, a few days — but it is the first agreed pause either side has confirmed in weeks, and restored Starlink access matters far beyond it: it affects how Ukrainian forces communicate, coordinate and receive warning of incoming missiles. The November talks are the real test of whether a humanitarian opening becomes anything bigger.

In short: both armies now agree the Oleshky guns fall silent October 27, Starlink restrictions appear lifted, and three-way talks are set for early November — modest steps, but the first ones in the same direction in weeks.

Sources: Reuters, CNN, ISW October 24 assessment

Oil at $117: the US taps its emergency reserve as Hormuz claims go unverified

Brent crude briefly topped $117 a barrel on Friday — its highest since the war involving Iran began — and settled at $116.72, with WTI at $103.27, as the Strait of Hormuz shipping crisis kept tightening. The US Department of Energy on Friday authorized an emergency exchange of up to 400,000 barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve for Phillips 66's Lake Charles refinery in Louisiana, to offset a crude delivery disruption. Reuters notes the reserve is already at its lowest since 1982 after the releases tied to the Iran war, and this draw, like the 4-million-barrel hurricane exchange earlier in October, is a loan: Phillips 66 must return more barrels than it borrows.

The price spike is being driven by claims that remain unverified. Iran's Revolutionary Guard said two supertankers carrying more than 4.5 million barrels combined struck naval mines on what Tehran calls an unauthorized southern route, with crews abandoning both vessels — a claim carried by Iranian state media and several regional outlets but confirmed by no maritime authority. President Trump dismissed the claims on Thursday as fabricated to lift oil prices before the November 3 midterms, saying more oil is moving through Hormuz than before the war. Independent monitors split the difference: IMF PortWatch recorded just 4 transits on October 18 against a pre-crisis baseline of 85 per day, while Kpler put total oil flows for the week ending October 18 at 24.5 million barrels per day — roughly pre-war levels, rerouted rather than stopped.

October is already the worst month on record for attacks on commercial shipping in and around the strait, per MarineLink and Reuters, with 28 attacks in the month and 113 since February 28. An Iranian envoy also said Iran remains open to talks with the US under a Pakistan-brokered deal, according to AP — a diplomatic thread running alongside the military one.

For ordinary people, this is already a fuel-price story: diesel prices in Europe have jumped sharply, US fuel markets are leaning on an emergency reserve at 1980s lows, and every unverified mine claim moves the pump price regardless of whether a ship actually sank. The gap between what Tehran claims, what Washington denies, and what shippers can confirm is itself now a market force.

In short: oil is at wartime highs, the US is lending from a depleted emergency stockpile, and the Hormuz shipping war is being fought as much through claims and counterclaims as through actual strikes — watch whether any independent authority confirms a vessel loss, which would be the next real escalation.

Sources: Reuters, US Department of Energy, Straits Daily Brief

  • Microduck — a tiny open-source robot duck that walks with reinforcement learning. Pollen Robotics' Microduck is a 25 cm, 800 g biped driven by a 50 Hz control loop over 15 servos, with policies trained in simulation (MuJoCo/PPO) and exported to ONNX. Cybersecurity

  • Capcom breach: 11.7 million CAPCOM ID emails exposed (catch-up, disclosed October 22). Capcom confirmed unauthorized third-party access to its CAPCOM ID membership service, exposing email addresses plus account creation date, last login, login count and last-login IP for 11,774,247 accounts. For 43 accounts, birth dates, gender, country and nicknames were also exposed, and 196 accounts had passwords changed without authorization (since reset). No credit card data was involved. Capcom detected the intrusion October 19 and reported it to Osaka police and Japan's privacy regulator October 21. The real risk now is phishing using the leaked emails. Sources: Beebom, BleepingComputer via Cybernoz
  • Electrum 4.8.3 patches a flaw that let rogue servers steal Bitcoin. A malicious server could swap a user's payment for a fraudulent one, and when the user bumped the fee (RBF), they signed the theft themselves. One user reported a loss; every version before 4.8.3 is affected. Update at electrum.org. Source: CoinLab
  • INTERPOL Operation Serengeti III: 1,462 suspects arrested, 10,909 cybercrime networks disrupted, $105M+ recovered. One of the largest coordinated cybercrime operations to date. Source: SparTech roundup
  • ESET: Russian group UAC-0099 breached a Ukrainian grain logistics firm, then handed access to Sandworm, which deployed a new Yggdrasil-linked backdoor and wiped data — a reminder that initial-access brokers and state actors still work as a pipeline. Source: SparTech roundup
