Morning brief — Friday, November 13, 2026

Ukraine fires nearly 1,200 drones at Russia in its biggest barrage in years — as the peace talks widen to Europe, and Europe pushes back

Ukraine launched one of its biggest aerial attacks of the war overnight, firing almost 1,200 drones at targets across Russia, while Russia hit Ukraine with 117 drones of its own — including a strike that set a Kyiv hospital on fire and one that wounded 25 people in Zaporizhzhia. The exchange came as US envoys tried to widen the peace talks to include Europe, and European leaders pushed back hard against Moscow's attempt to put the post-Cold War security order itself on the table.

The details are stark on both sides. Russia's Defense Ministry said its air defences downed 1,198 Ukrainian long-range drones between late Thursday and early Friday, across more than 15 regions — but strikes still got through in three regions, killing seven people including a 12-year-old and wounding 17, according to local officials. Moscow's mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, said 467 drones targeted the capital overnight. In Volgograd, in southern Russia, drones hit an industrial area, killing four people and injuring two, regional governor Andrei Bocharov said; the city is home to a major Lukoil refinery, and unverified video on social media purported to show it on fire, though there was no official confirmation the refinery was hit. Ukraine has concentrated its long-range strikes this year on Russian oil infrastructure, aiming to cut the energy earnings funding Moscow's invasion.

Russia's reply was smaller but hit civilians. Ukraine's Air Force said it shot down 80 of 117 Russian drones launched overnight. In Kyiv, a Russian drone struck the Institute of Traumatology and Orthopedics in the Shevchenkivskyi district at about 3 a.m. local time, setting it on fire and badly damaging it while patients and medical staff were inside; one person was injured. Ukrainian Prime Minister Serhiy Koretskyi said Russian forces targeted energy infrastructure — mainly high-voltage substations — in nine regions, with damage also to a logistics terminal in Kharkiv and a dormitory in Rivne. In Odesa, two people were killed and five wounded; in Zaporizhzhia, a 17-year-old boy was killed and a single strike on two five-storey apartment blocks wounded 25 people, including six children. First Deputy Energy Minister Artem Nekrasov said the attacks caused widespread power outages across about half of the country's regions.

What each side is arguing about now is bigger than the battlefield. After two days of talks in Abu Dhabi, US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner briefed Putin in Moscow on Thursday, then Zelenskyy in Dublin, where European leaders were meeting. Kushner said late Thursday that Russia had agreed to include Europe in the next round of talks, writing that "we cannot conclude this deal without the Europeans being at the table" — a reversal of Moscow's long insistence that European governments be excluded. But on Friday afternoon the Kremlin said it had not yet agreed: spokesman Dmitry Peskov said Moscow would decide on an expanded format only "once we understand their real intentions and their willingness to contribute to the peace process." European leaders, meanwhile, are alarmed at what Moscow wants to discuss. Unconfirmed reports say Russia has demanded wider European security concessions, including Finland leaving NATO and a pledge not to base troops in the Baltics. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz rejected the premise outright: "Russia wants to talk about questions of European security architecture. We don't need to talk about that. There is one," he said, referring to NATO. The EU's top diplomat, Kaja Kallas, said Europe must insist on a ceasefire before taking the negotiations further — the opposite of what Putin demands — warning that "if we start to talk about what Russia is asking regarding the security architecture, we actually walk into the traps that Russia is setting us." The Institute for the Study of War assessed late Thursday that Moscow is aiming "to dismantle NATO and the post-Cold War European security architecture."

For ordinary people the war's grind is now measured in power cuts and hospital evacuations. Russia has battered Ukraine's power grid every winter since the invasion began, and Friday's outages hit about half the country's regions as temperatures drop. In Kyiv, a hospital treating civilians caught fire in the middle of the night; in Zaporizhzhia, families in two apartment blocks were wounded, six of them children. On the Russian side, four workers died in Volgograd's industrial zone. Zelenskyy also repeated his complaint on Friday about speed limits on the US Starshield system — SpaceX's military-grade Starlink — which he says caps Ukrainian drones at 161 km/h (100 mph) across the front line, making it impossible to intercept Russia's fast jet-powered drones. SpaceX told CNN there had been no change in service, and the White House and Pentagon have not responded.

In short: Ukraine answered the diplomatic push with its largest drone barrage in years, Russia answered with strikes on hospitals, homes and power stations, and the talks themselves are now stuck on a new fight — whether Europe gets a seat, and whether Moscow gets to renegotiate the security order that has held since the Cold War. Watch the Dublin summit's decision on a European envoy, and whether the next round of talks gets a date.

Iran hands Washington a seven-day roadmap to end the war — and Trump says he doesn't need a deal

Iran formally submitted a seven-day roadmap to end its war with the United States, its foreign minister said on Friday — and within hours, President Donald Trump appeared to reject it, saying the US "doesn't need a deal with Iran" and redirecting Vice President JD Vance away from the diplomacy entirely. The whiplash came a day after US and Iranian forces exchanged fire in the Strait of Hormuz, in the sharpest direct clash there in this round of the war.

The details of Tehran's offer, as laid out by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi to reporters outside the UN Security Council on Friday, are specific: the plan was conveyed to Washington through Qatar, and the seven-day clock would start as soon as the US accepts it. The first steps would take four to five days, the Strait of Hormuz would reopen on the sixth day, and on day seven, talks with the US would begin on a final deal covering Iran's nuclear programme. In return, Al Jazeera reports, Washington would lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive sanctions on Iranian oil sales, release an estimated $12 billion in frozen Iranian assets, and observe a ceasefire covering the region, including Lebanon and Yemen. "The choice now rests with the United States," Araghchi said, adding that "if there is seriousness on the US side to come to a deal and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, everything is now prepared."

What Washington is saying, though, points the other way. The Wall Street Journal reported later on Friday that Trump had rejected Iran's proposal, and that he is "privately sceptical" Iran would meet his demands, telling aides he expects a renewed bombing campaign after the November midterm elections. On Thursday, Trump wrote on social media: "We don't need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed. We've got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we've got their Strait!" — and he directed Vance, who has played a central role in the diplomacy, to focus "his complete and total attention" on alleged crime and corruption in Democratic-run cities and states. The directive came a day after the Journal reported Vance was considering face-to-face peace talks with a hard-line Iranian security official. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called Iran's demands "absurd" on Tuesday, citing in particular the demand for reparations for the war. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera were sceptical the roadmap changes anything: "Basically nothing has really changed," said Cyrus Schayegh of the Geneva Graduate Institute, arguing Iran wants to keep the leverage it gained during the war while Washington resists returning to the June framework — a memorandum of understanding brokered by Qatar and Pakistan that collapsed within weeks.

The shooting, meanwhile, has not stopped. US Central Command said it launched strikes on Iran on Thursday after three US Navy guided-missile destroyers came under attack from Iranian missiles, drones and small boats in the strait. Iran's Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters accused the US of violating the ceasefire by attacking an Iranian oil tanker and another vessel, and of targeting civilian areas including Qeshm Island. Trump on Thursday appeared to downplay the clash, saying the ceasefire remained in effect, while Iran's state-run Press TV said the situation had gone "back to normal." The exchange followed Iran's military saying four oil tankers were hit by explosions in the strait after ignoring warnings — a claim Reuters said it could not independently verify.

For ordinary people the cost keeps compounding at the pump and the checkout. Brent crude settled Thursday at $95.82 a barrel, up $3.06 or 3.3%, and WTI closed at $88.74, up $2.21 — after the Houthi claims against Saudi energy sites and the strait incidents — though both benchmarks were still down about 2.5% for the week and eased slightly on Friday morning, with Brent around $95.50. US gasoline averaged $3.98 a gallon and diesel $6.16 on November 10, roughly 30% and 65% above a year earlier, according to AAA. Shipping through the strait has been at a near standstill since late February, and Brent is up about 40% compared with before the war.

In short: Tehran put a concrete, dated offer on the table — blockade lifted, $12 billion released, Hormuz open in six days — and Trump answered that America doesn't need a deal at all, pulling his vice president off the file. The one thing both sides agree on is that the shooting continues: US destroyers and Iranian forces traded fire in the strait on Thursday. Watch whether the White House formally responds to the roadmap, and whether the post-midterm bombing campaign Trump reportedly expects actually materialises.

Houthis / Saudi Arabia

No new Houthi attacks overnight — but Pakistan condemned the strikes at the UN and Saudi Arabia talked to China

No new Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia were reported overnight. The fallout from Thursday's claimed strikes continued instead: Pakistan's ambassador to the UN, Syed Haider Shah, condemned the Houthi attacks "in the strongest terms" at the Security Council after members renewed the Yemen sanctions, calling for an immediate and unconditional end to them. And Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan met China's special envoy for Middle East affairs, Zhai Jun, in Riyadh to discuss the Houthi attacks and diplomatic efforts to prevent further escalation. The Houthis' Thursday claims — a drone and a ballistic missile at East-West Pipeline pumping station 7, about 330 km west of Riyadh, plus strikes on Yanbu, an Aramco facility in Riyadh, a cargo plane at Sharurah airport and Khamis Mushait airbase — remain unconfirmed by Riyadh, and the coalition says it intercepted two drones on Thursday. Tech

Nvidia: the interim CEO is the CFO who pushed back on Huang's bets

Reuters profiled Colette Kress, the 59-year-old CFO who took over as Nvidia's interim CEO after Jensen Huang resigned on Thursday. Kress, CFO since September 2013, spent years as the one Nvidia executive willing to check Huang's enormous, often risky ambitions, pushing back as he drove the chipmaker deeper into multibillion-dollar AI investments, according to two people familiar with the matter. She keeps the CFO job while running the company, and whether she keeps the CEO role permanently is undecided — Nvidia executive Ian Buck, who helped launch AI on Nvidia chips and led development of CUDA, is among the names surfacing as a successor. The board has started looking at internal and external candidates with no set timeline. The next real test is earnings on Tuesday, November 17. (Reuters via WNFL Sports · Technology.org)

Courts

Murdaugh retrial: dismissal hearing is today in Lexington

Alex Murdaugh's lawyers are in court this morning in Lexington, South Carolina, asking Judge Debra McCaslin to throw out the original murder indictments for the 2021 killings of his wife and son. The defence argues lead SLED agent David Owen misled the July 2022 grand jury, telling it the spatter on Murdaugh's shirt held Maggie and Paul's DNA eight months after the state's own lab reported no human blood on it. Even if McCaslin grants the long-shot request, it likely won't stop the April 5, 2027 retrial — prosecutors can seek fresh indictments from the Colleton County grand jury, which next sits December 10, and the defence says it won't oppose that. Murdaugh's 2023 convictions and life sentences were thrown out by the South Carolina Supreme Court in May after a court clerk was found to have improperly influenced jurors. (AP via HeadlinesXtra · DeWitt Substack)
