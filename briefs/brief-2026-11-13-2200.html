Trump, Putin and Xi to meet face-to-face for the first time — but Ukraine is off the agenda

The White House confirmed Friday that President Donald Trump, Russian President Vladimir Putin and Chinese President Xi Jinping will hold the first-ever three-way meeting of the leaders of the United States, Russia and China on Thursday, November 19, on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen. The stated agenda is cutting nuclear weapons and the war in Iran. Ukraine, notably, is not on it.

The meeting, announced by White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales, will bring together the leaders of the world's three biggest nuclear powers. According to Reuters, Trump leaves Washington on Monday for a four-day Asia trip — the Philippines first for the ASEAN summit, then Shenzhen for APEC, which runs November 18–19. He will also hold a separate bilateral working lunch with Xi on Thursday, but no one-on-one with Putin is scheduled. Elon Musk is expected to travel with Trump, though he is not expected to attend the trilateral session.

The arms-control backdrop is stark. The last nuclear arms treaty between the U.S. and Russia expired in February, leaving the two largest atomic arsenals uncapped for the first time in more than half a century, while China has been rapidly building up its own stockpile. The White House says Trump will push for reductions, and the meeting is billed as the start of a process rather than a deal-making session — a senior U.S. official told reporters, "We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement." China has never been party to a U.S.-Russia arms treaty, and analysts quoted by Reuters note Beijing is hesitant to engage on disarmament because it holds far fewer weapons than the other two.

The Iran war is also on the agenda, according to U.S. officials. No ceasefire has been in force since one collapsed in early July, and the U.S. naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in place. Ukraine, by contrast, was deliberately left out. "China is not really a part of that track, so it doesn't seem appropriate to have the Ukraine discussion there," one U.S. official said, referring to the separate U.S.-led negotiations with Kyiv and Moscow. Trump is also not expected to raise Ukraine's access to SpaceX's Starlink/StarShield service, which has been a running sore in U.S.-Ukraine relations this week.

Reactions split along familiar lines. The White House frames the gathering as historic diplomacy — three nuclear powers talking directly about reducing arsenals. Analysts are far more cautious: Hoo Tiang Boon of Nanyang Technological University told Reuters the meeting could undercut China's effort at APEC to present itself as a defender of free trade, while other analysts say it may overshadow the summit itself. Critics, including voices quoted by Political.org, warn the format risks legitimizing Putin while the Ukraine war continues and sidelines Kyiv and European allies. For Moscow, the meeting is a coveted chance to posture as a superpower on par with Washington and Beijing; for Beijing, hosting it is a showcase of growing clout. Xi and Putin have met more than 40 times since 2013; Trump and Putin seven times, most recently in Alaska in August 2025.

For ordinary people, the stakes are real even if no deal emerges Thursday. With the last U.S.-Russia treaty gone, there are currently no caps on the world's two biggest nuclear arsenals — the guardrails that defined the last half-century of arms control are simply absent. Any discussion of the Iran war at the same table also feeds directly into oil prices and shipping costs, which have already been climbing through the autumn. And for Ukrainians, the exclusion stings: the war heads into its fifth winter, and Kyiv and European capitals fear decisions about their future being signaled in rooms they are not in. Former U.S. official Edgard Kagan put it bluntly — the key for Putin is signaling that leaders are making decisions on Ukraine in a way Ukrainians and Europeans do not like.

In short: a first-of-its-kind Trump–Putin–Xi sit-down is set for Thursday in Shenzhen, focused on nuclear arms cuts and Iran, with Ukraine explicitly off the agenda and officials themselves warning not to expect an agreement. Watch whether any joint statement or framework emerges, and whether China signals any willingness to join arms limits it has always avoided.

Sources: Reuters via KZ1023, Political.org, Arab News via EuropeSays, Jawawa

Iran threatens "unlimited response" after a day of claims and counter-claims in the Gulf

Iran's Revolutionary Guard raised the temperature again on Friday, declaring its Aerospace Force on "full alert to respond without limits" to any further aggression in the Strait of Hormuz — the sharpest operational threat from Tehran since Supreme Leader Khamenei's October 31 order to senior commanders. The warning followed a day in which both sides claimed victories the other denies, and a gas carrier reportedly hit a mine and exploded in the strait.

The trigger, according to Iran, was a strike on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, a port city on the Strait of Hormuz. Iranian state media said the tower was hit by "enemy fire" but named no attacker; U.S. Central Command has not publicly acknowledged any strike on Sirik. The IRGC says it retaliated by targeting a U.S. early-warning radar at Jabal ad Dukhan in Bahrain. CENTCOM says the reality was different: two Iranian one-way attack drones were launched toward Bahrain and both were shot down by U.S. forces, with no injuries and no damage — and it called the radar-destruction claim false. Bahrain's government had not confirmed any attack on the radar as of Friday evening. No independent third party has verified either account, and the IRGC released no imagery or coordinates for the claimed hit.

Iranian state media piled on further claims on Friday: the IRGC Navy said a liquefied petroleum gas carrier struck a naval mine and exploded while transiting the eastern Strait of Hormuz, after ignoring warnings to stay off an unauthorized route. The Associated Press reports that claim could not be independently verified and there was no immediate report of the incident from UKMTO, the British military agency that monitors incidents at sea. Tehran's hardline Kayhan newspaper added that Iranian forces downed a U.S. MQ-1 Predator drone near Kuhestak in southern Iran — also unverified, with no U.S. confirmation. The IRGC's commander-in-chief, Ahmad Vahidi, separately warned that "the slightest aggression" would draw "devastating and painful strikes."

The numbers behind the standoff keep climbing. The Joint Maritime Information Center counted 181 confirmed maritime incidents since March 1, including four in its latest reporting window, and rates the threat level in the strait as severe, with deliberate attacks highly likely. UKMTO's weekly report through November 10 put commercial transits roughly 65 percent below pre-conflict levels. U.S.-facilitated transits totaled 135 between November 8 and 11, against a historical average of about 138 vessels a day. Lloyd's List, cited in earlier reporting, counted 37 vessel attacks in October — a record monthly total — with more than 14 already in November.

The two sides are telling very different stories about where this is heading. Tehran frames its actions as defensive retaliation for attacks on its own infrastructure, and insists the radar strike was a "serious warning" to stop aerial provocations. Washington's line, via CENTCOM, is that the drones were intercepted, nothing was hit, and the ceasefire Trump described earlier this week remains in effect. The broader context cuts against calm: Trump declared this week that "we don't need a deal with Iran" and pulled Vice President Vance off the negotiations file, while a third U.S. carrier strike group is en route to the region and Iran has unveiled an underground missile production site.

For people in the region, the practical effects are already visible: shipping through the world's most important oil chokepoint is running at about a third of normal volume, which is what keeps oil prices elevated and pushes up fuel and transport costs far beyond the Gulf. U.S. personnel at Naval Support Activity Bahrain — headquarters of the Fifth Fleet — are the direct target of these drone launches, and the U.S. has already issued a rare coordinated security alert for citizens across 12 Middle Eastern countries. Gulf carriers and insurers are pricing in a strait that could shut with little notice.

In short: Friday brought a new round of unverified Iranian claims — a destroyed U.S. radar, a downed Predator drone, a mined gas carrier — all denied or unconfirmed by the U.S., plus Tehran's bluntest threat yet of an "unlimited response." Nothing has been independently confirmed, but with shipping already down 65 percent and 181 maritime incidents since March, the Gulf is one miscalculation away from a much worse week. Watch for any UKMTO confirmation of the gas carrier incident and whether the U.S. responds publicly to the Sirik strike.

Sources: House of Saud, Military.com / AP, Reuters via KTWB, EL7.AI / JMIC, Kayhan (Iranian state media)

Houthis claim new missile strikes on Saudi forces in Jazan

Houthis say they fired missiles at Saudi forces in Jazan — Yemen's Houthis said Friday they fired two ballistic missiles at what they described as concentrations of Saudi forces in Al-Tuwal in Jazan province, hours after Saudi civil defence issued an all-clear for the province. The group also claimed it attacked Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport and a pumping station on Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline. There was no immediate Saudi confirmation of any of the claims. Jazan is home to a Saudi Aramco refinery with a capacity of 400,000 barrels of crude a day, and southern Saudi Arabia has been hit repeatedly in recent weeks. Why it matters: the claims, if real, would show the Houthis still reaching deep into Saudi territory despite weeks of coalition strikes — but with no Saudi confirmation, treat them as claims, not confirmed hits. Source: Reuters via FroggyWeb

Cybersecurity

SCKit worm rides ScreenConnect into MSPs — 288 computers, 48 organizations — A worm-like toolkit called SCKit has been spreading through rogue ScreenConnect clients, hitting at least 288 computers across 48 organizations since November 11. Attackers used cloud-hosted ScreenConnect panels to push commands that installed attacker-controlled clients, built to spread from a customer's device up the support session to the technician's machine and onward into other managed networks. Huntress, which spotted the campaign, says the malware tries to exploit CVE-2026-84869 during support sessions. ConnectWise says the access came through abuse of a third-party extension that bypassed authentication. Patch ScreenConnect to version 26.6.5 or later. Why it matters: this is the second remote-management tool hit this week — N-central was compromised the same way, hours after a public exploit — and MSPs are the soft underbelly because one technician's machine can open dozens of client networks. Sources: PwnSpectrum, NinjaLabz, CVETodo

FBI and DOJ take down North Korea's FaceStealer crypto scheme — $400M+ stolen via fake video calls — North Korean actors operating as Sapphire Sleet / BlueNoroff / APT38 stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over 18 months by hijacking Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord accounts and inviting targets to video meetings on typosquatted fake Zoom or Teams sites, replaying stolen webcam video to impersonate real people. The FBI issued a FLASH alert and the Justice Department seized the scheme's cloud infrastructure, along with more than $1.2 billion tied to the Binance theft attributed to TraderTraitor, and over 900 victim videos. Why it matters: it is one of the largest crypto-theft takedowns ever, and the social-engineering trick — a "colleague" on a video call who is not who they seem — works on anyone, not just crypto firms. Sources: NinjaLabz, CVETodo

Europol's Operation Endgame seizes Gootloader, CastleRAT and StealC infrastructure — Europol and partners from nine countries seized hundreds of servers and domains behind the Gootloader, CastleRAT and StealC operations. The DOJ cited at least 44 U.S. Gootloader ransomware victims. German investigators traced the rebuilt StealC network to a bulletproof hosting provider the FBI seized in October. Why it matters: StealC has now been taken down twice in one year — expect the stealer crews to rebuild again, so refresh blocklists rather than trusting last month's. Source: NinjaLabz

Microsoft Exchange flaw reportedly exploited in the wild — A report of in-the-wild exploitation of CVE-2026-118207, an improper input validation flaw in Microsoft Exchange Server Subscription Edition (CVSS 8.8), surfaced today. Single source so far — treat as reported, not confirmed, and patch Exchange promptly if you run it. Source: CVETodo

Check Point attack follow-up: wiper hit VMware hosts in Israel-focused first wave — New detail on the Check Point gateway attacks added to CISA's KEV catalog on Thursday: in the first wave from October 30, aimed mostly at Israeli organizations, attackers ran a wiper called destroy_v6 on VMware ESXi hosts after gaining root via the PHP2Shell + CVE-2026-123039 chain. The wiper note is in Hebrew and politically themed, rejecting any ransom negotiation. A second, wider wave from November 2 hit several countries and sectors with no destructive activity seen so far. Check Point says a couple of dozen gateways were compromised. The fix is the November Jumbo Hotfix. Source: Reef Sentinel

This follows the Gemini 4 Argon release last month. Why it matters: Google is now shipping a new Gemini 4 variant roughly monthly, and the pricing undercuts most rivals for agent workloads.
