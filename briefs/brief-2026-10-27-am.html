World escalation

A truce begins in Oleshky — while Kyiv takes another night of missiles

A rare local ceasefire has begun in the Russian-occupied town of Oleshky, even as Russia pounded Kyiv and other Ukrainian cities overnight. The two facts sitting side by side are the story: a humanitarian pause in one town, and no pause anywhere else.

Reuters reports that both sides confirmed the Oleshky ceasefire on Tuesday morning. Russia's defence ministry said it stopped shelling toward the Ukrainian-held city of Kherson from 4 a.m. Moscow time (0100 GMT), and Ukraine's human rights commissioner Dmytro Lubinets confirmed on Facebook that "the Ukrainian side is honouring its commitments." The deal, coordinated with US First Lady Melania Trump and Russia's human rights commissioner Yana Lantratova, is meant to let food, water and medicine into a town where up to 2,000 civilians remain, many elderly or with limited mobility, and to prepare evacuations. A wider humanitarian operation is planned to start on October 29, according to a summary of the arrangement. The ceasefire covers only the Oleshky area — it is not a wider truce, and neither side has said who monitors it.

At the same time, Reuters reports new strikes on Tuesday hit Kyiv's Southern Bridge and subway infrastructure, forcing traffic to halt, while a Reuters witness heard explosions. Ukraine's Air Force said the overnight assault, running from 6 p.m. Monday, used Iskander-M and S-400 ballistic missiles plus 280 strike drones, with Kyiv the main direction of attack. By 9 a.m., air defences had destroyed or suppressed 257 targets — two ballistic missiles and 255 drones — with hits recorded at 25 locations and debris falling at 16 more. President Volodymyr Zelensky said 16 ballistic missiles were launched in total, the injured from the university-campus strike had risen to 12, and strikes also hit the Kyiv, Vinnytsia, Zaporizhzhia, Dnipro, Kharkiv, Odesa, Chernihiv, Sumy, Donetsk, Kherson and Chernivtsi regions.

The two sides describe the Kyiv strikes in entirely different terms. Zelensky called the bridge strike "a serious escalation in Russia's operation against our logistics" and renewed his attack on delays to Patriot and NASAMS interceptor deliveries, saying "every such delay means another Russian strike on one facility or another." Moscow's defence ministry said it hit transport infrastructure in Kyiv, a logistics hub in the Kharkiv region, and industrial facilities and cargo vessels it said were put to military use. Reuters notes it could not independently verify the reports, and both sides deny deliberately targeting civilians. Casualty figures are still moving: Reuters' Tuesday wire puts the Kyiv toll since Monday evening at at least one killed and 15 injured, including students in a dormitory, while Zelensky's Tuesday statement put the injured at 12; people were also killed and injured in Kharkiv and Pavlohrad, and a drone attack killed a man in the Russian city of Bryansk.

For ordinary people, the practical effects are immediate and concrete. Kyiv commuters lost a main Dnipro crossing and metro service on the bridge section on Tuesday morning, on top of a night of sirens and explosions; in Oleshky, the question is whether aid trucks and evacuation routes actually move in the coming days, after years in which mined roads and drone attacks made leaving dangerous. The truce's first test is delivery, not declaration.

In short: Oleshky's humanitarian pause is real and confirmed by both sides, but it is narrow, unmonitored in any published detail, and surrounded by a war that escalated overnight — with Kyiv's bridge and metro hit, and Ukraine's parliament voting to extend the war footing into next year.

Israelis vote in their first election since October 7 — and neither bloc looks set to win it

Israelis are voting Tuesday in a parliamentary election that is, above all, a verdict on Benjamin Netanyahu's leadership after three years of war — and one that, on the final polls, neither side is projected to win outright.

Polls opened at 7 a.m. local time for about 7.3 million eligible voters choosing the 120-seat Knesset, with voting running until 10 p.m. and exit polls expected shortly after. The contest pits Netanyahu's Likud and its right-wing, far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies against an opposition alliance led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot and his Yashar party. Final television-network polls put Yashar ahead of Likud, roughly 22–23 seats to 19–21, but Netanyahu's bloc at about 50 seats and the main opposition bloc at 52–56 — both short of the 61 needed for a majority, with Arab-led parties projected at 14–16 seats and therefore potentially decisive in coalition talks.

The two campaigns are arguing about different things, and that is the point of disagreement. Eisenkot, whose son died in Gaza in December 2023 and who quit the wartime cabinet in June 2024, opened election day by invoking the October 7, 2023 Hamas massacre, posting: "Today we have an opportunity to put a slip in the ballot box, to replace the government of October 7th." His bloc is demanding accountability for the failures around the attacks and an independent commission of inquiry, and casting Netanyahu as dangerous for Israel's security. Netanyahu, by contrast, is campaigning on his wartime leadership, his alliance with President Trump — who has endorsed him — and a strong-man image, with Likud billboards carrying the slogan "Right or Palestine." Eisenkot counters that Trump would work with any leader voters choose. On policy, the gap is narrower than the rhetoric: Eisenkot shares some of Netanyahu's positions on the conflict, and the sharpest divides are over accountability, ultra-Orthodox military-service exemptions, and who should answer for October 7.

For ordinary Israelis, the stakes are who governs through the unresolved confrontation with Iran, the aftermath of the Gaza war, and the corruption trial still hanging over Netanyahu — plus an independent UN inquiry report reaffirming allegations of grave abuses in Gaza, issued the day before voting. If neither bloc reaches 61, coalition negotiations with smaller and Arab parties will decide the government, and Netanyahu could remain as caretaker if no coalition forms.

In short: a close, high-stakes vote with no projected winner — the result will be decided less by who gets the most votes than by who can build 61 seats afterwards, with exit polls due Tuesday night local time.

Tech products

Apple launches the M6 MacBook Pro and iMac — a day before its touchscreen Mac event

Apple on Tuesday unveiled a new 14-inch MacBook Pro and iMac powered by its M6 chip, pitching faster on-device AI — the second day of a product rollout that peaks Wednesday with its "Pro and tell" event in Los Angeles.

Reuters reports the 14-inch MacBook Pro starts at $1,999 and the iMac at $1,499, with pre-orders opening Wednesday in 35 countries and regions and availability starting November 6. Apple says the M6 MacBook Pro delivers up to 30% faster graphics and up to 25% faster AI performance than its M5 predecessor, with up to 24 hours of battery life — the longest ever in a Mac — while the new iMac delivers up to 5x faster on-device AI performance than the previous M4 model. Pocket-lint adds the context that matters for buyers: the MacBook Pro jump is modest because the 14-inch model already ran M5, while the iMac jump is bigger because Apple last refreshed it in 2024 with M4. MacRumors adds the specs: a 12-core CPU and 12-core GPU, Wi-Fi 7, 16GB of unified memory and 1TB of storage as standard on the MacBook Pro, in Space Black and Silver, configurable up to 32GB and 4TB.

The timing is the other half of the story. The launches come a day before Apple's October 28 event, which Bloomberg has reported will feature Apple's first touchscreen MacBook Pro — a product Apple executives once publicly dismissed, with Steve Jobs calling touchscreen laptops "ergonomically terrible" and Tim Cook comparing a Mac-iPad merge to "combining a toaster and a refrigerator." Apple is expected to frame touch as a secondary input — scrolling, zooming, panning — with keyboard and trackpad remaining primary. The M6 refresh is deliberately limited to the 14-inch MacBook Pro, Pocket-lint notes, because the touchscreen model is expected to take the spotlight tomorrow.

For buyers, the practical read is simple: if you want a conventional MacBook Pro or an iMac, the M6 models are the new baseline from November 6; if you want a touchscreen Mac, wait one more day before deciding, because tomorrow's announcement may change the lineup and pricing picture.

In short: a solid, AI-pitched M6 refresh for the 14-inch MacBook Pro and iMac, priced at $1,999 and $1,499, landing November 6 — and clearly staged as the warm-up act for Wednesday's touchscreen Mac reveal.

Nvidia puts $3 billion into Meta's giant AI fundraising as the two deepen their Muse partnership

Nvidia and Meta on Tuesday deepened their partnership around Meta's Muse AI assistant and models, with Nvidia investing $3 billion in Meta's equity offering — a chipmaker buying into its biggest customer's AI buildout.

Reuters reports the two companies will work more closely to improve Muse's training and inference performance and reduce costs, with Muse — known internally as Hatch — central to CEO Mark Zuckerberg's plan to deliver "personal superintelligence" to the billions of people who use Meta's services daily. Dow Jones adds that Meta intends to increase deployment of Nvidia infrastructure, including Vera Rubin and Vera CPUs, for next-generation AI agents, and that the companies may also work together on monitoring agent behavior. The deal sits inside Meta's enormous fundraising: Meta increased its equity offering to $97.5 billion, the largest in its history, to bankroll AI infrastructure spending, after initially announcing roughly $35 billion in tranches and then upsizing. Nvidia's $3 billion is a slice of that, not a takeover — no purchase price, share count or ownership percentage was disclosed.

The two sides frame the deal differently, and both framings matter. For Meta, it is about securing compute and credibility for an agent — modeled on the open-source OpenClaw — that can access a user's email, calendar, payments, health, shopping and smart-home apps, with users able to revoke access at any time. For Nvidia, it is about locking its Vera Rubin platform, Spectrum-X Ethernet and BlueField-4 networking into Meta's data centers, plus a possible role for its OpenShell framework and Sentry agent-monitoring tool in how Muse agents are rolled out safely. Critics of this kind of circular AI financing — a supplier funding its customer's spending — will note the structure; supporters will note Meta is also raising tens of billions from public markets, not just from Nvidia.

For ordinary users, the near-term effect is not a new product but a bet: Meta's AI assistant gets more Nvidia hardware behind it, and the companies are explicitly tying the partnership to agents that act across your apps — which makes the access controls and monitoring they mention worth watching as closely as the speed claims.

In short: Nvidia buys $3 billion into Meta's record $97.5 billion raise, Meta buys deeper into Nvidia's Vera Rubin stack, and Muse — Meta's agent bet — is the product both sides are really investing in.

Sources: Reuters via KWSN, October 27; Dow Jones via Morningstar, October 27; TradingKey, October 27
