World escalation

Aid finally reaches Oleshky — while Russia hits Kyiv, Dnipro and Odesa overnight

The war's strangest contrast played out overnight: in the Russian-held town of Oleshky, Ukrainian and Russian human-rights commissioners stood side by side as a long-awaited aid convoy arrived — while across the rest of Ukraine, Russia launched a massive overnight missile-and-drone strike on energy infrastructure, killing at least one person in Kyiv and leaving Dnipro's power system struggling after Thursday's deadly platform attack.

Reuters reports that residents of Oleshky were due to receive food and medical supplies on Friday and be allowed to leave if they wish, under a local ceasefire brokered by US First Lady Melania Trump that has now held for more than three days. Russian commissioner Yana Lantratova and Ukrainian commissioner Dmytro Lubinets appeared together on Friday to update reporters. "The aid was assembled based on the specific needs reported to us; it includes food, hygiene products, essential medical supplies, fuel – everything the people require," Lantratova said. Lubinets said everyone in need would get help, including those who wished to leave — for Ukraine or for Russia. A fleet of 25 buses with room for 750 people, 50 medical personnel, ambulances for people with limited mobility, and even a bus with pet cages stood ready, according to Reuters and Lubinets' own account. The convoy had been assembled in Armiansk, about 85 km south of Oleshky, and departed Friday morning for the town's central hospital. The demining that made it possible cost lives: Lantratova said a sapper and one other person were killed during clearance operations.

At the same time, Ukraine's Air Force reported that from 6 p.m. Thursday, Russia attacked with Iskander-M and S-400 ballistic missiles, KN-23 missiles and four Kh-59/69 guided aviation missiles, plus 113 strike drones — 83 of them jet-powered. Air defences downed 99 targets, including 96 drones, but hits were recorded at 17 locations and debris fell at five more, according to Kyiv24 and 112.ua, citing the Air Force. The main directions were Kyiv, Poltava and Dnipropetrovsk regions, but Prime Minister Serhiy Korytskyi said Kyiv, Chernihiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Kharkiv, Poltava, Donetsk and Volyn regions were all under attack, with energy infrastructure the main target. In Kyiv, one person was killed and at least 12 residential buildings damaged across the capital, Kyiv region and Kharkiv region; four more people were injured in Kyiv, Donetsk, Kharkiv and Volyn. In Poltava, energy infrastructure and railway facilities were hit. Russia's Defence Ministry claimed a "massive" strike on drone-production and storage plants, the energy facilities powering them, and Odesa-region port infrastructure and vessels — claims that, as usual, came without named sites, numbers or imagery and could not be independently verified. Moscow also claimed it shot down 1,043 Ukrainian drones over Russian regions overnight, another unverified figure.

Caption: Kyiv24's lead image for its report on the overnight Russian strike on Kyiv and seven Ukrainian regions. It matters because the strike's main target, according to Ukraine's prime minister, was energy infrastructure — the same winter-war pattern that left hundreds of thousands in Dnipro without power on Thursday.

The two sides' framing of the wider war has not moved, even as they cooperated in Oleshky. Reuters notes Moscow still hopes President Trump can pressure Kyiv into accepting a deal meeting many of Russia's conditions, including full Russian control of the Donbas and a bar on Ukraine joining NATO. Kyiv, Reuters reports, wants US intelligence-sharing to continue, along with access to Elon Musk's recently disrupted Starshield battlefield communications system and continued flows of European-bought US hardware, including Patriot interceptors. On the next round of US-mediated talks, the picture clouded further: Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov linked the timing of the Abu Dhabi round to the Middle East conflict and the battlefield situation, according to Reuters via Zark News — putting Zelensky's hoped-for November 6–10 window in doubt, with Moscow still proposing no specific date. Earlier rounds have stalled over eastern-Ukrainian territory and security guarantees.

For ordinary people, the human ledger is stark. In Oleshky, a town seized in the first days of the 2022 invasion and badly flooded after the Kakhovka dam destruction in 2023, residents have described surviving on a cup of rice or buckwheat a day, travelling mine-strewn roads — one known as the "road of death" — just for food and water, according to Reuters. Friday's convoy is the first relief of its kind in four years and eight months of war. In Dnipro, the toll from Thursday's railway-platform strike rose to 15 after a 40-year-old man died in hospital, and the city's mayor said Friday morning the power system could not withstand the attacks, urging residents to stay home as electric transport stopped running. The Oleshky ceasefire runs only until the evening of November 1, and evacuations onward — reportedly via Anapa in Russia to Gomel in Belarus on October 31 — are still to be confirmed by Kyiv.

In short: one town got its first aid in years because both sides agreed to stop shooting there for six days, while everywhere else the war escalated overnight — 113 drones and ballistic missiles at energy targets, one dead in Kyiv, Dnipro's grid buckling, and the next peace round drifting. Friday's convoy and Saturday's planned evacuations are the test of whether the Oleshky exception means anything beyond itself.

A new kind of tanker attack in Hormuz — limpet mines on a moving ship — as Houthi oil claims push Brent toward $117

A tanker transiting the Strait of Hormuz was hit by explosive devices attached to its hull by a small boat on Thursday night, in the first successful limpet-mine attack in the waterway since the US war with Iran began in February — and it landed on the same day Yemen's Houthis claimed strikes on Saudi Arabia's biggest refineries, sending Brent crude toward $117 a barrel.

Reuters, via gCaptain, reports that a small boat attached two limpet mines to the hull of the Panama-flagged tanker Louise 1, causing a fire in its engine room and temporarily disabling the vessel. The incident happened Thursday about 25 nautical miles northeast of Oman's Khasab, according to the British navy's United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO), which said the crew was safe, there was no environmental impact, the fire was extinguished and the vessel was continuing to its next port of call. Three maritime security sources identified the ship as the Louise 1; UKMTO itself did not name it. No one has been officially blamed. A September limpet-mine attempt in the area failed to detonate, two sources said, making this the first successful use of the method in this war. Greece-based maritime risk group Marisks warned that "the apparent ability of a small craft to approach a transiting tanker, deploy explosive devices and withdraw presents a significant security concern, particularly in the Strait's congested shipping lanes," calling the method "cheap, low tech and very efficient."

Iran's account differs. Iran's Army Navy claimed a tanker caught fire after striking a naval mine in a restricted area east of the strait, and the IRGC claimed three — later four — tankers hit mines on an unauthorised southern route. UKMTO's verified account describes attached explosives, not a mine strike, and the two versions cannot both describe the same event in the same way. Separately, the Louise 1 has its own history: Reuters notes Ukrainian forces hit it in July with naval drones in the Black Sea over what Ukraine's SBU said was its role transporting Russian oil as part of the so-called shadow fleet.

The Hormuz attack came hours after the Houthi claims already covered in Thursday night's brief — six targets including Ras Tanura, Abqaiq and Rabigh refineries, Riyadh and Dammam airports and a Najran military headquarters — with the coalition confirming only two Riyadh intercepts. Friday's coverage adds no Saudi or Aramco confirmation of damage to any refinery, but Reuters' oil-market reporting confirms the market moved anyway: Brent rose 86 cents to $115.74 in early Friday trading and later hit $117, on track for a fourth monthly gain, with traders bracing for a possible post-midterms escalation in the US-Iran conflict.

For ordinary people, the stakes are fuel prices and shipping safety, not just geopolitics. Hormuz is the key sea outlet for Gulf crude, and a method that lets a small boat quietly attach explosives to a moving tanker in congested lanes is harder to defend against than a missile or drone that radar can see. The Louise 1 crew escaped unharmed and no oil spilled this time — but the incident, plus the unconfirmed refinery claims, is exactly the kind of supply-risk news that feeds through to petrol and diesel prices within weeks.

In short: nobody is officially blamed for the limpet-mine attack, Iran's mine story conflicts with UKMTO's attached-explosives account, and no refinery damage is confirmed — yet oil is up toward $117 on the risk alone. Watch for any Aramco statement, satellite imagery, or a second limpet incident, which would turn a one-off into a tactic.

Cybersecurity

Binance suffers what analysts call the biggest crypto heist ever — ~$6.3 billion impact, ~$3 billion frozen

Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, said a security breach had an estimated $6.3 billion impact, which analysts said could amount to the biggest crypto heist on record — roughly four times the Bybit theft of 2025, according to Chainalysis, which put the amount actually stolen at $5.912 billion at the time of theft.

Reuters reports Binance worked with partners to freeze about $3 billion of affected assets so far, with no update on the remaining ~$3.3 billion. Co-founder Yi He said the company would cover "all hacking losses" from its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and company funds, and Binance said user funds remain safe. The exchange temporarily suspended withdrawals (deposits continued normally, per later updates) and said it expects withdrawals to resume within about 45–48 hours. Tether froze nearly $1.57 billion, Circle more than $800 million, Ripple roughly $200 million, and World Liberty Financial froze funds with custodian BitGo.

What happened is still developing, and Binance's own account shifted during Friday. Its initial investigation pointed to a compromised laptop belonging to a DevOps engineer, which let an attacker access a service container and submit unauthorised withdrawal transactions. By Friday morning, Binance said investigators had uncovered "additional points of breach," and co-CEO Richard Teng said the attacker "did not simply use one engineer's permissions" — so the laptop story is not the whole attack. Binance said it found no evidence of wallet private-key leaks, and analysts suspect North Korea's TraderTraitor operation, though that attribution is unconfirmed. Bitcoin fell to a six-week low around $76,470, down about 3%, with ether down more than 3%.

For ordinary people: if you hold crypto on Binance, withdrawals are paused while the exchange rebuilds its withdrawal service — Binance says user funds are covered, but expect delays and a detailed post-mortem, not instant clarity. The bigger lesson is the one analysts flagged: a single compromised employee laptop apparently opened a path to billions, which is why the "additional breach points" finding matters more than the first explanation.

Sources: Reuters via BusinessWorld, Reuters factbox via WNCY, TradingView live updates, The Hindu BusinessLine

  • TeamViewer patches three high-severity flaws — update to 15.83: CVE-2026-111585 (CVSS 8.8, integer overflow in remote-control screen processing) and CVE-2026-92372 (CVSS 8.8, out-of-bounds write in the screen-image decoder) could let a malicious or compromised host execute code on the support technician's (viewer's) machine — the attack runs the wrong way round from the usual remote-access threat. CVE-2026-92374 (7.8, macOS) is a local privilege-escalation via a TOCTOU/symlink flaw in the privileged helper. TeamViewer says it was not aware of any of these being exploited in the wild when it published bulletin TV-2026-1011 on October 27. If you use TeamViewer to support others, patch before connecting to untrusted hosts. Source: SecurityWeek
  • Drupal Commerce Core flaw actively exploited — update and audit orders: CVE-2026-118539, a "highly critical" (21/25 on Drupal's scale) missing-authorisation flaw in Commerce Core, is being actively exploited in the wild, Drupal warned. Unauthenticated attackers can bypass protections on certain routes and entity fields, with possible financial impact for store owners. Fix: Commerce Core 3.3.13 (3.x) or 8.x-2.41 (2.x) — updating Drupal core alone does not fix it — then audit orders for inconsistencies. The module is used on 42,000+ sites. Drupal has not said how many sites were hit or whether it was exploited as a zero-day. Source: The Hacker News, SecurityWeek
  • Deadlines landing this weekend — reminder, not new: Federal patch deadlines for GitLab's CVSS 10.0 CVE-2026-97229 and Cisco ASA/FTD CVE-2026-76418 both fall Sunday, November 1; Linux kernel "Fragnesia" CVE-2026-46300 falls Saturday, October 31. The GitLab fix lesson from the night brief still stands: verify the bundled-Ruby fix for your install type, not just the GitLab version.
