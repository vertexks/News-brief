Evening brief — Thursday, November 19, 2026

Putin rules out any ceasefire after Kerch Bridge hit, as Berlin allies rally behind Kyiv

Vladimir Putin said on Thursday he will not agree to a ceasefire or any deal to stop hitting energy infrastructure, after Ukraine struck the Kerch Bridge — the only direct link between Russia and occupied Crimea — for the third time in four days. Hours later, allies gathered in Berlin to back Kyiv, announcing a prisoner swap for next week and pledging billions more in support.

The overnight strike hit the bridge's rail section with Ukrainian-made Neptune missiles, according to Ukraine's Defence Ministry, halting train traffic while Russian authorities assessed the damage. Rail service resumed on Thursday afternoon, the Moscow-appointed head of Crimea, Sergei Aksyonov, said. The road section was already out of action after a large section of road deck and an arch collapsed in Tuesday's strike. Ukraine says Russia will now have to reroute fuel and ammunition supplies overland, where "our drones are waiting for the cargo," its Defence Ministry said. Russia's Defence Ministry said its air defences intercepted 53 Ukrainian drones over 10 Russian regions overnight, and Crimea's occupation authorities announced free Kerch–Kavkaz ferries from November 20 to replace the crossing.

Putin, speaking to reporters in Shenzhen after his trilateral meeting with Donald Trump and Xi Jinping, was blunt. "After the strike on the Crimean Bridge, we will not be agreeing" to a cease-fire or any agreement "to not hit the energy infrastructure," he said, according to the New York Post. "We will respond to these attacks, and I want everyone to be clear about that," he told reporters, the Associated Press reports. He also claimed the bridge was "exclusively civilian" infrastructure and said he had ordered it closed to the transport of military hardware or ammunition — even as the AP notes the 19-kilometre bridge is a key route for military and civilian supplies to Crimea, which Russia uses to support frontline troops.

Russia answered overnight with anti-ship missiles and 98 drones at Ukraine, with hits recorded at 18 locations, according to Ukrainian authorities cited by the AP. At least 10 people were reported wounded and damage was recorded in 12 regions, Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi said. The strikes came a day after daytime drone attacks on Kyiv, including one that crashed near the Dutch Embassy.

The two sides are arguing over the same logic from opposite ends. Kyiv says the bridge and refinery strikes are retaliation for Russia's nightly campaign against Ukrainian power plants and cities, and will continue until Russia stops hitting Ukrainian infrastructure. Moscow calls the bridge civilian and vows retaliation, while its own energy strikes continue. Washington is split from its European allies on the strategy: at Tuesday's Ukraine Defense Contact Group meeting in Brussels, Pentagon policy chief Elbridge Colby argued the deep strikes have not shortened the war and hardened Russian resolve — a position Germany and Britain rejected the same day, and which Berlin's summit effectively overruled by doubling down on support.

In Berlin, the Coalition of the Willing — more than 40 countries — met with President Zelenskyy and US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said "a Yalta-style format will not bring about peace" and that any negotiations must include both Ukraine and Europe, a direct answer to the Shenzhen summit's silence on Ukraine. NATO allies reaffirmed €70 billion in military assistance for 2026, with comparable support planned for 2027, and the European Commission's Ursula von der Leyen cited a new €45 billion loan and a 22nd sanctions package on the way. Kushner announced a new prisoner exchange in the week of November 23, covering more than 1,000 prisoners of war, after talks resumed in Abu Dhabi under US mediation. He also said Washington had brought Russia around to the concept of Article 5-style security guarantees for Ukraine — though no Russian official has been quoted accepting that, and a proposed four-party format bringing together Ukraine, Europe, the US and Russia has no agreed date, agenda or location. Trump joined the Berlin meeting by phone and, according to Politico, asked why allies keep spending millions on military support for Kyiv.

For people on the ground, the winter war is now a daily cycle with no safe hours. In Kyiv, homes are getting only around four hours of electricity a day and daytime drone attacks have removed the idea of a safe part of the day. In Crimea, losing the bridge's rail link — even temporarily — squeezes supplies to a peninsula already under a state of emergency over power outages and fuel shortages, with ferries now the fallback.

In short: Ukraine's third bridge strike in four days killed any talk of an energy ceasefire — Putin said so himself in Shenzhen — while Berlin answered with more money, more drones and a prisoner swap next week. The diplomacy is moving, but the two sides are further apart on a ceasefire than they were a week ago. Watch whether Russia's promised retaliation materialises, and whether the prisoner swap goes ahead as announced.

*Sources: Associated Press via WPTF, New York Post, Pollar, tut0ugh.news, The Library Estate*

Houthi missile hits Riyadh's tallest tower as Saudi Arabia braces for a six-month Yemen war

A suspected Houthi missile struck the PIF Tower in Riyadh early on Thursday — the kingdom's tallest skyscraper and headquarters of its sovereign wealth fund — blowing out windows across seven to eight floors and bending parts of its metal facade. It was the second time in less than two weeks that a Houthi attack damaged a landmark in the capital's financial district, and it came as Reuters reported Saudi war planners now expect the Yemen campaign to last up to six months, well into 2026.

Residents of the Saudi capital were woken by a loud boom in the early hours of Thursday. Entrances to the King Abdullah Financial District were closed and people were evacuated, with ambulances and fire trucks at the scene; no injuries were reported. Saudi Arabia's civil defence said a Houthi attack on a tower in the financial centre caused material damage to several floors and nearby buildings, and state news agency SPA published a photograph showing the PIF Tower, though officials did not name the building. Three sources familiar with the matter told Reuters the 80-storey tower — the tallest in the financial district, housing the Public Investment Fund, which manages more than $900 billion in assets — had been hit by an incoming Houthi missile. The Saudi-led coalition said it had "dealt with a missile threat" launched towards Riyadh, adding that its initial assessment showed debris had fallen and material damage had been reported. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the group had targeted Riyadh's international airport and the financial district with cruise missiles, as well as Khamis Mushait and Abha in the south, though the Houthis did not immediately confirm the PIF Tower specifically.

The strike is the second hit on a financial-district landmark in eleven days, after a November 8 attack damaged state oil giant Aramco's building. The Houthis have been targeting Saudi airports and oil infrastructure since Yemen's frozen civil war reignited in July, and on the ground in Yemen they have seized swathes of territory from the Saudi-backed government, forcing a major retreat from the Bab el-Mandeb coast at the weekend to defend Aden.

The two sides read the war very differently. Riyadh, according to two Western officials briefed by the Saudis and cited by Reuters, now expects the campaign against the Houthis to last up to six months into 2027, with the goal of achieving territorial gains for Yemen's internationally recognised government before entering any negotiations — a calculation that subordinates diplomacy entirely to battlefield outcomes. A third source familiar with the Saudi plans confirmed the accounts. The same reporting says US offensive support — intelligence sharing, precision-guided munitions and targeting assistance that the Trump administration has so far withheld — could compress the ground campaign from months to weeks, but no such authorisation has been granted. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman met US Central Command chief Admiral Brad Cooper in Riyadh on Wednesday, Cooper's third publicly reported visit since the Yemen conflict resumed. The Houthis, meanwhile, say they will continue under a principle of "siege for siege, airport for airport, and escalation for escalation," and have warned airlines and shipping companies to avoid Saudi airports and waters.

For ordinary people, the war is now hitting the symbols of Saudi Arabia's economic ambitions. The King Abdullah Financial District — home to the stock exchange and global firms such as Goldman Sachs — was closed to visitors after the attack, and the strike is a fresh blow to Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's plans to develop and diversify the economy, Reuters notes. In Yemen, the fighting grinds on around Taiz, where authorities accuse the Houthis of blocking medicine shipments, and displacement continues. In the Gulf, no new attacks were reported on Thursday beyond Wednesday's incidents, and oil steadied — Brent around $92 a barrel and WTI around $82–83 — as tanker tracking showed flows out of the Persian Gulf still below pre-war levels, with more than half of Gulf tankers now moving oil via ship-to-ship transfers in the Gulf of Oman, according to Vortexa.

In short: the Houthis can now hit the heart of Riyadh's financial district, and Saudi Arabia is settling in for a long war rather than a quick campaign — up to six months, by its own planners' reckoning, unless Washington agrees to help on offence. Watch whether the US authorises the offensive support Riyadh wants, and whether Houthi forces keep closing on Aden.

Short items

Anthropic closed up 17% on its first day as a public company

Anthropic's shares (Nasdaq: ANT) closed at $97.17 on Thursday, up about 17% on the $83 IPO price, after opening at $111.50 — a 34% premium — and peaking at $115.10 just before noon. The stock slipped as low as $92.72 during the day and was trading around $95.60 by mid-afternoon. More than 846 million shares changed hands, ahead of the 522 million traded on SpaceX's opening day in June, making ANT the most actively traded name on the Nasdaq. At the $111.50 open, Reuters put Anthropic's market value at about $2 trillion. The offering raised about $81.3 billion, the largest IPO on record, and underwriters have the option to buy up to 147 million more shares, which would take the total to $93.5 billion.

Nvidia's morning rebound reversed — closed down 3.3% at $145.98

Nvidia fell 3.30% on Thursday to close at $145.98, after opening at $152.92 and touching $158.13 intraday — a sixth straight down session, roughly 40% below its October record of $243.37. Volume hit 311.8 million shares, about 95% above its three-month average. The slide continued despite the record $115.8 billion quarter reported Tuesday. New today: UBS cut its price target to $220 from $240 — its second cut in three days — and securities firm Hagens Berman updated its Nvidia class-action investigation, with an expanded class period of December 19, 2024 to November 12, 2026 and a January 8, 2027 lead-plaintiff deadline. Moody's also cut its outlook on Nvidia to stable from positive.

Google's Gemini can now work through Mac tasks in the background

Google added computer-use to Gemini for Mac: the app can now use apps, files and websites to complete multi-step tasks in a background window while you work in the foreground, with a live preview and the ability to pause it and approve key actions. It runs on Apple-silicon Macs on macOS Sequoia 15.0 or later, in English in the US, for Google AI Plus, Pro and Ultra subscribers (plans from $4.99/month). Apple is also due to warn users more clearly about the risks of giving apps unrestricted access to their data when macOS 27.3 lands.

*Source: MacRumors*

Java4Shell probing ticks up — 192 requests, still no confirmed intrusions

GreyNoise has now logged 192 Java4Shell-shaped requests from six IP addresses since November 17, with no successful intrusions on its sensors. WatchTowr's honeypots have received blind exploit attempts, Cloudflare saw one scanner return about 17 hours after an initial probe with the exploit trigger while posing as Chrome, and Previdian logged nine attempts from six IPs on November 18–19. Vendors are still catching up: Red Hat shipped its RHEL 8/9/10 OpenJDK errata on Thursday, Keycloak shipped fixed container images, and Cloudflare added WAF rules. Cisco lists more than a dozen affected products with no fixed releases yet, and Siemens lists Industrial Edge Management products as affected with no fixes available. The flaw is still not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Note: one SecurityWeek headline reads "Exploited in the Wild," but its own reporting describes probing and attempts only — no confirmed compromise.

*Sources: SecurityWeek, CyberSixt, Abijita*

Netanyahu trial: High Court struck out the petition — prosecutors can cross-examine Mandelblit

Israel's High Court on Thursday struck out the prosecution's petition over restrictions on questioning defence witnesses, finding the defence's change of position had already secured the practical result prosecutors sought. Prosecutors are free to cross-examine former attorney-general Avichai Mandelblit and future defence witnesses under the arrangement adopted by the Jerusalem District Court on Wednesday, and Sunday's hearing is cancelled. But the court — Supreme Court President Isaac Amit and Justices Yael Willner and Khaled Kabub — did not settle the broader legal question, calling it "a substantial question" while concluding this petition was no longer the right setting to resolve it. Attorney-General Gali Baharav-Miara is due before the trial panel on November 25.

*Source: JBizNews*
