Zelensky says the US floated swapping Donbas for NATO membership — as overnight strikes kill a family in Belgorod

The US floated the idea of Ukraine ceding the eastern Donbas region to Russia in exchange for NATO membership during the recent US-brokered talks, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said on Saturday — the first time, he said, that such a trade has ever been put to Kyiv. The revelation landed on the same morning both countries reported fresh deadly overnight drone strikes.

According to CNN and the BBC, Zelensky told journalists that American negotiators raised the question during the talks in Abu Dhabi earlier this week: "What if Ukraine joins NATO? Would Ukraine withdraw from Donbas?" He called it "the first time this had been raised." US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff had shuttled from Moscow — where they met Vladimir Putin on Thursday — to Dublin to meet Zelensky, and the Abu Dhabi sessions on Tuesday and Wednesday were described as constructive by both Zelensky and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, though they ended without a breakthrough. The Kyiv Independent, cited by several outlets, reports Zelensky was told Washington had sketched security guarantees modeled on NATO's Article 4 and Article 5 commitments, delivered bilaterally and more like the guarantees the US gives Israel, with no American troops stationed in Ukraine — but he stressed the details do not exist yet: "We need specifics, and we don't have them yet."

Zelensky was careful not to treat the idea as an offer. He said Ukraine would not give up territory, and that if he received a "comprehensive security package" — a strong Ukrainian army, guarantees from NATO, money to rebuild — he would put it to the public: "If the American side confirms precisely this to us, together with other NATO partners, then I will ask our Ukrainian society what it thinks: do we accept it or not? I have no right to make that decision on my own." He also insisted any deal could not recognize occupied areas as Russian. US officials had previously said Ukrainian NATO membership was off the table; Zelensky said the exchange made him believe "NATO is back on the agenda." No confirmation from Washington of Zelensky's account has been reported, and it is not known who on the American side raised it.

The two sides are far apart on both halves of the proposed bargain. Moscow has long demanded all of Donbas, including the slice Ukraine still holds, and has cited NATO membership as a reason for the invasion in the first place. A list of Russia's demands obtained by Bild and reported by Politico — including keeping occupied land and gaining more, demilitarizing Ukraine's ports, and removing NATO bases from the Baltic states — was already partly reported on Friday; the Bild list adds the ports demand. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz has rejected renegotiating the European security architecture ("There is one" — NATO), and EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas has warned against walking into Russia's traps, insisting on a ceasefire first. And NATO membership is not Washington's to give: it needs the unanimous agreement of all 32 allies, 30 of them European.

Meanwhile the war went on overnight. Russia's Defense Ministry said it intercepted 29 Ukrainian drones overnight, including several over the Moscow region. A drone hit a private house in Proletarsky, in the Belgorod region about 20km from the Ukrainian border, killing a married couple and their daughter — reports differ on the daughter's age, with the regional headquarters saying 24 years old and Al Jazeera reporting 24 months. Nine more people were injured in Belgorod. In the other direction, Ukraine's air force said Russia launched 96 drones from Friday evening, of which 81 were shot down or electronically suppressed, with hits at eight locations. A woman was killed in Yasna Polyana in the Donetsk region, three people were injured in Hlukhiv in the Sumy region, and one person was killed and two injured in the Kharkiv region. In Odesa, nearly 50,000 people lost electricity after a morning strike on a DTEK power facility, and the road across the Dnipro hydroelectric dam in Zaporizhzhia was closed to traffic on Saturday morning. Russia's Defense Ministry said its strikes hit an energy facility in the Chernihiv region and a cargo ship delivering military supplies to Odesa. Neither side's claims could be independently verified.

Separately, Finland's military detected a strike on a Russian warship in the Gulf of Finland on Wednesday, while the Abu Dhabi talks were under way. Ukraine's Security Service said it attacked the Buyan-M-class small missile ship Naro-Fominsk; Estonian media reported the vessel later returned to St. Petersburg. Finland said it was not under any immediate military threat. The same day, Ukrainian drones caused a fire in the Russian port area of Ust-Luga, injuring three workers — part of the Baltic spillover already reported this week.

For ordinary people, the Donbas question is the war in miniature: the towns Ukraine still holds in Donetsk are home to civilians who would be handed to Russian control under any withdrawal, and the NATO-membership half of the trade would decide whether Ukraine ever gets the guarantee it has sought since 2022 — or whether it gives up land first and waits on a promise that 32 capitals must still approve. On the ground, the overnight toll is the familiar one: families killed in their homes, tens of thousands without power as winter approaches, and energy infrastructure hit on both sides.

In short: Zelensky says Washington has, for the first time, raised trading Donbas for NATO membership — a question, not a deal, with no US confirmation and no specifics yet, and with Moscow's demands running in the opposite direction. Watch whether Washington confirms the account, and whether the idea survives contact with Moscow or with Ukraine's own public. The next diplomatic date is the November 19 trilateral in Shenzhen, which pointedly excludes Ukraine.

Iran claims four more tanker mine strikes in about a day — none verified, and oil fell anyway

Iran's military claimed another round of tanker explosions in the Strait of Hormuz overnight, the fourth mine-strike claim in about a day — but as with every claim in this run, no vessel has been named, no flag or crew reported, and no maritime authority has confirmed any of them.

The Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy said on Friday night that two oil tankers, including a supertanker, had exploded and caught fire after hitting naval mines while trying to cross the strait by what it called an "unauthorized" route, with their radar and navigation systems switched off. Iran's conventional navy then said on Saturday morning that another tanker had caught fire after hitting a mine east of the strait. That follows Friday's claim of a gas carrier hitting a mine, and a run of similar claims since October 17 — two tankers on November 1, more on November 11 and 12 — all unverified. Reuters reported it could not immediately verify the latest reports.

The gap between Iran's claims and the incident record keeps widening. The UK Maritime Trade Operations center had logged 163 conflict-related maritime incidents since February 28 as of its November 13 weekly report, 141 of them involving damage to a vessel, while the International Maritime Organization had verified at least 130 attacks on commercial vessels, in which 25 seafarers have died and at least three ships have sunk. UKMTO has confirmed only a handful of the incidents Iran has claimed in the past week. US Central Command has repeatedly called Iran's reports false and says the main transit routes remain cleared of mines.

The two sides tell different stories about why this is happening. Tehran says it is asserting control over the strait and escalating attacks on ships it considers unauthorized, in response to the US naval blockade of its ports and tougher sanctions. Washington's line is that the claims are fabricated, the routes are clear, and US escorts continue — and that the blockade, not new strikes, is the pressure that works. The diplomacy is frozen: Iran's seven-day roadmap (blockade lifted, sanctions waived, ~$12bn in frozen assets released, Hormuz reopened on day 6) was rejected by Trump, who said this week "we don't need a deal with Iran."

For everyone else, the market is telling a different story than the headlines. Brent crude settled Friday at $95.84 a barrel, up two cents, and WTI at $88.56 — both down about 2.5 percent for the week, a second straight weekly loss. The International Energy Agency said Friday that global supply exceeded demand for the first time since the war began, with the market expected to return to surplus in the fourth quarter, and US crude output hit a record 14.013 million barrels a day in the week ending November 6. Traders are pricing in the blockade and the surplus, not the mine claims — but shipping through the strait is still running roughly 65 percent below pre-conflict volume, and the cost of moving oil around the Gulf keeps climbing.

In short: Iran claimed four tanker mine strikes in about a day, none independently confirmed, while the verified count stands at 163 incidents since February with 25 seafarers dead. Oil ended the week down anyway on a growing supply surplus. Watch for any UKMTO confirmation of the latest incidents — that, not another Iranian claim, is what would move prices and policy.

Reuters: Huang misled Nvidia's top lawyer about the Megaspeed affair

Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang misled several of the company's leaders, including general counsel Tim Teter and chief marketing officer Alison Wagonfeld, about the nature of his relationship with Chinese businesswoman Alice Huang, according to two people familiar with the matter and internal correspondence reviewed by Reuters. It is the first reporting on the correspondence, and it deepens the scandal that forced Huang's resignation on Thursday.

Reuters reviewed several exchanges from October, on condition of not quoting verbatim. In two separate messages that month, Huang told Nvidia leaders, including Teter and Wagonfeld, that his interactions with Alice Huang and her company Megaspeed reflected "standard business dealings with customers." In other messages he affirmed the routine nature of the interactions. He had given similar assurances in autumn 2025 after press queries drew leaders' attention to the relationship. Huang also discussed taking legal action against the New York Times after it published an article in October 2025 drawing attention to his public meetings with Alice Huang; no lawsuit was ever filed. Reuters verified photos of the two together in the US, China and elsewhere dating as far back as December 2023. It is unclear when the relationship became intimate. Teter did not respond to Reuters' request for comment, Wagonfeld did not immediately respond, and a representative for Huang and an Nvidia spokesperson declined to comment.

Alice Huang is a former top executive of Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based cloud computing firm and Nvidia business partner in the Asia-Pacific region; she became its controlling shareholder in March 2023, according to the research group C4ADS. The New York Times reported in October 2025 that Megaspeed was under US investigation over whether it helped Chinese customers obtain advanced Nvidia chips subject to export controls; there has been no public finding that it violated the rules, and Megaspeed says it has cooperated with US inspections. The Times also reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversaw the drafting of a classified memo in spring 2026 detailing intelligence indicating China's Ministry of State Security had recruited Alice Huang to target Nvidia's CEO, and that Rubio briefed President Trump about it aboard Air Force One in May. Reuters says it has not independently verified whether Alice Huang was working for the Chinese government, and Beijing has denied she is a spy. Attorney General Todd Blanche said on November 12 that the Justice Department had not yet reached a conclusion on whether she was a spy. The Times reported earlier this week that Huang also had an extramarital relationship with an Nvidia employee, and Reuters reported Thursday that he had another intimate relationship with a different Nvidia employee.

The political fallout is widening beyond the boardroom. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Friday that his department had favored putting Megaspeed on the US entity list for months, but that it was up to the Commerce Department to act. "The real story here should be: Did Nvidia know their client, and was there a robust effort at Nvidia to make sure that there was no leakage and that they were selling to what were obviously pass-through shell companies?" Bessent told CNBC. Lawmakers have called for tougher restrictions on China's access to advanced AI chips, and the scandal has drawn in Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whom Axios reported had stalled a crackdown on suspected Nvidia chip diverters. The Information reported that Huang's ties to Alice Huang facilitated at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip sales to China. Nvidia's board hired the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz on November 10 to run an independent investigation.

Huang's side has said little. He apologized in a statement on Sunday for being unfaithful to his wife and for disappointing the company, without naming Alice Huang, and said in a Bloomberg interview published October 29 that the US government had not informed him about its suspicions regarding her. The board's investigation continues, Congress is preparing a probe, and interim CEO Colette Kress faces her first earnings call on Tuesday, November 17.

For ordinary people, the stakes are in their portfolios and in the AI economy: Nvidia's stock has fallen more than 30 percent since its October 6 closing high of $239.24, erasing more than $1.8 trillion in market value, and the affair has turned the question of who gets America's most advanced AI chips — and how they are policed — into a Washington fight. What began as a personal scandal is now a test of whether the export-control system around the world's most important chip company actually works.

In short: Reuters reports Huang told Nvidia's own general counsel and marketing chief his dealings with Alice Huang were routine business, in messages reviewed by the outlet — the first direct evidence he misled the company's leadership. The spying allegations against her remain unverified, the DOJ has reached no conclusion, and Treasury wanted Megaspeed blacklisted months ago. Watch Tuesday's earnings call, the board's investigation, and whether Commerce finally puts Megaspeed on the entity list.

Sources: Reuters, Reuters on Alice Huang, Reuters on Bessent/Megaspeed

The FAA grounded every departing flight in America on Friday

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered a nationwide ground stop on Friday afternoon, halting departures at every US airport — the first nationwide stop since January 2023 and only the second since the September 11 attacks. It was lifted Friday night, but the disruption is expected to linger for days.

The FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center issued the order at 3:54 p.m. ET, "due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety," after a telecommunications outage hit the Air Route Traffic Control Centers that manage high-altitude en-route traffic across the country. "There has been a temporary telecommunications outage — out of an abundance of caution all flights have been halted. Every flight has been accounted for and international flights will be handled on a case by case basis," the agency said. More than 3,500 aircraft were airborne when the stop was issued; Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said they were all coordinated to safe landings, with fewer than 400 still in the air by the time of his briefing. FlightAware counted roughly 7,000 flights delayed or cancelled, and other tallies put the total affected above 10,000. The ground stop was lifted at about 8:19 p.m. ET, and the FAA said Saturday morning that air traffic operations had returned to normal — while warning that it will take time to resume normal operations and passengers should keep checking with their airlines.

The cause is still being pinned down, and the accounts differ on the details. CBS News reported the outage was caused by a failed update to the L3Harris-operated communications system connecting air traffic control centers, which forced a reboot and then crashed. The FBI does not believe the outage was a cyberattack or criminality, according to a person familiar with the matter, and a software update is one area of inquiry. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters there was no indication the stop was due to a threat. Duffy blamed aging infrastructure: the system still runs on analog TDM telecom, he said, and he is seeking an additional $30 billion from Congress for the air traffic control overhaul on top of the $12.5 billion obtained in 2025. The disruption began at about 3:36 p.m., when radar and communications at control centers and TRACON facilities nationwide became unstable.

The timing could hardly be worse. Thanksgiving is November 26, less than two weeks away, and the system was already strained: about 28,852 flights were scheduled to leave US airports on Friday, carrying 3.54 million seats, roughly 90 percent of them domestic. Airline stocks fell after the close — JetBlue down 3.5 percent, Delta 1.3 percent, American 1.7 percent, United 1.5 percent. The stop also coincided with a widespread Microsoft 365 outage affecting Outlook, Teams and Copilot, though there is no indication the two incidents are related.

For travelers, the practical effect is a weekend of rebookings and ripple delays: the FAA itself says normal operations will take time to resume, and with the holiday travel peak approaching, a system that can be brought down nationwide by a single telecom failure is now a political question as much as a technical one. Duffy is using the outage to press Congress for the extra $30 billion; critics will ask why $12.5 billion in overhaul funding did not prevent it.

In short: a telecom outage knocked out communications at every en-route air traffic control center on Friday afternoon, grounding all US departures for about four and a half hours in the first nationwide stop since January 2023. It was not a cyberattack, per the FBI, and flights are moving again — but delays will linger, and the failure has put the FAA's aging systems back at the center of a funding fight days before Thanksgiving.

Cybersecurity

SCKit fallout: Microsoft publishes the full attack chain, five ScreenConnect extensions disabled — Microsoft's Security Blog published a detailed analysis of SCKit on Friday: the worm abuses cloud-hosted ScreenConnect panels to install rogue clients, decrypts and extracts the toolkit with device-specific key material, establishes persistence through scheduled tasks and registry Run entries, and deploys a component called RelayTrap designed to deliver a payload to a connecting technician's workstation — spreading from a customer's device up to the technician and onward into other managed networks. ConnectWise disabled five third-party ScreenConnect extensions across roughly 400 partner environments, and Huntress found unauthorized sessions on more than 100 ScreenConnect servers disguised with crypto-exchange names, apparently as bait for technicians. The initial access route is still unidentified; the RMM+ extension (versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32), which accepted a blank access key when none was configured, is the leading suspect, and Huntress says it has not observed the previously patched CVE-2026-84869 being exploited in this campaign. Patch ScreenConnect to 26.6.5 or later. Why it matters: this is the second remote-management tool hit in a week, and the entry point is still unknown — MSPs should treat any ScreenConnect session as untrusted until the investigation closes. Sources: Microsoft Security Blog, IT Security News overnight roundup, PwnSpectrum
