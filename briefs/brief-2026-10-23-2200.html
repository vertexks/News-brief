1. Ukraine/Russia + Poland

Diplomacy on paper, drones over Moscow, and a ship hit off Romania

Friday evening's war news undercut the day's diplomacy in three directions at once: Ukrainian drones shut down Moscow's airports for the second time in two days, a likely Russian drone struck a cargo ship just outside Romanian waters, and Vladimir Putin publicly rejected the freeze-the-frontline idea that underpins the talks — all while the Oleshky humanitarian deal itself turned out to carry two different start dates.

The details, in order. According to Russia's aviation regulator Rosaviatsia, all four Moscow airports — Sheremetyevo, Domodedovo, Vnukovo and Zhukovsky — suspended arrivals and departures on Friday afternoon as air defences engaged drones approaching the capital, with restrictions published at 2:05 p.m. Moscow time. Moscow mayor Sergei Sobyanin said 17 drones were shot down heading toward the city that afternoon, on top of 155 he said targeted Moscow Oblast overnight. At Sheremetyevo, an air alert sounded and passengers were directed to shelters, and an unverified social-media image purported to show drone debris on the tarmac; the Kyiv Independent could not independently verify it. Flight trackers disagreed on the scale — one count put Sheremetyevo alone at 85 cancellations and 229 delays over the day, another put all four airports at 147 delays and 31 cancellations as of 2:31 p.m. — so treat any single tally as partial. An evacuation was also reported at the IQ-Quarter tower in Moscow City, which houses several Russian government ministries; TASS later said, citing emergency services, that alarm was false. The Institute for the Study of War (ISW) notes this was the second full closure of all four Moscow airports in two days, after a similar shutdown on October 22.

Caption: ISW's assessed-control map for October 23, 2026. It matters because it shows why Friday's diplomacy is so narrow: the front line itself barely moved, while the war's effects — Moscow airport closures, a Black Sea ship strike — spread far beyond it.

What each side argues has, if anything, hardened. Putin said on October 22 that occupied Ukraine is "the territory of historical Russia" while rejecting Zelenskyy's October 21 proposal to freeze the current frontline, according to ISW's summary of his remarks — invoking "Novorossiya," a claimed region that, on ISW's reading, stretches beyond the four illegally annexed oblasts to include Kharkiv, Dnipropetrovsk, Mykolaiv and Odesa. ISW's assessment, stated bluntly, is that Putin's remarks show the Kremlin is willing to negotiate only to accept Kyiv's surrender, not a compromise peace — that is ISW's analysis, not a neutral fact, and Moscow would frame its demands as security guarantees, not surrender terms. Zelenskyy's side, as reported Friday, is still holding the freeze-along-current-lines offer, plus demands for security guarantees and 10 years of army funding. Meanwhile the Starlink dispute stayed unresolved into the evening: ISW reports Ukrainian officials, including the General Staff and defence ministry sources, widely reported continued in-motion speed restrictions on Starlink and Starshield on October 23, with defence minister Serhii Sternenko calling the situation "ambiguous" — some units report resumption above the old limits, others still see dropouts at speeds around 100 mph, and there is, per a ministry source, "no confirmation" the restrictions have been lifted. That directly contradicts Thursday's claim that full access had been restored.

For ordinary people, the spillover is the story. Romanian president Nicusor Dan said on October 23 that an unspecified drone struck the Comoros-flagged cargo ship Khoryv near Romanian territorial waters — about 13 nautical miles off Constanta, just outside the 12-mile limit — calling it a consequence of "Russia's aggression against Ukraine"; European Commission president Ursula von der Leyen called it yet another "Russian escalation" in the Black Sea. Moldovan police separately reported drone debris falling up to 100 meters from a residential building near Chistelnica on October 23. ISW notes this follows likely Russian strikes on two tankers inside Romanian waters on October 14–15 and on civilian vessels in Bulgaria's exclusive economic zone on October 12–13 — a pattern, in ISW's view, of growing willingness to hit targets that touch NATO or NATO-aligned territory. And the Oleshky date gap matters for the 1,700–2,000 mostly elderly people still there: Gerasimov said Russian forces would cease fire on the Kherson axis from 4 a.m. Moscow time on October 27, while the White House release — and Melania Trump's own statement — put the localized ceasefire's start on October 29. Until one date is confirmed by the aid operation itself, the evacuation timetable is uncertain.

In short: Friday's talks window and prisoner swap are real, but Putin's own words point away from a frontline freeze, the Starlink limits are still reported in place, and the war disrupted Moscow air travel and Black Sea shipping on the same day. Watch for which Oleshky date the aid convoys actually run on, for any verified change in Starlink speeds, and for whether the Black Sea strikes draw a NATO response beyond statements.

2. Iran/US + Yemen/Saudi + Hormuz

Washington pulls its people out as October becomes the worst month on the water

The sharpest Friday-night development on the Gulf front is not a new strike but a withdrawal: the United States has ordered non-emergency government staff and their families to leave Saudi Arabia, and Canada has added Dammam's airport to its avoid-all-travel list — while new Reuters data confirms October is already the worst month for Strait of Hormuz shipping attacks since records began.

The details first. According to NonRev Travel News' October 23 roundup, citing the State Department, spokesman Tommy Pigott said on X that non-emergency US government employees and family members must leave Saudi Arabia, citing Houthi attacks on civilian airports; the order applies to Saudi Arabia only, and Pigott called reports of a Gulf-wide order "inaccurate," though Reuters and AFP sources said families at some other Gulf posts are also being told to leave. The underlying reporting dates the order to October 22 — it was not in the evening brief, so it is reported here as a catch-up, not a Friday-night decision. Canada's travel advice, updated October 22, now says to avoid all travel to King Fahd International Airport in Dammam and King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh because of military activity disrupting airport operations; the overall level is unchanged at avoid non-essential travel. On the ground, Riyadh's airport has resumed only a limited number of domestic flights on Saudia and flynas, subject to prior approval, with most foreign airlines still away — which settles the evening brief's open question in favour of "partly open, not really open." Saudi aviation authority GACA says Dammam resumed operations after Wednesday night's attack, which killed two people and injured eight.

On the fighting itself, ISW's October 23 Iran update reports Saudi air defences intercepted two ballistic missiles over Riyadh and Khamis Mushait and two drones over the southern region on October 23, in addition to a missile over Najran on October 22 — coalition-side claims, as usual, without independent confirmation of what was hit or missed. In Yemen, ISW also notes Yemeni government-aligned forces repelled Houthi attempts around Taiz City's "Old Airport" neighbourhood on October 22, after similar attempts on October 17–18, and that the Houthis fully encircled the city on October 12 — a reminder the ground war in Taiz is moving even as the air war dominates headlines.

The shipping numbers are the reason this is a major article, not just a travel notice. Reuters reported Friday, from Washington Institute fellow Noam Raydan's incident map, that October has seen 28 confirmed and reported attacks in the Strait of Hormuz and surrounding waterways — the highest monthly number on record for a tracker running since 2017 — and 113 since the US-Israeli war with Iran began on February 28. The International Maritime Organization's tally broadly aligns at 120 incidents since the conflict began, with more than 30 supertankers (VLCCs) damaged, including 11 in October alone — about 3.5% of the entire global VLCC fleet. Greece-based consultancy Marisks adds that October 5–11 alone saw a dozen ships attacked, the highest weekly count of the war. Raydan's reading, via Reuters, is that Iran is intensifying attacks to slow the recovery in shipments and keep pressure on energy markets ahead of the November US midterms. Tehran's side of the argument is its claim of seven tankers hit overnight October 21–22, which President Trump publicly dismissed as fabricated, saying more oil is flowing through the Gulf than before the war; the claim remains unverified, with no vessel names given, and should not be counted as seven confirmed casualties. The US Central Command position, via Al Jazeera's reporting, is that about 95% of vessels are getting through.

For ordinary people, the costs are already concrete. Brent crude traded above $114 a barrel on Friday, a five-month peak, according to Reuters' market commentary — which is why fuel prices, not just headlines, are the transmission mechanism to watch. Airline suspensions now run deep into the calendar: Etihad and Qatar Airways suspended Riyadh and Dammam through October 24, Air France through October 24, KLM and SalamAir through October 31, Lufthansa Group through November 18 (Riyadh) and November 30 (Dammam), British Airways resuming one daily Riyadh flight only on November 3, Cathay Pacific out of Riyadh through January 31, 2027, and Delta's Atlanta–Riyadh launch postponed to March 2027. When the US pulls its own non-emergency staff, consular help for private travellers thins out too — the practical advice in the roundup is to postpone non-essential trips to Saudi Arabia.

In short: no newly verified Houthi hit on Friday night, but the US ordered departure, Canada's Dammam warning, and the record October shipping tally together say the Gulf crisis is deepening rather than stabilising. Watch for Egypt's parliament vote on military support on October 26, for any CENTCOM word on the claimed Sirik strike, and for whether Brent holds above $114 or retreats. 4. Open source

OBS Studio 33.0.0 released October 23 — The free, open-source streaming and recording app's new stable release overhauls plugin loading (first-party plugins move to a new "core" folder; third-party plugins get a new location, with legacy locations still loading until version 34.0), improves source snapping in the preview with a new visual indicator, and runs on Windows 10+, macOS 13+ (Apple Silicon native) and Linux. Why it matters: OBS is the default streaming tool for a huge share of creators, and a plugin-loading overhaul is the kind of change to check before upgrading mid-project. OBS Versions (tracks the official GitHub releases)

Claude controls Photoshop via an open-source MCP server — unofficial — An independent developer released a Photoshop MCP server on October 23 under an MIT licence on GitHub, letting Claude automate Photoshop tasks through natural-language commands via ExtendScript modules. It needs Node.js 18+, a Claude API subscription and a licensed Photoshop copy. Flag: this is explicitly not affiliated with Anthropic or Adobe — a community project, not an official integration — so treat its roadmap (generative fill, batch processing) as aspirational. Why it matters: it shows MCP moving from chatbots into professional creative tools. Emergent

5. Cybersecurity

Nextcloud Collabora flaw joins CISA's exploited list — disabling the app does not protect you — CISA added CVE-2026-110447 to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 23, with a federal deadline of Monday, October 26. The flaw is an unauthenticated command injection (CVSS 9.2) in Nextcloud's optional Collabora Online – Built-in CODE Server app (richdocumentscode); attackers have used it since September 10 to plant web shells and hunt for password vaults and crypto wallets, per the Carthage Electronics threat brief. The fix is version 26.4.303, and Collabora says simply disabling the app does *not* protect you. Note: exploitation of this flaw was first reported October 15; the KEV listing and Monday deadline are what's new. Carthage Electronics, October 23 CVE Threat Brief

CISA updates its PHP2Shell alert — patch or take systems offline, plus a detection rule — CISA updated its alert on October 23 to reinforce the same actions for CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 (patch, or take PHP systems offline if you cannot update) and added a detection rule, echoing the NSA's October 21 advisory and the FBI's October 23 public service announcement reported in the evening brief. The Plex2Shell federal deadline landed today, October 23. Defcon Alerts summary

Cisco fixes a CVSS 9.8 root flaw in clustered Expressway gateways, plus two 9.0 Snort 3 bugs — From the same October 23 threat brief: the Expressway flaw reaches root in clustered deployments, and the Snort 3 bugs reach Cisco Secure Firewall. Separately, Canada's Cyber Centre says the Redis RESTORE flaw CVE-2026-25243 is being exploited, WordPress sites running Prime Mover have an unauthenticated object-injection bug to patch, and Wiz reports the China-aligned Storm-0940 crew is aiming an AI attack agent at other software vendors. Check vendor advisories for fixed versions before acting — the brief is remediation-only by design. Carthage Electronics, October 23 CVE Threat Brief

Metasploit adds new modules for 4gaBoards and OpenCTI flaws (October 23) — The new 4gaBoards module exploits CVE-2026-53959 to pull sensitive user data (emails, phone numbers) from the /api/users endpoint; the OpenCTI module chains CVE-2026-27960 and CVE-2026-39980 for command execution. Why it matters: these are now standardised testing tools, which also means unpatched 4gaBoards and OpenCTI installs are easier targets — patch first, test second. Wisevoter
