World escalation

Zelenskyy and Erdoğan agree to work on a Black Sea grain corridor — while Erdoğan rebukes both sides over attacks on civilian ships

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan agreed in Istanbul on Saturday to work together on making the Black Sea grain corridor "exist and be effective," in Zelenskyy's words at their joint news conference — the most concrete diplomatic product of a day that also saw Ukraine strike deep inside Russia and Russia keep hitting Ukraine's power grid. Erdoğan used the same press conference to condemn attacks on civilian vessels in the Black Sea as unacceptable "under any circumstances, regardless of who carries them out or what justification is offered," and urged both Russia and Ukraine to "act responsibly."

The details go beyond the corridor itself. According to Xinhua's account of the talks, Zelenskyy said a significant part of the conversation was devoted to Black Sea security and food security, and that the two sides are preparing new joint industrial and economic projects. Turkey also agreed to step up support for Ukraine's battered energy sector, with generators and other power equipment expected to reach Ukraine as early as next week — a direct response to the Russian strikes that left around 280,000 Ukrainian households without power on Saturday. Zelenskyy said the two discussed the trilateral US–Ukraine–Russia talks expected in early November, with Kyiv wanting Europe — and potentially Turkey — involved, and a meeting in Turkey itself raised as a possibility. The visit fell on International Black Sea Day, October 31, and Zelenskyy described it afterward as "important and substantive."

The two sides' arguments frame the same sea very differently. Erdoğan's position, reported by AFP and Reuters, is deliberately even-handed: he disclosed that Ankara has already sent both Moscow and Kyiv a proposal to stop strikes on civilian ships, and neither side has acted on it. He said he hopes to raise the matter with Russia as well, and that an agreement on shipping safety "would also give fresh impetus to efforts to secure lasting peace." Zelenskyy's argument, by contrast, puts the blame squarely on Moscow: he said Russia has steadily worsened Black Sea security since 2014, and that Turkey supports Ukraine's sovereignty and territorial integrity. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio added a third angle on Saturday, saying from the G20 foreign ministers' meeting in Atlanta that Turkey and India had "come to the table with some ideas" about getting grain moving — though no mechanism, timeline or guarantees were announced in Istanbul, and Russia previously rejected a Turkey–India–Egypt scheme under which flagged ships would call at both Ukrainian and Russian ports without being attacked.

For ordinary people, this is about food prices and winter heat as much as diplomacy. Ukraine's farm economy depends on the corridor, and a significant share of the world's wheat and corn supply moves through it; Zelenskyy told Bloomberg on October 23 that since Russian strikes intensified in August, Ukraine's grain export capacity has fallen to 38% of normal, with agricultural companies reportedly losing around $3 billion. The scale of the shipping danger behind Erdoğan's rebuke is stark: shipping data cited by the Financial Times counts more than 570 vessels hit in the Black Sea this year, with 40 seafarers dead. And for Ukrainians facing winter blackouts, the Turkish generators pledged for next week are the most immediate, tangible part of the deal.

In short: Kyiv and Ankara agreed to rebuild an effective grain corridor and Turkey pledged energy equipment for next week, while Erdoğan publicly rebuked both Moscow and Kyiv over attacks on civilian ships — but with no mechanism announced, no Russian response yet, and Ankara's own shipping-safety proposal still unanswered by both sides, the test will be whether ships stop being hit.

Houthi barrage hit Saudi cities Saturday evening into Sunday — and Riyadh airport starts flying again

A Houthi missile and drone barrage against Saudi Arabia that began Saturday evening ran into Sunday, according to Al Jazeera's reporting, with Saudi air defences intercepting projectiles over several cities and shrapnel falling in the Mecca province area. Saudi Civil Defence said shrapnel from two intercepted ballistic missiles fell in Jeddah, Taif, Rabigh and Wadi Al-Mughammas, while a drone and a ballistic missile were intercepted over Riyadh during the barrage. By midday Sunday, coalition forces had intercepted a cruise missile targeting Khamis Mushait, with warning sirens sounding across Taif and Jazan, and Al Jazeera reports 10 ballistic missiles directed at the southern region were intercepted in total. The figures are still developing and come from coalition and civil-defence statements as reported by Al Jazeera; independent casualty or damage totals were not available at brief time.

The other side of the exchange kept running too. Early Sunday, Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree warned civilians away from Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport — the same airport hit on October 10, killing 13 people and wounding hundreds. Yemen's government-aligned armed forces said they carried out 736 strikes on Houthi positions over the past 24 hours, with 307 people "neutralised" and 68 military vehicles destroyed — government figures that, like Houthi claims throughout this campaign, are unverified by independent sources. On Saturday itself, the coalition had already reported intercepting a hostile drone over the Riyadh region with no casualties, according to coalition spokesman Turki al-Maliki, and Air India extended its cancellation of Saudi flights through November 14.

For travellers, there is one concrete change: Riyadh announced late Saturday that international flights from King Khalid International Airport would resume from Terminal 2 on Sunday, with Terminal 1 designated for domestic flights, after weeks of suspension and disruption. That partial reopening sits uneasily next to Saree's fresh warning to stay away from the airport — the gap between the official reopening and the threat against it is exactly what passengers and airlines now have to judge.

In short: a Saturday-evening Houthi barrage stretched into Sunday with interceptions reported over Riyadh, Mecca province and the south, Yemen's government claims a heavy counter-strike toll, and Riyadh airport partially reopens Sunday even as the Houthis warn people away from it — all still developing, with casualty and damage figures unconfirmed.

Major court cases

Judge throws out Trump's $10-billion-per-claim defamation suit against the Wall Street Journal — permanently

A federal judge in Miami on Saturday permanently dismissed President Donald Trump's defamation lawsuit against The Wall Street Journal over its reporting on a birthday letter to Jeffrey Epstein, ending the case in the trial court for good. US District Judge Darrin P. Gayles dismissed the amended complaint with prejudice in a 20-page order, ruling that Trump had failed for a second time to plausibly allege "actual malice" — the standard a public figure must meet to win a defamation case. Because the dismissal is with prejudice, Trump cannot file another amended version in that court; only an appeal to the Eleventh Circuit remains.

The details matter because this was Trump's second attempt. Gayles first dismissed the case in April 2026, ruling the original complaint relied on "formulaic" assertions of malice that came "nowhere close" to the legal standard. The amended complaint, filed May 27, added new details — including an allegation that Rupert Murdoch told Trump "I will handle it" during a phone call about the article — but Gayles was unpersuaded, describing the malice allegations as "conclusory, vague, sometimes inconsistent with the Article," and writing, "As with his first attempt, he has failed." The suit, filed July 18, 2025, a day after the Journal's July 17 report on a bawdy 50th-birthday letter bearing Trump's name in a book compiled for Epstein, named Dow Jones, News Corp, Murdoch, chief executive Robert Thomson and two reporters, seeking at least $10 billion in damages on each of two claims (outlets differ on whether to call the total $10 billion or $20 billion). CNN reports Gayles also rejected Trump's argument that the Journal should have withheld the story after he denied writing the letter, saying such a requirement would violate the First Amendment: "Were that the law, any time a public figure or his team denies an accusation, journalists would be immediately blocked from publishing."

Each side's next move is already sketched. Trump's legal team said he will appeal and "continue to press forward," and a source familiar with his strategy told CBS News an appeal is likely. The Journal's side, meanwhile, avoids discovery entirely — dismissal at the pleading stage means Trump's team never got the internal newsroom communications it would have sought. Press-freedom groups are treating the ruling as a vindication of the actual-malice standard from New York Times v. Sullivan (1964), which Gayles cited; Trump's supporters frame it as one more round in his broader legal campaign against news organisations, which includes a separate $15 billion suit against The New York Times. Whether the defendants pursue legal fees remains open — the order reportedly did not resolve that question.

For ordinary people, the practical effect is narrow but real: it reaffirms how hard it is for any public figure — including a sitting president — to win a defamation case over reporting they dispute, and it closes, at least at trial level, a case that has shadowed the administration's handling of the Epstein files since last summer. The White House maintains the signature on the letter was not Trump's; the Journal has argued the birthday book, produced by Epstein's estate under congressional subpoena, contains the letter exactly as reported.

In short: Judge Gayles threw out Trump's amended WSJ defamation suit with prejudice on Saturday — the second dismissal this year — finding the actual-malice allegations conclusory and vague; Trump says he will appeal to the Eleventh Circuit, and the question of defendants' legal fees is still open.
