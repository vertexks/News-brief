World escalation

Komyshuvakha toll rises to 40 as Brussels talks on Ukraine's security guarantees open

The death toll from Russia's bombing of an aid distribution point in Komyshuvakha, in Ukraine's southeastern Zaporizhzhia region, rose to 40 on Wednesday, Reuters reports, with dozens still missing as search-and-rescue work entered its third day. Regional official Ivan Fedorov put the missing at 59, while President Volodymyr Zelensky said more than 30 people may still be trapped under the rubble. Two rescue workers and four police officers are among the dead, according to Ukraine's emergency services. The Institute for the Study of War, in its November 4 assessment, likewise puts the toll at at least 40 and notes the strike hit a humanitarian aid station southeast of Zaporizhzhia city.

Zelensky's account, repeated in the Reuters reporting, is that Russian forces first dropped aerial bombs on an underground school building being used as a distribution point as people arrived to collect aid, then struck the shelter with more bombs — a "double-tap" pattern, as the Kyiv Independent's coverage, carried by EuropeSays, describes it, with nine guided aerial bombs reported. Reuters also reports verified bodycam footage from one of the policemen killed, showing residents rushing into an underground shelter moments before a large explosion. Russia's defence ministry, by contrast, maintains the attack targeted Ukrainian military units around Komyshuvakha and two other settlements, killing up to 25 servicemen — a claim that does not explain the reported civilian toll at an aid site and has not been independently verified. Both sides deny deliberately targeting civilians.

For ordinary people in the village, the practical consequences are immediate and grim: the regional administration said the Zaporizhzhia budget will cover funeral costs, with moderate-injury victims receiving about Hr.20,000 (about $440) and severe-injury victims up to about Hr.50,000 (about $1,100), according to EuropeSays. More broadly, the strike landed a day after a brief US-brokered local ceasefire for evacuations from occupied Oleshky expired, and less than a week before US envoy Steve Witkoff's Abu Dhabi talks on November 10–11, which are to cover post-war security agreements, de-escalation and humanitarian measures. On Wednesday, officials from Ukraine, the United States, Germany, France, Britain, NATO and the European Union met at the European Commission's headquarters in Brussels on security guarantees, reconstruction and post-war economic opportunities — the meeting already flagged in the morning brief, with a separate NATO security-guarantee session due Thursday. No outcome from Wednesday's Brussels session had been reported in the coverage checked by brief time.

In short: 40 dead in Komyshuvakha, 59 missing by one official count, more than 30 possibly still under rubble, Russia still claiming a military target — and in Brussels, the allies spent Wednesday trying to turn security-guarantee talk into something Kyiv can take to Abu Dhabi.

Sources: Reuters on the toll rising to 40, EuropeSays/Kyiv Independent on the Wednesday-evening toll and victim payments, ISW Russian Offensive Campaign Assessment, November 4

Houthis claim a new round of Saudi strikes as Turkey's pact vote looms — and Riyadh stays silent on the airport claim

Yemen's Houthis claimed a fresh series of strikes against Saudi targets on Wednesday, according to Demócrata's account of spokesman Yahya Saree's statements: a ballistic missile that "directly" hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, causing what Saree called significant damage, plus a drone strike on a radar installation on the Al Qahma peninsula, a ballistic missile on a camp in Najran, a strike on a Border Guard command-and-control building in Jizan, six ballistic missiles on Saudi reinforcements east of Al Jawf governorate, and a downed reconnaissance drone over Taiz. Saree again urged airlines, workers and passengers to avoid Saudi airspace, except over Mecca and Medina. Saudi authorities had not provided an official version of the airport claim by the time of that reporting — so the airport hit, like the morning's claim, remains unverified, and should be read as a Houthi claim, not a confirmed strike.

The backdrop has not eased. The US Mission in Saudi Arabia warned American citizens on Monday of potential Houthi attacks, airspace closures and flight disruptions, the Philippine News Agency reports, noting the Saudi-led coalition said it had intercepted two drones over the Riyadh region on Monday, and that civil-defence alerts for Khamis Mushait, Abha, Taif and Najran were later lifted. A separate Dubai Standard roundup notes Hormuz traffic has collapsed — only five commodity-carrying ships passed through on Saturday, none on Sunday, against more than 130 a day before the conflict — and Brent crude was trading around $103 a barrel on Wednesday morning. Meanwhile Turkey's parliament was set on Wednesday to ratify the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement with Saudi Arabia and Pakistan, Reuters reported in the morning, with a collective-defence clause mirroring NATO's Article 5; ratification alone would not send Turkish troops, which need a separate mandate. No confirmed vote result had appeared in English-language coverage checked by 5 PM CT, so this brief does not claim the pact passed — watch the night brief for the result.

For ordinary people, the through-line is travel and fuel: Riyadh airport claims, confirmed or not, keep airlines and passengers guessing, US staff face tighter travel rules, and Gulf shipping remains a fraction of normal, which is exactly the pressure Iran and the Houthis are applying while Tehran says Washington's answer to its seven conditions is still "not convincing."

In short: the Houthis claimed a Wednesday missile hit on Riyadh airport plus five other operations, Riyadh has not confirmed the airport claim, US warnings to citizens stand, Hormuz traffic is near-empty — and Turkey's pact ratification was due Wednesday, with its result still unconfirmed at brief time.

Sources: Demócrata on the Houthis' Wednesday claims, Reuters via mymixfm on Turkey's parliament set to ratify the pact, Philippine News Agency on the US Mission warning and Monday interceptions, Dubai Standard on Hormuz traffic and Brent

Lock down switch management interfaces and update to that firmware. Source: Carthage Electronics November 4 CVE threat brief

This updates the November 3 KEV item; it is not a second listing. Source: 0daynews, November 4

Major court cases

Israel's consultations, day one: Eisenkot ahead on expected recommendations, but neither man has 61 — and the Arab parties and UTJ hold the balance

President Isaac Herzog opened consultations with the factions of the 26th Knesset on Wednesday evening, hearing the smallest parties first in a break from custom — Religious Zionism-Zehut first at 5 PM, then Shas and Yisrael Beiteinu — with the larger parties, including Yashar and Likud, due Thursday evening, JFeed reports. Herzog told the factions beforehand that he holds "very broad discretion" over who gets the first mandate, and that no party clearly won and not every campaign promise can be kept. The certified count, already covered in the morning brief, stands at 53 for Netanyahu's bloc, 48 for Eisenkot's, and a record 19 for the Arab-led parties, with 61 needed.

Wednesday's new information is the recommendation arithmetic. Ynet's tally, reported by JFeed, gives Eisenkot 48 expected recommendations — from Yashar, B'Yachad, the Democrats and Yisrael Beiteinu — against 46 for Netanyahu, from Likud, Otzma Yehudit, Shas and Religious Zionism. The Joint List, Ra'am and United Torah Judaism have not committed. The Jewish Weekly, meanwhile, reports both major Arab lists, Ra'am and the Joint List, have announced they will recommend Eisenkot — which would take him to 67 recommendations — while warning that recommending a candidate does not commit a party to supporting the government he then proposes, that Ra'am's position on an Eisenkot administration remains uncertain, and that Joint List support for a government is considered extremely unlikely. UTJ, with seven seats, is the other unresolved piece: it has yet to announce its recommendation. Herzog has not ruled out a second round of consultations, and the mandate is likely to be handed out sometime from Friday through next Tuesday; the nominee then gets 28 days, extendable by 14.

The two camps' arguments are mirror images. Netanyahu's camp, which had accused Arab groupings of having "stolen" the vote, saw that claim undercut Wednesday when the Central Elections Committee said its review found no irregularities compromising the election or changing its results. Eisenkot's camp argues the opposition's combined strength — parties opposed to Netanyahu hold 67 seats against his bloc's 53 — entitles him to the first attempt, and is already eyeing replacing the Knesset speaker, with Yair Lapid viewed as a likely candidate. For ordinary Israelis, nothing changes yet: Netanyahu remains in office, the new Knesset is sworn in November 10 regardless, and the paths — an Eisenkot government with Arab support, a unity arrangement, or failure and new elections — are all still open.

In short: day one of consultations leaves Eisenkot ahead on paper (48, or 67 with both Arab lists), Netanyahu at 46, UTJ uncommitted — and Herzog, who says the choice is his discretion, not an automatic tally, likely decides between Friday and next Tuesday.

Sources: JFeed on the consultation opening and Ynet tally, JFeed on Herzog's broad discretion, The Jewish Weekly on the Arab lists and UTJ, Crisis.Zone on the certified results and consultation order
