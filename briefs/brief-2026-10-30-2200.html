World escalation

US approves a $2.1 billion Patriot missile sale to Ukraine — but the missiles may not arrive until the 2030s

The US State Department said on Friday it had approved the sale of 500 Patriot PAC-3 missiles to Ukraine for an estimated $2.1 billion, in what Reuters describes as one of the most significant steps to supply defensive weapons to Kyiv since the start of the second Trump administration — and, in the same breath, a reminder of how slow the pipeline is: two people briefed on the decision told Reuters the missiles have yet to be manufactured and are not expected to be delivered until a couple of years into the 2030s.

The deal's shape matters as much as its size. According to Reuters, Ukraine will pay with contributions from Canada, Germany and Norway routed through the US JUMPSTART program, along with national funds financed by the European Union's Ukraine Support Loan, and Lockheed Martin is the principal contractor. JUMPSTART uses allied donations to buy weapons, services and training for Ukraine; Reuters notes it was used in August to buy 3,350 air-launched cruise missiles worth $825 million. In July, the US Army awarded Lockheed a contract worth up to $58.6 billion to produce Patriot interceptors, with Lockheed saying that deal would let it triple PAC-3 MSE production capacity by the end of 2030.

What each side says about the timing is where the tension sits. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said earlier this week that Ukraine had been waiting for almost a month for the US to sign off on critical missile supplies, as Russia stepped up attacks on energy and civilian infrastructure, and he has repeatedly urged Washington to speed up deliveries ahead of winter. Reuters reports Ukraine's Defence Ministry is encouraging the Pentagon to bring the delivery date forward, possibly by giving Kyiv missiles already in the US arsenal and backfilling with the new interceptors. The US side has not publicly committed to that acceleration. The approval also lands against the other Friday friction covered in the evening brief: delayed deliveries have added to US-Ukraine tension after President Trump announced a diesel deal with Russia and criticised Zelenskyy for not stopping attacks on Russian refineries — attacks Ukraine resumed before dawn on Friday at Novokuibyshevsk.

For ordinary people in Ukraine, the gap between approval and arrival is the story. Patriot is the most effective weapon against fast-moving ballistic missiles, and Russia's campaign against the power grid is already the winter's defining threat — Thursday's drone strike on a crowded Dnipro rail platform killed at least 14 people, and five more died in daytime strikes on two other cities that day, according to officials cited by Reuters. A 500-missile order approved now but delivered in the 2030s does not keep the lights on this winter; what does, in the near term, is whatever interceptors allies can pull from existing stocks, which is exactly what Kyiv is now asking Washington to arrange.

In short: Friday's $2.1 billion approval is real and large, but it is a long-term order, not a winter delivery — and the thing to watch is whether the Pentagon agrees to hand over missiles from its own arsenal now and replace them later, because that is the only version of this deal that helps Ukraine before the 2030s.

Sources: Reuters, Devdiscourse (Reuters wire)

Second holy-city drone claim in a day — Medina this time — as Houthi missiles hit Aden's airport and palace

The Saudi-led coalition said on Friday evening it had intercepted and destroyed a drone launched by Yemen's Houthis over Saudi Arabia's Medina region, hours after the Mecca-region interception covered in the evening brief — and the Houthis denied both, calling the Medina claim a fabrication, on the same evening Yemeni government forces said Houthi ballistic missiles struck Aden's airport and the Maashiq presidential palace.

Reuters reports the coalition's Medina announcement came on Friday, with no statement of what the drone was aimed at or whether debris caused damage. Medina is home to one of Islam's holiest sites and is visited by millions of Muslim pilgrims each year, which is why the claim carries weight far beyond one drone. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree denied any operation directed at Saudi territory, saying in a social media post that the claim a drone was shot down over Medina was "merely a continuation of their grand and egregious fabrications." The Houthis have repeatedly denied targeting Mecca and Medina, saying their operations target Saudi oil facilities and military bases, and that they publicly announce attacks when they occur. The coalition has now placed Houthi drones over the regions of both holy cities within a single day, according to a roundup of Friday's coalition statements, which also counted a cruise missile destroyed en route to western Saudi Arabia and a ballistic missile aimed at the southern region that day — four interceptions claimed in total, with the Houthis denying the holy-city claims.

The Aden side of Friday evening is reported by Yemen's government, not the coalition. Yemen Monitor reports that armed forces spokesman Colonel Majid Al-Nuzili said the Houthis targeted Aden International Airport and the Al-Maashiq Presidential Palace with ballistic missiles, in what he called a new attack on vital infrastructure in the temporary capital. Local residents reported two missiles launched from Ibb and Al-Dhalea governorates toward Aden, followed by three loud explosions across the city, and witnesses reported ambulances entering the palace compound. No casualty or damage figures had been made public by the early hours of Saturday. The Yemeni government called on the UN Security Council to take deterrent measures and enforce the arms embargo on the group. Aden airport had only resumed flights on Thursday morning after Wednesday evening's shelling forced a temporary suspension and emergency runway repairs.

For ordinary people, the two Friday threads hit different nerves. A drone claim over Medina, true or fabricated, lands on pilgrims and on Muslim public opinion worldwide — the Gulf Cooperation Council has previously called such strikes a violation of the sanctity of the holy sites — while Aden's airport is the south's humanitarian and civilian lifeline, already struck twice on October 28 and again on October 15, when the palace attack killed at least four people according to government officials. Each side is also using Friday's claims to frame the other: Riyadh presenting a pattern of holy-city targeting, the Houthis presenting a pattern of fabricated Saudi claims, and the Yemeni government presenting a pattern of attacks on civilian infrastructure that contradicts the Houthis' stated aim of ending the blockade on Yemen.

Caption: The Maashiq Presidential Palace in Aden, the seat of Yemen's internationally recognised government in its temporary capital. Yemeni government forces said Houthi ballistic missiles targeted the palace complex and Aden's airport on Friday evening; no casualty or damage figures had been released, but the palace is both a government seat and a symbol of the south's administration, which is why strikes on it carry political weight beyond any physical damage.

In short: Friday ended with the coalition claiming a drone downed over Medina and the Houthis denying it outright, while in Aden the government says missiles hit the airport and palace — claims and denials, no confirmed casualties yet, and two holy-city regions now named in coalition claims in a single day. Watch for any debris evidence, pilgrim-area impact, or casualty reporting that would settle whether Friday's Medina claim was a real interception or, as the Houthis insist, a fabrication.

Sources: Reuters on the Medina interception and denial, Yemen Monitor on the Aden strikes, Silent Dossier roundup of Friday's four claimed interceptions

Cybersecurity

  • Binance second update — withdrawals now targeted in phases within ~34 hours: Binance's second security-incident update, signed by co-CEOs He Yi and Richard Teng, narrows the evening brief's 42–45 hour estimate: withdrawals are to resume in phases by token within about 34 hours, subject to security checks. About 80% of the stolen funds have been replenished, with $5 billion+ of the $6.34 billion company reserve already held in the affected tokens, about $3 billion frozen on-chain, and no evidence of wallet private-key leakage found. A first proof-of-reserves snapshot is due within 12 hours, with results published before Monday, November 2, and further technical findings in about 48 hours. Sources: PANews, Bitcoin.com
  • Weekend patch deadlines — three land this weekend: Linux kernel "Fragnesia" CVE-2026-46300 is due Saturday, October 31, and GitLab CVE-2026-97229 plus Cisco ASA/FTD CVE-2026-76418 are due Sunday, November 1, with Ivanti's CVE-2026-111357 due Monday, November 2. If you run any of these, the weekend is the deadline. Source: DataWater CISA KEV weekly roundup
  • Palo Alto firewall chain — single-source report, flagged: One report, citing BleepingComputer, claims attackers chained the PHP2Shell flaws (CVE-2026-109618/109619) with the Fragnesia kernel flaw (CVE-2026-46300) to reach unauthenticated root on Palo Alto PAN-OS 12.1/12.2 firewalls in under 15 minutes. That is a single-source chain claim, not yet corroborated by Palo Alto or CISA in the coverage checked — but if you run PAN-OS 12.1/12.2 exposed to the internet, the patching advice in it (update PHP and kernel components) matches the KEV deadlines above regardless. Source: Pacific
