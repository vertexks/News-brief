Evening brief — Thursday, November 12, 2026

Trilateral talks are back on — but Europe's seat at the table is still contested

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner went from the Kremlin to Dublin in a single day, and came out saying the US–Russia–Ukraine talks are running again. "We've restarted it. We've set a new meeting," Witkoff told CNN at Moscow's Vnukovo Airport before flying out. No date or venue was given, and the one concrete result from the whole round remains the prisoner swap agreed in Abu Dhabi — which Zelenskyy called "a real result" in Dublin on Thursday night, saying it will be the biggest exchange of the war. But the two sides are already pulling in different directions over who gets a seat next time.

The day started in Moscow, where the envoys briefed Vladimir Putin for about two hours on the two days of US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi — the first direct Russian–Ukrainian discussions in nine months. According to the US official account, the conversation "focused on potential pathways to ending the war, addressing the security concerns of both sides, and establishing the conditions for a durable peace." Witkoff said Putin was invited to give Russia's view of a stable postwar security environment, and Kushner said the US is working on "really incredible" post-war security plans for Ukraine. Putin, for his part, said Russia had "never objected (to) pursuing dialogue" with Europe and wanted a peace that would be "durable and lasting… eternal, for good." Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov called the talks constructive and said Putin will keep discussing Ukraine with Donald Trump at the APEC summit in Shenzhen later this month. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, meanwhile, said the EU is preparing a 22nd sanctions package against Russia, to be presented before the European Council meets on November 26–27.

What each side is saying splits on Europe. The US and Ukraine want Europeans in the room: Witkoff said "they're our partners, and it's important for them to be involved," and Kushner said after the Dublin meeting that "we cannot conclude this deal without the Europeans being at the table." Zelenskyy, speaking at the European Political Community summit in Dublin, said the past days' talks "give (us) hope that we will end this war, that after this meeting will be another meeting and each meeting has to give a result." Moscow is pushing back: Ushakov played down European participation, saying "they are making every effort to get involved in the negotiations, but, as our president has said, they need to decide whether they want peace or a continuation of the war. So far, all their practical actions point precisely to the latter." Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov had already criticised the European presence in Abu Dhabi as not a "promising approach," and the Kremlin said no contacts with EU representatives took place there. And the central issue hasn't moved at all: Kyiv still refuses to surrender the Donbas, and Moscow still demands it.

For ordinary Ukrainians the diplomacy changed nothing on the ground. Russia launched another ballistic missile and drone attack overnight into Thursday — the same barrage covered in the morning brief, which killed a woman in a Kyiv apartment block — and Zelenskyy warned on Thursday that Starshield, the military-grade version of Starlink his forces rely on, has faced "significant disruptions" again in recent days. "These are not random errors but systematic speed restrictions," he said. "For us, this primarily means a significant complication in intercepting Russian jet-powered drones." He added on X that the restrictions are "helping the Russians kill more Ukrainians and carry out more accurate strikes." SpaceX told CNN there had been no change in its service, and the White House and Pentagon did not immediately respond. The same dispute flared in October, when Trump denied approving speed limits on Ukrainian terminals while Musk said the Trump administration was behind them. Kyiv says it is "trying to establish all the circumstances with the American side."

In short: the envoys left Moscow claiming the trilateral process is restarted with a new meeting set and Europeans joining next time — but Moscow's own aides are already downplaying Europe's role, the Donbas deadlock is untouched, and the only agreed outcome so far is the prisoner swap. Watch whether the swap actually happens, and whether a date for the next round appears.

Houthis claim strikes on Saudi Arabia's oil lifeline as Iran's tanker toll keeps growing

Yemen's Houthis said on Thursday they had attacked a pumping station on Saudi Arabia's East-West oil pipeline and an oil facility at Yanbu on the Red Sea coast — the pipeline that now carries about 4 million barrels a day of Saudi crude, roughly 4% of global supply, around the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia did not immediately respond to a request for comment, and the Houthis did not say which day the attacks were carried out. It is the latest turn in a campaign that has already hit the kingdom's airports, an Aramco headquarters tower and its Riyadh financial-district offices, and it comes as Iran's Revolutionary Guards keep raising their claimed toll on shipping: four tankers now, they say, caught fire after hitting mines, two still burning, two more intercepted — plus three more tankers damaged on Thursday.

The details, as far as they can be verified, are thin on the tanker side and sharper on the Saudi side. Reuters reports the Houthi claim on the pipeline and Yanbu facility, and notes the pipeline is what has spared Saudi Arabia — the world's biggest oil exporter — from the worst of the Hormuz shutdown that has crippled its neighbours' exports. The coalition said its air defences destroyed a Houthi ballistic missile launched toward the Medina region on Thursday, and Saudi civil defence issued a warning for the Yanbu governorate, the Red Sea terminal complex that handles the kingdom's oil exports and is its main Hormuz bypass. The Houthis again denied targeting Mecca, after Saudi civil defence said two ballistic missiles intercepted on Wednesday had debris fall in the Mecca region, including Sabuhah village. On the shipping side, Iran's account has grown from two supertankers on Wednesday night to four tankers caught fire after mine strikes, with two still burning — but no vessel names, flags, owners or crew conditions have been given, and the maritime reporting body UKMTO has logged only one strike, off Oman on Tuesday. Reuters said it could not independently verify the Iranian reports. The gap between the two counts is now the story: Iran claims a wave of strikes, while the international incident record lists far fewer.

What each side is saying is now a straight contradiction. The Houthis present the pipeline and Yanbu attacks as part of a widening campaign against Saudi airports, ports and oil installations, and have demanded a large financial settlement to stop. Saudi Arabia has not confirmed damage to the pipeline or Yanbu, and its public line remains the intercepts: missiles destroyed, no casualties. Iran's Guards warn the same fate awaits any vessel that turns off its navigation systems and cooperates with the US military on the southern route. Tehran, for its part, is reviewing a US proposal through mediator Pakistan, according to its foreign ministry — while its forces keep the pressure on shipping, with Reuters reporting Iran also attacked ship-navigation systems on Oman's Didamar island in the strait.

For ordinary people the stakes run through fuel prices and flights. The East-West pipeline is Saudi Arabia's workaround for a closed Hormuz; if it were knocked out for long, Reuters reported earlier that stocks at Yanbu could sustain exports for only around five to seven days, and oil has already been volatile on every new incident. The Houthi campaign has also kept Riyadh's King Khalid airport disrupted for days, and the US Embassy still requires special approval for government staff to use Saudi airports. The shipping picture is murkier: Reuters, citing Washington Institute data, counts at least 10 ships hit in the first 10 days of November after 37 attacks in October, and the International Maritime Organization has recorded 133 confirmed incidents in the strait and wider region since February, with 25 seafarers killed.

In short: the Houthis claim they hit the pipeline that lets Saudi oil bypass Hormuz — unconfirmed by Riyadh — while Iran's claimed tanker toll keeps climbing with no independent verification. The verified facts are the Medina intercept, the Yanbu warning, and a pipeline carrying a twentieth of the world's oil now sitting in the crosshairs. Watch for a Saudi damage statement and any UKMTO confirmation of the tanker claims.

Nvidia shares fell as much as 14% after the resignation, then recovered — Bernstein cut its price target in half

Nvidia shares opened more than 13% lower on Thursday after Jensen Huang's resignation, dropping as low as $148.80 — a new 52-week low — before recovering to close down 4.34% at $166.19. Google reverses course on Android sideloading — "experienced users" can bypass verification

Google said it is revising its sideloading policy and will allow advanced users to bypass the restriction on installing unverified apps, accepting the risks themselves. The change follows developer complaints about limiting user choice and developer freedom. Google is building a new advanced flow for it; no date was given. (Gadgets360)

Cyber

New N-able zero-day "4EverAble2Shell" exploited within hours — 21 servers hit, most on the latest patch

Hackers are actively exploiting a newly disclosed N-able N-central zero-day, dubbed 4EverAble2Shell, to compromise servers and deploy cryptocurrency miners — researchers counted at least 21 affected systems, including 19 running the company's latest security update. Security researcher Sean Bales published proof-of-concept exploits for two new flaws on Thursday morning (4EverAble2Shell and Agent2Shell), tested against the November 10 hotfix that was supposed to fix the first batch of 30+ zero-days he disclosed earlier in the week. Probing started around 08:30 UTC, hours after publication; by 12:50 UTC researchers counted 21 live Monero miners across seven countries (US, Canada, UK, Germany, France, Sweden, South Africa). The chain combines an Apache Axis flaw (CVE-2026-90436) with an N-central privilege escalation to get root; it requires the Legacy API to be enabled, which N-able says is off by default and not recommended. N-able's own advisory said it had seen no signs of compromise via the new flaws — the researchers' timestamps say otherwise. N-central is the management plane MSPs use to run their customers' devices, which is why this one matters beyond the miners: so far the payloads are cryptojacking, but the same access could do far worse. (BleepingComputer · 0daynews)

Qubole breach: attackers abused a trusted AWS role to raid customer accounts

Attackers breached AWS accounts belonging to multiple Qubole customers between October 14 and 26, stealing stored secrets and data by exploiting credentials for an AWS account Qubole's platform uses to access those customer environments. Cloud security firm Wiz disclosed the incident. Qubole is a cloud data platform that manages compute and storage in customers' AWS accounts through cross-account IAM roles — so the attack abused a trusted third-party relationship rather than breaking into each customer directly. The number of affected organisations was not disclosed. If you use a platform with cross-account AWS access, this is the one to check your trust roles for. (defend.network briefing, citing The Hacker News)

Fridge-hack follow-up: Iran formally rejects France's attribution; two Russian-aligned groups claimed it instead

Iran's Foreign Ministry has formally rejected France's attribution of the October supermarket refrigeration attacks to the IRGC's Shahid Kaveh unit, calling the allegations "baseless and hostile." France named the unit on November 9, citing Farsi-language indicators and IP addresses linked to Iranian infrastructure, and said it would seek EU sanctions. Meanwhile two Russian-aligned hacktivist groups claimed responsibility for the attacks on Wednesday, though their claims have attracted limited credibility. France's attribution covers only the French attacks on October 11–12 (~700 stores); the second wave of refrigeration incidents reported across Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden, Ireland, the Netherlands, Estonia and Finland earlier this month remains under separate investigation. (ACR Journal · EU Today)

Open source

GitHub trending, November 12 — agents auditing code lead the week

cloudflare/security-audit-skill is the biggest weekly gainer on all of GitHub, adding 11,507 stars for a total of ~70,500. It's a six-phase audit workflow written as markdown for any coding agent that can spawn parallel sub-agents — the agent checks its own findings instead of just flagging them. openai/codex-security (~11,900 stars) is the packaged version of the same idea: a cs CLI, TypeScript SDK, Codex plugin and GitHub Action that scan, validate, patch and verify fixes. Also new: anefzaoui/misterdownloadmanager, a free open-source download manager with the familiar IDM-style window, and NVIDIA/auto-ontology, the week's #1 new repo by stars (+7,947), a library that builds a context layer from enterprise metadata so agents know what data means and where it came from. Courts

Murdaugh retrial — tomorrow's hearing could throw out all four murder indictments

Alex Murdaugh is due back in court Friday, November 13, in Lexington County, where Judge Debra McCaslin will hear the defence's motion to dismiss all four murder indictments. The defence argues SLED's lead agent told the grand jury in July 2022 that an expert had found Maggie and Paul Murdaugh's blood on the shirt Alex wore the night of the killings — eight months after SLED's own lab reported finding no human blood on it. Prosecutors call the agent's year-long miss inadvertent and oppose dismissal. McCaslin has already warned both sides the retrial starts April 5, 2027, "whether you like it or not." (Substack — DeWitt · Parade · True Crime Today)
