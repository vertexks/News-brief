World escalation

After the Oleshky truce: Kyiv says Russia used the pause to dig in, and the drone war is back at full volume

The six-day humanitarian truce around Oleshky ended Sunday night without a successor arrangement, and Monday morning brought Kyiv's sharpest charge yet about how it was used: Ukraine's General Staff said Russian forces spent the pause strengthening their positions rather than just holding fire. The claim, carried by Reuters, is the first official Ukrainian assessment of the ceasefire's military side — and it arrived on a morning when the wider war showed no sign of pausing at all.

The details, as reported by Reuters and Ukraine's air force, show both halves of that story. The General Staff said it had recorded rotations of Russian units, fortification of positions, expanded anti-drone measures along roads, and increased logistics activity, including ammunition deliveries, during the October 27–November 1 pause. Ukraine's 34th Separate Marine Brigade "Borysfen" echoed the warning in a video address, telling Kherson residents not to ignore air-raid sirens and saying there were reasons to expect an intensification of hostilities. Kherson governor Oleksandr Prokudin said Russian forces had shelled and attacked with drones and aircraft 16 settlements in Ukrainian-controlled parts of the region, including the city of Kherson itself, in the 24 hours to 0400 GMT Monday — and the city's military administration reported a drone strike in central Kherson on Monday, with no casualties reported. Russia has not publicly responded to the reinforcement charge; its only praise so far has been for how the humanitarian operation itself was conducted.

Overnight, the air war resumed at scale. Ukraine's air force said Russia launched 193 drones during the night of November 1–2, including 75 jet-powered Shahed-type drones, and that air defences shot down or suppressed 154 of them by 08:00, with hits recorded at 26 locations and debris falling at nine more. Local reports tallied the human cost: in the Vinnytsia region, a drone hit a dormitory housing internally displaced people from Donetsk, killing a 13-year-old girl and a woman and injuring six more, including four children; in Sumy region, a 70-year-old woman was killed when a drone hit a car; in Kharkiv region, 11 people were injured, including a 3-year-old girl; and in Odesa and Zaporizhzhia, further injuries and damaged homes were reported. The previous night had been heavier still — Reuters reported Sunday strikes on energy facilities in four regions that left hundreds of thousands of households without power, with Ukraine's air force saying it downed 371 of 433 Russian long-range drones, while Kyiv said it hit the Kamensky plant in Russia's Rostov region and the governor of Nizhny Novgorod reported "significant damage" to an industrial facility there. Russia's Defence Ministry, for its part, said it downed 235 Ukrainian drones overnight.

There is also a stranger front opening: the alert systems themselves. Ukraine's air force confirmed Sunday that its Air Alarm app was hacked with false radiation-threat alerts minutes after the truce expired, with Kyiv's strategic-communications centre blaming Russia — an attribution that is Kyiv's claim, not independently verified. A roundup of the weekend's incidents reports that public alert systems in Russia also sent false chemical and nuclear warnings after what both sides described as cyberattacks on their mobile alert apps. Neither side's claim about the other's hack has been independently confirmed, and both are worth treating as wartime claims rather than established fact.

For ordinary people, the picture is bleakly familiar: a humanitarian experiment that evacuated nearly 800 people and held without violations, according to both sides' own human-rights officials, is now being argued over as a military episode — while the energy strikes, the drone alerts and the hacked warning apps all point the same way, toward another winter in which power, heat and trustworthy warnings are all under pressure.

In short: Kyiv says Russia used the Oleshky pause to rotate troops, fortify positions and move ammunition, and is warning Kherson residents to expect heavier fighting — as overnight drone exchanges, civilian casualties in several regions, and duelling claims of hacked alert apps show the wider war never actually stopped.

Doha talks produce a draft — and oil traders bet on it before any tanker moves safely

Indirect talks in Doha between Iranian negotiators and Qatari mediators produced something new on Monday: a draft proposal now being circulated, with a follow-up meeting possible, according to an official briefed on the talks who spoke to Reuters. Iranian negotiators have left Doha for now, but the talks are not declared dead — and oil markets moved first, falling more than 4% on Monday on hopes that the Strait of Hormuz, the waterway at the centre of the whole standoff, might actually reopen.

The details, as reported by Reuters and the maritime press, show a negotiation that has narrowed to a concrete, if still unaccepted, offer. Iran has proposed reopening the strait within two days of Washington meeting its conditions — shortened from an earlier seven-day timeline during the Doha sessions, according to reports of the talks. Tehran's conditions, as stated by Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and security chief Mohsen Rezaei, include sanctions relief, the release of more than $6 billion in frozen Iranian assets, restored oil waivers, an end to the US naval blockade and maritime interdictions, and fewer US forces in the region. Araghchi said Sunday the talks were "ongoing and progressing" but had reached no final conclusion, and stressed there are no direct US–Iran talks at this stage. On the American side, Secretary of State Marco Rubio said Saturday that President Donald Trump had made no final decision on further strikes but preferred a diplomatic solution, and Axios reported Trump told Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu on Saturday he preferred to give diplomacy more time. The Guardian's framing, carried in earlier coverage, still holds: Gulf officials are braced for the worst after Tuesday's US midterm elections, when the US moratorium on strikes against Iran is set to expire.

The market reaction was swift — and, by the analysts' own admission, ahead of the facts on the water. Reuters reports Brent crude fell $4.79, or 4.2%, to $108.64 a barrel by 1020 GMT Monday, with US West Texas Intermediate down 4.2% to $99.71, after Brent ended October at its highest level in six months. "The new month brought with it renewed hopes of a breakthrough in the Iranian stalemate," said PVM's Tamas Varga, while KCM Trade's Tim Waterer cautioned that "lingering hopes of some form of diplomatic progress, combined with the absence of any meaningful military escalation over the weekend, have taken some of the immediate heat out of prices for now." The sceptical case got its own numbers Monday: Goldman Sachs raised its December Brent forecast to $105 from $90 on supply risks, and warned Brent could jump to $140 in 2027 if Gulf output stays 3.5 million barrels per day below its 2025 average; ADNOC's chief executive said the world has drained nearly 1 billion barrels from inventories since the war disrupted Hormuz shipping. Seven OPEC+ producers, including Saudi Arabia, Russia and Iraq, agreed Sunday to keep December output targets unchanged, with another meeting set for November 29.

Meanwhile the waterway itself has not gone quiet. The weekend's incidents — a UAE-owned tug reported partially sinking after a projectile strike, a tanker hit by two projectiles near Ras Al Khafji, and Iran's Revolutionary Guard claiming two tankers exploded after hitting mines on an unauthorised route — remain unverified in their attribution, and the Institute for the Study of War assesses Iran likely struck a vessel in the strait on November 1 as part of its effort to disrupt civilian shipping and pressure Washington. No new vessel strike was confirmed early Monday.

For ordinary people, the oil move is the most direct link between Doha and daily life: if the draft becomes a real reopening, fuel and heating costs could ease from levels driven by months of war-risk premiums; if it collapses after Tuesday's midterms, Goldman’s $140 scenario is a reminder of how fast the same prices could run the other way. For now, traders are pricing hope, not passage — no tanker has yet moved through a reopened strait.

In short: Doha produced a circulating draft and a two-day reopening offer that Washington has not accepted, oil fell more than 4% on the hope, but the strait is still effectively closed, the tanker attacks of the weekend are still unattributed, and everything now hinges on Washington's answer and what happens after Tuesday's midterms.

Saudi–Houthi exchange: missile intercepted, southern cities alerted, and a port strike's civilian toll

The Saudi–Houthi exchange continued into Monday morning on both sides of the border: the Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile aimed at the kingdom's southern region, Saudi civil defence issued and then lifted alerts for four southern and western cities, and the Houthis reported seven civilians injured in coalition strikes on a Red Sea port and an oil facility in Yemen.

The details, as reported by Reuters, Xinhua and Houthi-run media, show a familiar pattern with a newly documented civilian cost. Reuters reports Saudi civil defence said on Monday that the threat had passed shortly after issuing alerts for Khamis Mushait, Abha, Taif and Najran, amid weeks of escalating hostilities; the coalition later said it had intercepted and destroyed a ballistic missile launched by the Iran-aligned Houthis toward the southern region. The coalition had already said Sunday it intercepted a drone and a ballistic missile toward the same region. On the Yemeni side, Houthi health ministry spokesperson Anees al-Asbahi said coalition strikes on Sunday hit Ras Isa port in Hodeidah province, injuring four civilians, and an oil company station in the Bajil district, injuring three more — figures that come from Houthi officials and have not been independently verified, and which the coalition has not commented on specifically. Houthi-run al-Masirah TV had earlier reported the Ras Isa strikes hit a pier and a moored commercial vessel, plus a telecommunications facility southeast of Saada. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Sarea claimed 75 air and missile strikes hit eight provinces over 24 hours and put the total since the latest escalation began at 3,830 — again, Houthi figures, not independently verified.

The two sides' stated targets tell the story of the escalation's logic. The Houthis have repeatedly targeted Saudi airports, air defence facilities and energy infrastructure, including the Shaybah oilfield and the East-West pipeline and Yanbu export facilities, according to Reuters; Saudi strikes have concentrated on Houthi-held ports, fuel infrastructure and command sites in Hodeidah and elsewhere. The human impact beyond the battlefield is now measurable in a different register: the Philippines' Department of Migrant Workers said Monday it has extended 6,873 services to 5,257 overseas Filipino workers across Saudi Arabia as strikes intensified, after an OFW was killed by missile fragments when Saudi defences intercepted a Houthi drone targeting the Shaybah oilfield. The UN figures cited in Houthi coverage put more than 300,000 people displaced within Yemen by the renewed fighting.

For ordinary people in southern Saudi Arabia, the alerts-then-all-clear rhythm is itself the disruption — warnings for cities like Abha and Taif, followed by interceptions, followed by silence — while in Yemen's Hodeidah province, a working port and an oil station are the places where the war lands on civilian livelihoods.

In short: Monday brought another intercepted Houthi missile and alerts across four Saudi cities, matched by Houthi claims of seven civilians injured at Ras Isa port and a Bajil oil facility — the same tit-for-tat exchange, with the civilian and migrant-worker toll now more clearly documented than the military claims on either side.

Israel's coalition race sharpens: Liberman backs Eisenkot, Netanyahu keeps unity door ajar

Israel's post-election deadlock moved decisively on Monday — toward Gadi Eisenkot, not toward a Netanyahu-led government — while Netanyahu himself kept a second, contradictory track open: reported interest in a unity government with Eisenkot, guaranteed by Trump and Herzog, even as he publicly denies planning one.

The details, as reported by Kan, Channel 12, i24NEWS and the Jerusalem Post via several outlets, show the arithmetic shifting. Yisrael Beytenu chairman Avigdor Liberman confirmed Monday he will recommend Eisenkot to form the next government, saying, "The world will turn upside down before I sit with Netanyahu," even if offered first place in a rotation, and ruling out a minority government as well. Liberman's seven seats matter because the final results give Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats, the Jewish opposition parties 48 and the Arab-led parties 19, with 61 of 120 needed to govern; N12 counts 33 recommendations for Eisenkot from Yashar and B'Yachad, rising to 48 with the Democrats and Yisrael Beytenu, and potentially 67 if the Arab-led parties recommend him as expected. Liberman also proposed former prime minister Yair Lapid as Knesset Speaker, a post the opposition bloc plans to take from Likud's Amir Ohana at the November 10 swearing-in. Meanwhile, Kan reported via a Netanyahu confidant, Dimona mayor Benny Biton, that "Benjamin Netanyahu wants unity… this is neither deception nor an election manoeuvre," with a Likud official saying Trump and Herzog would act as guarantors of an agreement with Eisenkot — a claim that sits uneasily beside Netanyahu's public denials and Likud whip Ofir Katz's November 1 ruling-out of a rotation deal. Eisenkot, for his part, has set conditions for coalition partners and vowed to use his Knesset majority to reverse Netanyahu-era policies, though reports do not specify the four principles he is said to require or the exact policies targeted.

The two sides are therefore arguing about different things: Eisenkot's camp is counting recommendations and planning a legislative programme, while Netanyahu's camp is floating a unity arrangement whose guarantors, terms and very existence are all reported rather than confirmed. President Isaac Herzog receives the final results on Wednesday, November 4, begins consultations that day with smaller parties, meets Yashar and Likud on November 5, and must tap a candidate by November 11, who then gets 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension, to form a government.

For ordinary Israelis, the stakes are practical: another round of elections if no one reaches 61, versus a government — of whichever shape — that can pass a budget and make decisions on the war, the economy and the host of domestic issues frozen since the election. Liberman's move makes the Eisenkot-first path the most likely one to be tried first; it does not make it a done deal, given his own long refusal to sit with Arab-led parties, whose recommendations Eisenkot may still need.

In short: Liberman's backing puts Eisenkot on course to get the first mandate from Herzog, with consultations starting Wednesday — while Netanyahu's reported unity feeler, with Trump and Herzog as guarantors, remains unconfirmed and publicly denied, leaving the minority-government-versus-unity-government choice exactly where it was, only with the numbers now leaning Eisenkot's way.

Cybersecurity

  • Ivanti deadline is TODAY — patch and rebuild, don't just patch: The federal deadline for Ivanti Connect Secure CVE-2026-111357 (CVSS 8.8, missing authentication for a critical function) falls Monday, November 2. It is one of three flaws chained by the previously unknown actor UTA0576, tracked by Volexity, to reach root on VPN appliances without logging in — the chain ran from October 3 against even a fully patched appliance. Fixes are 25.1.1.3 and 22.8R2.6 (shipped October 29); CISA requires forensic triage for this one, not only a patch, and warns its Integrity Checker Tool may miss this activity — so affected organisations face rebuilds, not just upgrades. The second flaw in the chain, CVE-2026-118527 (4.3), is due November 13. Sources: SecurityWeek, SOCRadar, HOL triage notes
  • CISA adds GitLab and Cisco flaws to KEV: CISA added GitLab CVE-2026-97229 (CVSS 10.0, unauthenticated RCE via CI/CD regex validation; fixed in 19.2.8/19.3.5/19.4.2) and Cisco ASA/FTD CVE-2026-76418 (8.6, NULL-pointer dereference causing denial of service) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog, citing ongoing exploitation; Alpine Linux reported an unidentified actor attempted to exploit its unpatched GitLab system, causing worker crashes, with later attempts blocked. Note: the GitLab flaw itself was patched October 12 and covered then — the KEV addition is the new part. Source: DEV Community summary
  • Binance — reserve proof now verifiable, SAFU Bitcoin halved, next withdrawal phase Tuesday: Binance's Recovery Update #5 says the full zero-knowledge proof for its October 31 proof-of-reserves snapshot is complete, so holders of the ten fully backed tokens (BTC, ETH, USDT, BNB, SOL, USDC, XRP, USD1, RLUSD, ZEC) can now verify their own balances; the exchange says about 95% of the stolen assets are covered. CoinTelegraph reports Binance's disclosed SAFU Bitcoin wallet held about 7,500 BTC (~$583 million) on Monday, down from about 15,000 BTC before a 7,499 BTC transfer Saturday linked to the partial deployment of its user-protection fund — with no stated timeline for replenishing it. The next withdrawal phase is planned for November 3, with another snapshot covering more tokens planned for Wednesday. Separately, DeFiLlama data cited by Bitcoin.com shows Binance's tracked assets down about $16.6 billion since the October 29 hack ($144.9B to ~$128.3B), while Binance's own count put Sunday's net outflow at $3.64 billion ($4.65B withdrawn, $1.01B deposited in the first 9.5 hours) — the two counts measure different things, so don't read them as contradicting each other. Sources: CoinTelegraph, Bitcoin.com, CompareCex tracker
