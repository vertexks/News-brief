World escalation

The Oleshky truce ends — honored to the last hour, and now the question is whether the mechanism survives it

The six-day local ceasefire around the Russian-held town of Oleshky expired Sunday evening, and Ukraine's ombudsman said afterward that both sides had honored their commitments throughout — the longest local pause since Russia's full-scale invasion began, ending without a reported violation, and with no announced successor.

The details, as reported by Reuters, show a final day spent getting the last people out before the clock ran out. Ukraine's military said it evacuated 167 people on Sunday from threatened areas of southern Kherson region that had been under daily shelling — including 22 children and 21 people with limited mobility — two days after busloads of Oleshky residents reached Ukrainian-controlled territory on Saturday via Crimea, Anapa and Belarus. The ceasefire, brokered with mediation by US first lady Melania Trump and involvement from Pope Leo, was due to expire at midnight Moscow time (2100 GMT). Kherson governor Oleksandr Prokudin urged residents to stay out of the Dnipro "red zones," warning that "the intensity of Russian strikes there remains high, and the threat to life is extremely high."

The two sides' accounts of what the truce means now point in different directions. Kyiv's ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, who personally escorted evacuees out of Oleshky, said after the ceasefire ended that "both sides honored their commitments" and that "it is important to maintain this mechanism of cooperation so that we can continue helping people and saving lives" — language aimed at turning a one-off into a repeatable channel, with Lubinets telling CNN he hopes it can serve as inspiration for other cut-off villages facing the same food and medicine shortages. Moscow, for its part, took a narrower view: Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, quoted by Russian news agencies, said Russia had a positive view of how the Oleshky operation was conducted and of the coordination between the two countries' human rights commissioners — praise for the operation, not a commitment to repeat it. Pope Leo said he hoped the Oleshky cooperation would pave the way for broader peace efforts, and Melania Trump wrote on X that the ceasefire "has provided residents with the freedom to move, access to food, clean water, medicine, plus a renewed sense of dignity."

For ordinary people, the end of the truce is the return of the war to a place that had six quiet days. An estimated 2,000 civilians were in Oleshky when the truce took hold, of a pre-war population of around 24,000; some chose to stay, some went to Russia, and the rest made the long journey out — Saturday's arrivals included people who told CNN they had not had bread for more than a year and had survived on what they grew or found in abandoned apartments. Elsewhere, the war did not pause for the truce's final day: Reuters reports regional governors and emergency services counted one death in a strike in the Sumy region and at least one in Vinnytsia on Sunday, and in Kyiv, DTEK introduced emergency power outages on Sunday as the capital's grid struggled after repeated strikes — the daytime fallout of the overnight barrage covered in the morning brief.

In short: the Oleshky local truce expired Sunday evening having held without violations, with a final 167 people evacuated from shelling-threatened Kherson areas the same day — and with Kyiv pushing to keep the cooperation mechanism alive, Moscow praising only the operation itself, and the people who stayed in Oleshky now back under the threat Prokudin warned about.

Doha talks enter a third day with no deal — while a Qatari gas carrier is hit despite reported Iranian clearance

Indirect talks in Doha between Iranian negotiators and Qatari mediators entered a third day on Sunday with no agreement on reopening the Strait of Hormuz, even as Gulf states brace for what happens if the talks fail — and even as a Qatari LNG carrier was confirmed hit in the Gulf on Friday, reportedly hours after Tehran had cleared its passage.

The details, as reported by Reuters and carried in the Guardian's Sunday coverage, show a proposal narrowing but not closing. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi described a Qatari-mediated plan under which the strait could reopen within two days once Washington meets Tehran's conditions — down from an earlier seven-day implementation window — with the release of more than $6 billion in Iranian assets, including funds held in Qatar, at the center of the arrangement. Araghchi called a letter from US Vice President JD Vance a "positive step" but said Iran sees "no need to meet with the Americans at this stage," and no settlement has been announced. President Donald Trump said Saturday that negotiations were "going fine" but that Washington was "not in that much of a hurry," and the Guardian reports Gulf officials are "braced for the worst" ahead of Tuesday's US midterm elections, after which the US moratorium on strikes against Iran is set to expire.

The two sides' positions are still far apart on sequencing. Tehran's conditions, as laid out by Araghchi and by Supreme National Security Council secretary Mohsen Rezaei, include lifting sanctions imposed after the collapse of the earlier Islamabad memorandum, reducing US forces in the region, restoring oil waivers, releasing frozen funds, and recognition of an Oman-discussed shipping route — Rezaei has said the strait stays closed and no talks happen unless all seven conditions are met. Washington, by contrast, has not publicly accepted the linkage between sanctions relief and reopening, and US Central Command said Saturday its blockade of Iranian ports had redirected 144 vessels — a pressure campaign Tehran wants lifted first. The Al Mayeda incident sharpens the disagreement over whether any route is actually safe: owner Nakilat confirmed Sunday that its LNG carrier was hit by unidentified objects in the Gulf on Friday evening, with no crew injuries and no environmental damage and the vessel still operational — while Bloomberg, via reporting carried Sunday, said Pakistan had negotiated safe passage for the Pakistan-bound cargo with Iranian officials only hours before the strike. No one has been identified as responsible, and Qatar's Foreign Ministry condemned the attack and opened an investigation.

For ordinary people, the stakes are winter energy and prices. LNG shipments through the strait ran at roughly 30% of pre-war volumes in October, according to reporting Sunday, and the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre has counted 81 projectile strikes in the strait since July 6, with October the heaviest month of the war — the kind of risk that has already pushed war-risk insurance premiums and freight costs sharply higher and left Brent near $117. A Pakistan-bound LNG cargo turning back toward the Gulf after a strike, even with no one hurt, is exactly the disruption that feeds those costs and, for importers like Pakistan, Japan and South Korea, the worry about winter supply.

Caption: The Qatari LNG carrier Al Mayeda, shown in an archive photo, which owner Nakilat confirmed Sunday was hit by unidentified objects in the Gulf on Friday evening — no crew hurt, the vessel still operational — reportedly hours after its Pakistan-bound passage had been cleared, which is why the incident matters to the Doha talks even though no attacker has been identified.

In short: Doha's third day ended with a two-day reopening timetable on the table but no agreement, Gulf states bracing for failure ahead of Tuesday's US midterms, and a Qatari gas carrier hit despite reported Iranian clearance — a reminder that, deal or no deal, the waterway's safety is still the open question.

Cybersecurity

  • New CVSS 10.0 — LXD/MicroCloud auth bypass (CVE-2026-112816): A flaw in Canonical's LXD and MicroCloud, published October 30, lets an attacker pose as a trusted client on the LXD network API without that client's private key — giving access to the cluster and the VMs it runs. Fixed in LXD 6.10.1, 5.21.9, 5.0.11 and 4.0.15 and MicroCloud 3.4.1 and 2.1.5; no known exploitation and not on CISA KEV as of November 1. If you run LXD, patch the snaps. Source: Carthage Electronics November 1 CVE Threat Brief
  • Binance afternoon update — all 15 phase-one networks back, $3.64B net outflow: Binance said that by 12:36 UTC Sunday all 15 networks and assets in phase one were back online, and that between 06:00 and 15:30 UTC it processed $4.649 billion in withdrawals against $1.01 billion in deposits — a net outflow of about $3.64 billion, roughly 2.1% of the $171 billion it reports in total assets. The refreshed proof-of-reserves report is due November 2 (per earlier reporting, full cryptographic proof November 3). Remaining networks still return in phases as security checks complete. Source: SpendNode on Binance's afternoon status post
  • No new KEV additions October 31 or November 1: CISA's catalog still stands at the October 30 version (1,766 entries). Reminder: the Ivanti Connect Secure flaw CVE-2026-111357 (part of the UTA0576 unauthenticated-root chain) is due Monday, November 2 for federal agencies — and for everyone else, that's the patch-now deadline to beat. Source: Carthage Electronics November 1 CVE Threat Brief
