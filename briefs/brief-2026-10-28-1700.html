World escalation

Netanyahu's lead holds as the count nears completion — but still no majority

Israel's election count has moved from the morning's partial picture to a near-final one, and the headline has not changed: Benjamin Netanyahu is leading, and nobody has a majority. With about 85% of ballots counted, projections gave Netanyahu's bloc 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, short of the 61 needed to govern, according to one Wednesday report; a live tracker with 97.63% counted put it at 53 for Netanyahu's bloc, 48 for the Change bloc led by Gadi Eisenkot, and 19 for the Arab parties. Likud itself is the largest single party, with about 26 seats on roughly 21% of the vote, ahead of Eisenkot's Yashar on about 21. The remaining double-envelope ballots — soldiers' votes and others — are due to be counted from Wednesday night into Thursday, with definitive results expected Thursday and official results by November 4, so the seat numbers can still shift at the margins, particularly for parties near the 3.25% threshold.

The two camps are drawing opposite conclusions from the same count. Netanyahu, who called overnight for a "strong, right-wing national government," has also raised the prospect of a renewed joint move against Iran with the United States, calling it a "singular historical opportunity" — and the New York Post reports that observers now see a third act for the Iran war as almost certain if he stays, with one INSS Iran expert quoted as saying Tehran fears Netanyahu precisely because he talks about attacking Iran again. Eisenkot, for his part, has acknowledged Netanyahu remains caretaker prime minister but says he has lost public support, has urged him to retire from politics, and has warned him not to use security for political purposes. President Isaac Herzog, who will consult the party groups before choosing who gets the first attempt at forming a government, has urged leaders to consider different options and avoid another election.

For ordinary Israelis, the practical consequence is a stretch of caretaker government while coalition talks drag on — with the Iran confrontation, the Gaza aftermath and Netanyahu's own corruption trial all sitting with a government that has lost its old majority. The Arab parties, at 18–19 seats and nearly double their 2022 result, hold the balance of power in arithmetic terms even though both main camps have so far ruled out relying on them; Eisenkot has resisted governing with them, and Netanyahu has rejected a government backed by them. If neither bloc can assemble 61 seats, another election in 2027 becomes likely.

In short: the count is nearly done and Netanyahu leads 53–48–19, but no bloc reaches 61, the Arab parties hold the balance, and the decisive questions — who gets the mandate from Herzog, and whether Netanyahu's lead hardens into a government that can move on Iran — come after Thursday's near-final result.

Aden airport hit twice in one day as the Yemen war's civilian toll climbs

The Houthi war against Saudi Arabia and Yemen's government escalated again on Wednesday, with Aden International Airport — the main gateway for the government-held south — hit twice in a single day. Houthi missiles targeted the airport twice on October 28, the first while a Saudi military plane was on the ground; police said the plane left unharmed, according to one report. Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree had earlier ordered an immediate evacuation of the airport, alleging a Saudi cargo plane was moving weapons through it. The airport has now been attacked four times this month, including two ballistic missiles on October 24, and Médecins Sans Frontières said one of its aircraft was about to take off from Aden a few days ago when humanitarian workers were told to seek shelter as a missile struck nearby — the charity stressed that safe access to the airport is crucial because it is the main entry point for humanitarian workers, supplies and medical equipment to southern Yemen.

The other side of Wednesday's ledger came from Houthi-run media, which reported Saudi airstrikes on Al Hazm in the northern Jawf region killing 14 people and wounding 10, with women and children among the reported casualties — a toll that is preliminary and comes from Houthi sources, not independently verified. Reuters' own Wednesday account, carried by regional outlets, confirms the Aden strikes and the evacuation warning, and notes the renewed fighting has shattered a 2022 UN-brokered truce that had largely halted major attacks in the conflict. Aid agencies warn the latest violence is rapidly worsening conditions in a country already suffering from one of the world's worst humanitarian crises, with nearly 260,000 people reported displaced across Yemen since August.

For ordinary people, the airport attacks hit twice over: they endanger the humanitarian lifeline into the south, and they sit alongside the Saudi-side strikes that have now killed a reported 20+ people in the past week of airport attacks, plus the debris damage in Riyadh from interceptions. The Houthis present the Aden strikes as attacks on a military target; the government and aid agencies present them as a threat to civil aviation and to the aid pipeline. Neither framing changes the immediate fact: flights, aid and medical evacuations through Aden are now operating under repeated missile warnings.

In short: Aden airport was hit twice on Wednesday with an evacuation order, Houthi media report 14 killed in a Saudi strike on Jawf, and the airport — the south's humanitarian lifeline — is now a recurring target. Watch whether flights and aid movements through Aden continue or halt, and whether the coalition responds in kind.

Kharkiv hit twice in 24 hours as Ukraine strikes Russian steel and chemical plants

The war's long-range exchange continued on both sides on Wednesday. In Kharkiv, a Russian drone strike around 1 p.m. local time hit the Osnovianskyi district, killing a 60-year-old man and injuring three others, according to regional head Oleh Syniehubov as reported by Ukrinform — the second strike on the same neighbourhood in 24 hours, after a jet-powered drone late Tuesday evening set fires across some 2,500 square metres of residential and industrial terrain, injuring five people including a four-year-old child. City officials condemned the consecutive attacks on a single district, noting first responders had spent hours clearing the scene between the two strikes under dangerous, exposed conditions.

Ukraine, meanwhile, launched a major overnight drone attack into Russia on the night of October 27–28, targeting the country's largest steel plant and a chemical facility, according to the National Security Journal and other reports. Fires were reported at industrial sites in Lipetsk and Tula regions, with Ukrainian monitoring channels reporting a fire near the Novolipetsk Steel Plant and flames at the Novomoskovsk Azot chemical plant, one of Russia's largest nitrogen fertilizer makers. Moscow claimed to have intercepted more than 500 Ukrainian drones; Lipetsk's governor said 45 drones were shot down over his region and downplayed reports of a major industrial fire, attributing a fire to debris igniting in a wooded area. Ukraine has not formally claimed responsibility, and the full extent of the damage is still unclear — the two sides' accounts diverge sharply, as they usually do on strike damage.

The human impact runs in both directions. In Kharkiv, the repeated strikes on one district mean residents and responders are being hit while still clearing the previous attack. In Russia, the targeting of steel and fertilizer production is aimed at industrial capacity, but debris from interceptions damaged an apartment building in Lipetsk, according to the governor's own account. On the front itself, Ukraine's General Staff reported 191 combat engagements over the past day, with Russian losses claimed at 1,510 personnel — figures from one side, unverified independently. The Oleshky humanitarian operation remains the scheduled test for Thursday and Friday: demining Thursday, aid from October 29, evacuations planned Friday.

In short: Kharkiv's Osnovianskyi district was hit twice in a day, Ukraine hit back at Russian steel and chemical plants overnight with damage still disputed, and the front-line fighting has not eased. Watch Thursday for whether the Oleshky demining actually happens — that, more than the strike claims, is the next verifiable event.

Apple's first touchscreen MacBook is real — and $400 dearer than the model it sits above

Apple on Wednesday unveiled its first touchscreen MacBook, ending years in which the company insisted it would not combine the iPad and Mac experiences. At its "Pro and tell" event in Los Angeles, Apple debuted the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display in 14-inch and 16-inch sizes, with a tandem OLED touchscreen, a Dynamic Island replacing the notch, and the same M5 Pro and M5 Max chips as the MacBook Pros announced in March, according to MacRumors and USA Today. The display offers up to 2,000 nits of HDR and SDR brightness, a 10,000,000:1 contrast ratio, and ProMotion refresh from 10Hz to 120Hz, with an optional nano-texture finish. A touch sensor built directly into the display panel means no added latency, Apple says, and macOS automatically translates touch into clicks, so many existing apps work with touch right away — Canva, Zoom, Microsoft Word, PowerPoint, Adobe Lightroom, DaVinci Resolve and Blender are named as already compatible.

Caption: Apple's new MacBook Pro lineup with the PROLED XDR touchscreen display, shown in Apple's launch imagery. The touchscreen is the headline change — the first time Apple has put touch on a Mac — and it arrives with a thinner, lighter design and a Dynamic Island in place of the notch.

The pricing is where the two sides of the story meet. The 14-inch starts at $2,899 and the 16-inch at $3,399, with M5 Max configurations from $4,499 and $4,799 respectively — $400 above the mini-LED models they sit alongside, which Apple is keeping in the lineup for now, as is the separate $1,999 M6 14-inch MacBook Pro announced Tuesday. The new machines are lighter (three pounds for the 14-inch, four for the 16-inch), up to 1mm slimmer, with a redesigned hinge for touch stability, up to 128GB of unified memory, up to 16TB of storage, a six-speaker system with 50% deeper bass, three Thunderbolt 5 ports, HDMI, SDXC, MagSafe, Wi-Fi 7 and Bluetooth 6, and up to 24 hours of battery life. Pre-orders opened Wednesday, with launch on Friday, November 6.

For ordinary buyers, the practical question is whether touch is worth the premium: Apple positions it as an additional input alongside keyboard and trackpad, not a replacement, and the machines run the same M5 Pro/Max silicon as the March models — so the $400 buys the display, the touch layer, the thinner chassis and the Dynamic Island, not faster chips. For professionals who already live in the named touch-compatible apps, the pitch is a real workflow change; for everyone else, the kept-alive mini-LED models and the $1,999 M6 machine are the cheaper way into a new MacBook Pro.

In short: Apple's first touchscreen Mac is here, it is a genuine reversal of a long-held position, and it costs $400 more than the equivalent non-touch model for the same chips. Pre-orders are open now, machines land November 6 — the first hands-on reviews will say whether the touch layer earns its premium.

Sources: MacRumors, October 28; USA Today, October 28

Cybersecurity

  • Fragnesia: Linux kernel flaw added to CISA's exploited list, deadline Saturday. CISA added CVE-2026-46300 (Linux kernel, dubbed Fragnesia) to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog on October 28, with a federal deadline of October 31. Per SecurityWeek/The Hacker News via the daily threat brief, attackers are chaining it with the PHP2Shell flaws to plant PHP webshells and escalate to root; security firm Dream says a suspected China-linked group used the chain against 25 internet-facing firewalls on October 23–24, then used two of them to reach into a US government-affiliated network and target Active Directory. If you run Linux servers with PHP, patch PHP and the kernel and reboot so the new kernel actually runs. Source: Carthage Electronics threat brief, October 28

It requires admin access and is rated low severity (CVSS 2.7) — the risk is that an admin-level attacker could perform operations without being logged. Broadcom fixed it July 29 in VMSA-2026-0006. Major court cases

Brand under cross-examination: "reckless philandering" admitted, key details disputed

Russell Brand's defence case moved into its testing phase on Wednesday at Southwark Crown Court, as prosecutor Riel Karmy-Jones KC cross-examined him after his morning evidence in chief. Brand, 51, denies three counts of rape, three counts of sexual assault and one count of attempted indecent assault involving six women between 1999 and 2009. Under cross-examination he admitted he had "misused" his fame for "reckless philandering," telling the court he had been "persuasive, seductive, begging and flirting, and dedicated a lot of my life to what seems now beyond reckless, philandering and idiocy" — while insisting consent was "non-negotiable" and that he never forced himself on a woman. He also admitted he was "obsessed" with sex in his younger years but denied being unable to function without it, and denied pretending to be gay to make women trust him.

The prosecution's line of attack, as reported by The Times and The Guardian, is that Brand's account has shifted. Karmy-Jones pressed him on alleged inconsistencies between his defence statement and his testimony about the alleged 2009 anal rape of Woman 5, including details such as sex toys and "sexual talk" that he said he recollected after seeing her witness statement. Brand conceded the point in striking terms: "Since these events occurred, there's been escalating recollection. It's been a deeply traumatic episode of my life I have focused on at the expense of my marriage, my children and health. I recognise that this recollection includes small details that are inconsistent and I acknowledge that." He also rejected a former friend's claim that he had a goal of sleeping with 5,000 women — "No, not a goal" — and said he did not recall the conversation in question. On the origins of the case, he said he blamed the Channel 4 Dispatches documentary and Times/Sunday Times reporting for the allegations, answering "Yes" when asked if he blamed the documentary, and "In some ways" when pressed on blaming the media generally.

The two framings now in front of the jury are clear. The prosecution presents a man whose recollections grew after reading the evidence, whose admitted pursuit of women was relentless, and whose defence rests on consent in encounters the complainants describe very differently. The defence presents a man whose younger behaviour was reckless but consensual, whose life has been consumed by the case — he told the court in the morning that not a single person would give evidence on his behalf — and who argues the publicity itself shaped the prosecution. Neither framing settles the seven counts; the jury must decide each one separately, and Brand spent about four hours on the stand on Wednesday, with cross-examination continuing.

In short: Brand admitted misusing fame for reckless philandering but denied every charge, conceded his recollections contain inconsistencies, and blamed the 2023 documentary for the case — and the cross-examination, not his morning statement, is where the defence case is now being tested.
