World escalation

Ukraine update: aid-centre toll rises to 31 as outages and water cuts spread

The death toll from Monday's strike on the humanitarian aid centre and shelter in Komyshuvakha, in the Zaporizhzhia region, has risen to 31, with more than 45 people still missing, according to the New York Post's wire report. Rescuers were still searching the cratered site on Tuesday. The overnight barrage itself — 289 drones, 17 ballistic missiles and 59 cruise missiles, as President Volodymyr Zelensky reported — is the same wave covered in this morning's brief; what changed during the day is the scale of the civilian fallout now being counted.

Ukraine's national energy company said Russia attacked its energy facilities more than 20 times, forcing emergency power outages in seven regions including Kyiv, and scheduled cuts elsewhere left some consumers without electricity for more than 12 hours — the first such outages this autumn, according to Ukrainska Pravda via the Post. Energy Minister Denys Shmyhal said Russia also hit a substation critical to the Khmelnytskyi Nuclear Power Plant, calling it a threat to the nuclear safety of all of Europe and saying he had spoken to the International Atomic Energy Agency. Major water supply facilities left without power interrupted Kyiv's water supply. DTEK, Ukraine's largest private generator, called it one of the most serious attacks ever on its facilities, with three of its five thermal plants halted. The UN Secretary-General condemned the continuing attacks on population centres, including strikes on a medical facility and a hospice in Kharkiv and Dnipro, and noted the record civilian casualties.

Diplomatically, the day moved in two directions at once. Zelensky travelled to Germany for talks with European leaders ahead of next week's US-brokered talks, and Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed on Tuesday that a Russian delegation will attend the Abu Dhabi talks on November 10–11 — the first such talks since February. No ceasefire or de-escalation was announced by either side.

Sources: New York Post wire on the toll rising to 31, the outages, the water cuts and the Khmelnytskyi substation, Reuters via KTWB on the barrage and the Yaroslavl refinery fire

Iran update: "no rush" from Trump, "far from a deal" from Tehran — while the Qeshm strike stays unconfirmed on the record

No government has put the Qeshm Island strike on the record. As of Tuesday afternoon US time, multiple US officials had told CNN, AP, the BBC/CBS and others that the US carried out limited "defensive" strikes on Qeshm on Monday after Iranian surface-to-air fire against US aircraft or assets, targeting radar and missile launchers — the Wall Street Journal first reported the operation — but US Central Command had issued no formal public statement and Iranian authorities had not formally confirmed a US attack on the island, according to NewsCubby's Tuesday update and The Media Line. IRNA reported several explosions on Qeshm on Monday night.

The two sides' public lines pulled in opposite directions on Tuesday. Trump told Axios on Monday there is "no rush" to decide on new strikes — "one way or the other, we will do something" — and told NBC News he was not considering striking Iran after the midterms, while also again raising Pickaxe Mountain, the suspected underground nuclear site, as a target. A senior Iranian official told Reuters the two sides are far from a deal and that Tehran will not accept Washington's "excessive demands," and parliamentary speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf said Iran had given the US a "strong and rational response" and that "the ball is in the US' court." Qatari mediators were due in Tehran on Tuesday night to meet Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, according to Reuters via The Media Line. Oil, which fell sharply on Monday — Brent down 7.6% to $104.77, its biggest one-day drop since July — gave back part of that fall on Tuesday after the strike news broke.

Sources: NewsCubby on the officials' accounts, Trump's "no rush" and the oil move, The Media Line on the strikes, the mediator visit and Vance's "some progress", Cornerstone Futures morning note on Tehran's "far from a deal" line

Saudi–Houthi: reported cross-border ground raids, and the Asian Cup postponed

A new report says the Houthi war has crossed a line beyond missiles and drones. Middle East Eye, citing a Western and an Arab official with direct knowledge, reports that Houthi fighters have launched multiple small-scale cross-border ground raids into Saudi Arabia's mountainous southwest in recent months, targeting border posts, with at least one raid as recently as the end of October — and that the raiders appear to have captured small arms and surveillance equipment used only by Saudi border forces. That account is relayed by OilPrice/ZeroHedge and has not been confirmed by Riyadh or the Houthis, so treat the scale as reported, not established. Separately, Bloomberg reports that the Asian Football Confederation has postponed its flagship tournament in Saudi Arabia until late 2027, citing "exceptional circumstances arising from developments in the region" — the latest major event to slip as Houthi attacks continue. On the diplomatic side, Saudi Defence Minister Prince Khalid bin Salman discussed de-escalation efforts with US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth by phone on Monday, and the coalition said it intercepted two drones targeting the Riyadh region on Monday night.

Sources: OilPrice/ZeroHedge relaying the Middle East Eye raid report and the Asian Cup postponement, Virgin Radio Dubai on the Saudi–US defence call and the Monday-night interceptions

Israel update: Shas backs Netanyahu as Herzog's consultations open Wednesday

The opposition's Eisenkot plan, covered in full this morning, gained a counterweight on Tuesday: the ultra-Orthodox Shas party said it will recommend Benjamin Netanyahu to President Isaac Herzog, citing his commitment to regulating the status of Torah students, according to Yedioth Ahronoth via The Insight International. Herzog begins consultations with parliamentary factions on Wednesday — starting with the smaller parties, with Likud last on Thursday evening — and the Central Elections Committee is expected to announce final official results on Wednesday. The new Knesset is sworn in on November 10. Nothing in Tuesday's announcements changes the arithmetic from this morning: Netanyahu's bloc 53, the Jewish opposition 48, the Arab-led parties 19, with 61 needed. KAN also reported on Monday that Eisenkot has ruled out serving in a Netanyahu-led government.

Major court cases

Poland's top court splits in two — eight judges meet, seven stay away, and both sides claim the law

Poland's Constitutional Tribunal held its disputed general assembly on Tuesday, and the result was two courts claiming to be one. Eight judges, all elected by the current Sejm, met in Warsaw under Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, who suspended the tribunal's president and deputy on October 30. They ruled that they formed a quorum by counting 12 eligible judges — leaving out the suspended president Bogdan Święczkowski and deputy Bartłomiej Sochański, plus Judge Jarosław Wyrembak, whose election Korwin-Piotrowska called defective — and then adopted the tribunal's draft 2027 budget and drew lots for the disciplinary panels that will hear the cases of Święczkowski and Sochański. That is what RMF24 and Notes from Poland reported, via Crisis.Zone's live tracker. The other seven judges, including Święczkowski, stayed away; he dismissed the meeting as a "social gathering," and constitutional law expert Ryszard Piotrowski called it illegal.

The details behind the quorum fight matter because the whole crisis turns on a counting question. The tribunal has 15 seats, and a general assembly normally needs 10 judges present. Only eight judges elected by the current Sejm exist, so a 10-of-15 quorum was impossible without the other camp — which is why the eight redefined the denominator to 12. The budget vote was the practical trigger: without an approved revenue-and-expenditure plan, the tribunal's funds were not in next year's draft budget, and two earlier attempts to hold the assembly failed for lack of quorum, according to Interia's report of the meeting. Justice Minister Waldemar Żurek told Dziennik Gazeta Prawna that his immunity-lifting motions do not need a quorum, though adopting the budget does — and on Tuesday he filed a sixth motion to lift Święczkowski's immunity. Prosecutors separately opened an investigation into the alleged assault on judges Korwin-Piotrowska and Krystian Markiewicz at the building's entrance during President Karol Nawrocki's entry on October 31.

Each side's legal argument is now on the record, and neither has been tested before a body both accept. The government-backed judges say the Sejm's power to elect judges is constitutional, that the suspended leadership blocked the court from functioning, and that the European Commission and the Venice Commission back parliament's appointment power — the Commission said on Saturday that appointing the eight new members lies within the Sejm's constitutional power. The other camp, backed by the Supreme Court's College, says a suspension from duties does not end the president's term or create a vacancy, so Korwin-Piotrowska cannot lawfully direct the court; the College adopted a resolution on Tuesday saying the move to suspend the president and vice-president lacked a legal basis, with all 55 judges present backing it, according to PolandInsight. Nawrocki's camp calls the whole episode a forcible takeover — the tribunal's X account has used the word "coup" — while Prime Minister Donald Tusk has said restoring the rule of law is a duty and that there can be no compromise. The Supreme Court's own general assembly on Tuesday fell short of the quorum needed to adopt a resolution, leaving the College's statement as the judiciary's main counterweight.

For ordinary Poles, the immediate stakes are less abstract than the quorum maths. A paralysed constitutional court cannot rule on the laws parliament passes, the budget process for the court itself was the tripwire, and the standoff is already spilling into the street and the currency market: riot police were deployed at the court on Friday, protesters have gathered outside it for days, and Commerzbank noted on Tuesday that the institutional conflict is weighing on the zloty, with EUR/PLN briefly reaching 4.4372. With a general election a year away, both camps are also fighting over who gets to define the crisis — restoration of the rule of law, or a coup against a constitutional body.

In short: eight judges met, called themselves a quorum by counting 12 instead of 15, passed the 2027 budget and set disciplinary panels in motion against the suspended president and deputy — while the other seven judges stayed away, the Supreme Court's College said the suspensions had no legal basis, prosecutors opened an assault investigation, and the question of who lawfully runs Poland's top court remains unanswered by any body both sides accept.

Sources: Crisis.Zone live tracker on the assembly, the quorum ruling, the budget vote and the prosecutors' investigation, PolandInsight on the Supreme Court College's resolution, Interia via ByteSeu on the meeting's purpose and the earlier failed quorums
