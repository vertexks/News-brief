World escalation

Trump plays a waiting game as Iran raises the stakes — and the Houthis now hold the whole Bab el-Mandab Strait

Iran fired drones at a US base in Bahrain for the second time in three days, its Houthi allies consolidated full control of the Bab el-Mandab Strait, and a tanker was attacked in the Gulf on Sunday — while President Donald Trump signalled he is in no hurry to answer, telling reporters he is handling Iran "a little bit longer way, a bit more civilly" and that "we don't need a deal with Iran." The day's events sharpened the standoff the 5 PM brief covered: Tehran is testing whether Washington will actually hit back, and so far the answer has been no.

The details filled in through the evening. Bahrain's military said its air defences intercepted and destroyed several Iranian drones on Sunday, after the Revolutionary Guards said they had launched six drones at a US air-defence radar at the Al-Riffa helicopter base, calling it retaliation for "hostile US actions." The US Embassy in Bahrain urged Americans to exercise "heightened vigilance," citing an increased threat from Iran-backed Houthis and Iranian efforts to target US officials and facilities, and warned of possible flight cancellations and airspace closures. CNN reported that Trump has cancelled Vice President JD Vance's efforts to revive talks with Iran and told Vance to focus on domestic issues instead. The Wall Street Journal reported on Saturday, citing US officials, that Iran still holds an estimated 1,500 to 2,500 ballistic missiles despite months of American and Israeli strikes on its weapons infrastructure.

In Yemen, the picture hardened in the Houthis' favour. The New York Post reported that the Saudi-backed government forces' withdrawal leaves the Houthis in full control of the Bab el-Mandab Strait, the southern exit to the Red Sea through which about $1 trillion in goods passes each year. Houthi fighters have also nearly surrounded Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city, where aid cannot get in and civilians cannot get out, and have pushed to within about 60 kilometres of Aden, the seat of the internationally recognised government. The Houthis added fresh claims on Sunday: spokesman Yahya Saree said the group fired six ballistic missiles at Saudi forces at Qa'wa camp in Lahj governorate, and that drones hit two pumping stations on the East-West oil pipeline in the Riyadh region. Saree also claimed the group shot down a Saudi armed reconnaissance aircraft over al-Jawf — unverified, with no Saudi response. The coalition said on Sunday it had intercepted two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and three drones over the Eastern Province, and five drones across the country over Saturday and Sunday. At least 26 people have been killed in Houthi attacks on the kingdom since late September.

In the Strait of Hormuz, the day's one independently logged incident came from the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, which issued Warning 205-26: Indian shipping authorities reported an attack on a tanker sailing eastbound through the Gulf before it entered the strait, with all crew safe and the extent of damage and any environmental impact unknown. Hours later the Revolutionary Guards said two oil tankers on what they called an unauthorised route in the southern strait had hit naval mines, and that one — a supertanker carrying more than two million barrels of crude — had sunk. No vessel names, flags or owners were given, and no shipowner, flag state or naval force had confirmed a sinking by Sunday evening. US Central Command said on November 14 that more than 1.7 billion barrels of crude have moved through the strait under US military protection. The Guards' navy has declared the strait closed to any vessel that does not coordinate its passage with Tehran, while the US military says it has cleared mines from the main shipping routes.

Tehran's tone toward its neighbours also hardened. Foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said on Sunday that the Gulf states "must be careful not to reopen old wounds," referring to their support for Iraq in the 1980s war, after the Gulf Cooperation Council suggested Iran should pay its neighbours compensation for attacks on their territory. Baghaei was asked whether Iran would accept a temporary suspension of uranium enrichment in negotiations and answered: "No. We are not at that stage at all." Iran's parliament speaker, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, said it made no difference whether Washington wanted negotiations or war: "If the Americans make another mistake this time, the capabilities and will of Iran's armed forces will determine the geography of the battlefield." The GCC last week announced an advanced headquarters in Kuwait for its unified military command. Inside Iran, the economic squeeze is biting: long lines at gas stations, fuel shortages acknowledged by a vice president last week, and residents of Bandar Abbas blocking traffic on Thursday to protest.

Tehran's argument is that it is answering American military action, that the blockade is a direct threat to its economy and sovereignty, and that vessels sailing with tracking systems switched off on unauthorised routes are fair game. Washington's argument, as CNN framed it, is that the economic squeeze is working — Gulf oil exports are recovering, oil prices have fallen sharply from their peaks, and Iran has been unable to export any meaningful amount of its own crude for more than two months — so time is on America's side and Tehran will have to soften its terms. Riyadh and its coalition partners say the Houthi attacks are indiscriminate, aimed at airports, holy sites and civilian infrastructure, and that their interceptions are defensive. The Gulf states, for their part, have asked Trump to hold off broader strikes on Iran.

For ordinary people, the war is now a daily fact across the Gulf: Bahrain residents were woken by sirens and told to shelter, Riyadh faces regular missile alerts, Saudi airports remain disrupted, and shipping crews transit one of the world's most important oil chokepoints under threat of mines. In Yemen, about 330,000 people have been displaced by the renewed fighting, around half of them in Taiz, where aid cannot get in. Brent crude settled Friday at $95.84 a barrel; the weekend's claims had not yet been priced in when markets closed.

In short: Iran struck at a US base in Bahrain for the second time in three days and its Houthi allies now hold the whole Bab el-Mandab Strait, while Trump says he does not need a deal with Tehran and is content to let the blockade do the work — a calculation the next Iranian or Houthi attack will test again.

Tech products

Nvidia: comeback campaign grows — 160,000+ petition signatures, 7,500+ employees signed, but the sceptics are loud too

Follow-up to the 5 PM brief's article on Jensen Huang weighing a comeback. The "Stand With Jensen" petition passed 160,000 signatures on Sunday, including more than 86,000 people identifying as shareholders and over 7,500 who identified as Nvidia employees — about 18% of the workforce. Elon Musk, Epic Games chief Tim Sweeney and Y Combinator chief Garry Tan have signed. Deepwater's Gene Munster raised his odds of Huang returning to 51%, up from 10% on Friday. On the other side, SkyBridge founder Anthony Scaramucci said flatly "not happening," arguing this is not a sex scandal but an FBI counterintelligence review, DOJ chip-smuggling probes, a Wachtell investigation and a relationship with a direct report — "no board can reinstate a CEO it is still investigating." Analyst Dan Ives told Newsweek he sees "no chance Jensen comes back to Nvidia as CEO." Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent repeated his call for an outsider and a "dramatic shake-up," and Trump suggested former Apple CEO Tim Cook for the job. House China committee chair John Moolenaar said Huang and company representatives agreed to a voluntary closed-door interview, with dates still being discussed. Prediction markets are unconvinced: Kalshi traders gave Arm's Rene Haas a 24% chance of the job, Nvidia's Ian Buck 19% and Huang 13%. Nvidia reports earnings Tuesday, its first since Huang's resignation.

Sources: TradingView / Benzinga · Timeless.ai · Seoul Economic Daily · Stockbiz / AFP

Windows 11 now runs on the iPhone Duo — no jailbreak needed

Google AI Studio product lead Ammaar Reshi booted the full Arm version of Windows 11 on Apple's foldable iPhone Duo without jailbreaking it, then open-sourced the project, called WindowsiOS, on GitHub on November 15. The demo shows a retail copy of Windows 11 24H2 running Microsoft Edge and Google's Antigravity coding app with touch controls. It works by translating Windows' Arm code to run on the Duo's A20 Pro chip — hardware virtualization is off the table because Apple cut hypervisor access on stock iOS starting with iOS 16.4 — and each launch needs a debugger attached. Windows gets only 4.5GB of the Duo's 12GB of RAM, and setup needs an Apple Silicon Mac running Xcode plus your own Windows 11 Arm image. Fun proof of concept, not a daily driver.

Cybersecurity

CVE-2026-126173: AutomatorWP flaw lets anyone reset any WordPress admin password — no patch yet

A critical authorization bypass (CVSS 9.8) in the AutomatorWP Pro WordPress plugin's webhook addon, versions up to and including 1.4.4, lets an unauthenticated attacker reset the password of any WordPress account — including administrators — for full account takeover. It only works when an admin has configured a specific documented automation pairing a "Receive Webhook" trigger with an "Update-User" action, but that is a supported plugin configuration. A public exploit appeared on GitHub on November 14, the day the CVE was published. No fixed version is listed yet and it is not in CISA's KEV catalog. If you run AutomatorWP, disable that webhook-to-user-update automation until a patch lands.

Sources: Valters IT Hub · cve.now

Catch-up: DigiCert breach exposed customer identity documents — disclosed November 5

Certificate authority DigiCert told customers on November 5 that an internal business system archiving customer validation records had been exposed to the internet since a February 2025 migration moved it out of a VPN-restricted segment. A self-registered account without multi-factor authentication read part of the archive between June 27 and July 2, 2026; DigiCert only confirmed data access on October 19. Most of the data was business contact details, but a small minority of accounts also held identity documents submitted during certificate validation — passports, driver's licences or utility bills. No certificates were issued, changed or revoked, and DigiCert says its CA services, CertCentral and DigiCert ONE were not compromised. DigiCert has not said how many people are affected. If you submitted an ID document for a DigiCert certificate validation, treat it as exposed.

Sources: Zyphe · Cyber Breaches

No new CISA KEV additions; Check Point and UnrealIRCd federal deadline is Monday

CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog still stands at 1,781 entries with no new additions since Friday, November 13. The federal patch deadline for Check Point Gaia CVE-2026-123039 and UnrealIRCd CVE-2026-114713 is Monday, November 16.
