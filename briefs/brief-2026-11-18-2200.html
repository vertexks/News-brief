Anthropic just priced the biggest IPO in history — $83 a share, about $81 billion raised

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, priced its initial public offering at $83 a share on Wednesday evening, raising about $81.31 billion and eclipsing SpaceX's June offering to become the largest stock-market debut on record. The shares start trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday under the ticker ANT.

Reuters reports the offering was upsized by about 19% to 980 million shares, up from the planned 825 million at $70–$78 a share, after a roadshow that drew demand several times the shares on offer. On a fully diluted basis the IPO values Anthropic at about $1.5 trillion, Reuters reports; on a non-diluted basis the valuation is about $1.1 trillion. The company is also raising $26 billion through a private placement at the IPO price, with strategic investors including Google, Microsoft, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Micron and TSMC. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley are the lead underwriters. Le Monde, citing the Wall Street Journal, puts the share count slightly lower at 956 million shares and $79.3 billion raised — the price, the valuation and the record are not in dispute.

The numbers behind the valuation are striking and, in the other direction, sobering. Anthropic's annualised revenue run rate reached $81 billion as of October 31, up $10 billion in a single month, Reuters reports, driven by Claude Code, its coding agent. It had 11,900 customers generating at least $100,000 a year, up from more than 1,500 at the end of last year. Revenue rose 47% in the third quarter to $16.96 billion from the second quarter — but the company reported an operating loss of $567 million, after operating income of $974 million the previous quarter, and it spent nearly $9 billion on computing in the first half of 2026, nearly four times a year earlier, plus another $5 billion in the third quarter. It has committed to nearly $900 billion in computing spending with infrastructure partners including Amazon and Google, and its prospectus warns that some of those partners depend on third-party financing to buy chips and build data centres: "If such financing or support becomes unavailable or more costly, contracted capacity may be delayed, repriced, reduced, or not delivered."

What each side says: bulls argue the frontier is where the value accrues — Baillie Gifford's Peter Singlehurst, whose firm invested roughly $1.5 billion before the IPO, told Reuters Anthropic has "the highest probability of remaining at the frontier." Bears point to the spending: investors have grown increasingly concerned about debt-fuelled AI infrastructure expansion, and Anthropic's own filing warns its models could develop capabilities faster than it can evaluate them. The competitive picture is mixed — OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra accounted for about 13% of enterprise AI spending tracked by Ramp, against about 8% for Anthropic's Claude Fable, according to data cited by Reuters in September, though Anthropic's revenue run rate was ahead at the time. OpenAI is expected to pursue its own listing as early as next year. And Anthropic's own chief executive, Dario Amodei, has publicly called for the industry to slow the pace of releasing increasingly powerful models.

For ordinary people, the direct impact is mostly indirect: this is the first major frontier AI lab to go public, and how ANT trades on Thursday will be read as a verdict on whether the AI buildout — the data centres, the chips, the power — is a durable boom or an overextended bet. That matters because the same buildout is behind the chip sell-off, the power-grid strain and the jobs questions in the AI economy. Individual investors were supposed to get up to 20% of the shares offered.

In short: Anthropic priced above its range at $83, raised roughly $81 billion — the largest IPO ever — and debuts Thursday at a ~$1.5 trillion valuation, even as its own filings flag $900 billion in compute commitments and a return to operating losses. Watch how ANT trades on its first day, and whether it steadies or deepens the AI-stock sell-off.

Iran claims three more tankers hit mines on Wednesday evening — unverified, as the confirmed toll keeps rising

Iran's military claimed on Wednesday evening that three more oil tankers caught fire after striking naval mines east of the Strait of Hormuz, hours after a confirmed attack on a UAE-operated tanker left one crew member missing and 11 injured. No vessel was named, no flag or owner identified, and Reuters could not independently confirm the evening claim — but the pattern of the day points to a Gulf shipping war that is getting harder, not easier, to contain.

The confirmed incident is the attack on the Liberian-flagged tanker Navig8 Messi, operated by ADNOC Logistics & Services, the shipping arm of Abu Dhabi's state oil company. The vessel was struck by an unknown projectile about 13 nautical miles off Oman on Tuesday evening, according to the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre, and video shared on social media shows the ship listing as flames and black smoke pour into the sky. ADNOC confirmed on Wednesday that one crew member is missing, 11 were injured and receiving medical care, and the rest were evacuated. The New York Post reports it is the second time the Navig8 Messi has been attacked in four months, and that there have been some 18 confirmed attacks on commercial vessels in and around the strait this month alone. A second vessel, the Panamanian-flagged LPG tanker Tara Gas, was struck twice off Oman's Musandam Peninsula this week and abandoned; one Indian seafarer from that ship is also still missing, so two crew members from two ships are now unaccounted for.

Iran's account of what is happening differs sharply from the shipping record. The Revolutionary Guard and Iran's army have said tankers caught fire after hitting mines while sailing unauthorised routes with their tracking systems off, and on Wednesday evening Iran's army said three "violating" tankers had struck mines and that passage through the waterway "must be coordinated with Iranian authorities," according to reports carried by ANI and Al Jazeera. Independent monitors, however, have noted a pattern of projectile strikes, and the UKMTO reports describe projectiles, not mines. The gap between what Iran claims and what anyone can verify is itself the story: Reuters could not confirm the evening claim, and no shipowner, flag state or insurer has stepped forward to say one of its hulls was hit.

What each side says: Iran says its attacks — including the drone strikes on US facilities in Bahrain and Kuwait this week — are retaliation for US drone surveillance and the US strike on a Revolutionary Guard missile site on Qeshm Island on Tuesday, and for an attack on Iranian communications towers. Washington has confirmed the Qeshm strike as self-defence and says further strikes are "quite unlikely," while sending a third aircraft carrier and thousands more troops to the region. Analysts quoted by the Associated Press describe a deliberate, calibrated escalation: Hamidreza Azizi of the International Crisis Group wrote that "even if neither side intends to enter a broader confrontation, the accumulation of military incidents across multiple fronts increases the risk of miscalculation," while Danny Citrinowicz of Israel's Institute for National Security Studies said Iran's objective "is not necessarily to trigger an all-out war, but to steadily increase the military, economic, and political costs of maintaining the blockade." His conclusion: "As long as the U.S. naval blockade remains in place and no agreement is reached, further escalation will become increasingly difficult to avoid."

For ordinary people, the human cost is already concrete — two seafarers missing, 11 injured, crews abandoning burning ships — and the economic cost is creeping into fuel prices even as headline oil prices fall. Brent crude traded below $92 on Wednesday, down on a big US inventory build and the IEA's finding that global supply has surpassed demand, but shippers are paying more to move oil through the strait, and Iraq has resumed pumping oil to Turkey by pipeline specifically to avoid Hormuz. Mediation continues — Qatar says it met the Iranian side on Tuesday — but the fighting has now run for nine months with no agreement in sight.

In short: Iran claims three more tankers hit mines on Wednesday evening, but no one has independently confirmed it; what is confirmed is the ADNOC tanker strike that left one crew member missing and 11 hurt, plus a second missing seafarer from the Tara Gas. Analysts warn the pattern is deliberate escalation that risks a miscalculation. Watch whether any independent source confirms the evening mine claims, and whether Thursday's Shenzhen trilateral produces any movement on Iran.

The Houthis are now positioned to threaten Aden from two sides, analysts say

Houthi forces are likely attempting to capture Yemeni government positions on advantageous terrain near Jabal Kharaz, in a move that would set conditions to besiege and capture Aden, according to a new assessment from the Institute for the Study of War published on Wednesday. It is the sharpest warning yet that the southern port city — the seat of Yemen's internationally recognised government — is now the Houthis' objective, not just a distant threat.

Geolocated footage showed Houthi forces captured a Yemeni government defensive position east of Khour Umairah and south of Jabal Kharaz, along the Mokha–Aden road in Lahij governorate, on November 16, ISW reports. Houthi fighters separately engaged government-aligned forces in al-Maqatirah and Tur al-Bahah districts on November 17 and 18, suggesting they are simultaneously pressing Aden's northern and western flanks. By November 16 the Houthis had reached Khor Omairh, roughly 100 kilometres west of Aden, and fighting has resumed in Tur al-Bahah, described as Aden's first defensive line and about 80 kilometres from the city. No fighting inside Aden itself has been reported.

Caption: The port of Hodeidah on Yemen's Red Sea coast, in a 2021 file photo. Hodeidah is the Houthis' main port and was one of four governorates hit in the Saudi-led coalition's 60-target strike operation on Tuesday.

The advance follows a repositioning announced on November 15, when government and Southern forces withdrew from Dhubab airport and the Bab al-Mandeb area to defend Aden — a move they described as planned, but one that handed the Houthis the vacated coastal positions and consolidated their grip on the strategic strait. The Saudi-led coalition answered on Tuesday with a "precision operation" against 60 Houthi military targets in Sanaa, Hodeidah, Amran and Saada, including mountain tunnels used as workshops and storage for ballistic missiles and drone assembly facilities, according to coalition spokesman Maj. Gen. Turki al-Malki. The target count and claimed destruction have not been independently verified. Houthi health officials said at least 17 people were killed, including 15 in a strike on a sponge factory in Hodeidah, and spokesman Yahya Saree claimed 81 coalition strikes in 24 hours — none of it independently confirmed. The Houthis also kept up their own campaign against Saudi Arabia: ISW reports the group likely launched missiles and drones at Saudi critical infrastructure on November 17 and 18, and the coalition said it intercepted three Houthi cruise missiles and drones near Riyadh and one drone over southern Saudi Arabia.

What each side says: the coalition says its strikes are degrading Houthi missile and drone capabilities and neutralising the maritime threat. The Houthis say they are acting to compel Saudi Arabia to end military operations in Yemen and submit to their economic demands — ending Saudi support for the recognised government, lifting Saudi controls on Houthi-held ports and airports, and granting the Houthis control over Yemen's oil and gas resources, according to ISW. Meanwhile, further north, Taiz remains under siege: Al Jazeera's correspondent reported fierce fighting, villages on Jabal Sabr under complete siege, and Taiz authorities accusing the Houthis of blocking medicine shipments from Aden and confiscating supplies, with an appeal for UN intervention. The UN says more than 340,000 people have been displaced across Yemen since August.

For ordinary people, the stakes are immediate and grim. Aden is home to the government, the central bank and the main functioning port for the south; a siege would cut off food, fuel and medicine for hundreds of thousands of people, on top of a displacement crisis that has already uprooted more than 340,000 since August. In Taiz, civilians — mostly children and women, according to Al Jazeera's correspondent — are already under daily sniper fire. And the fighting is spilling into shipping: Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd have announced a conditional return of three Gemini services to the Suez route from December or January, a first test of whether Red Sea transits can resume despite the Houthi advance on Bab al-Mandeb.

In short: the Houthis have taken ground on both flanks of Aden and, in ISW's assessment, are positioning to besiege the city — and the coalition's 60-target air campaign has not stopped the advance. Watch whether government forces hold Tur al-Bahah, whether the Houthis push closer to Aden, and whether the Suez test sailings actually happen.

Nvidia posted a record quarter — and the stock fell anyway, for the fifth straight day

Nvidia shares closed at $150.96 on Wednesday, down 2.9%, marking a fifth consecutive session of declines despite the company reporting quarterly results and revenue guidance that beat analysts' estimates. The stock opened at $159.19, up 2.4% from Tuesday's close, touched $159.70 in the morning, then slid all day to a low of $150.40 on heavy volume of about 340 million shares — roughly 117% above its three-month average, according to the Motley Fool.

The results themselves were, by any normal measure, a blowout: revenue of $115.8 billion in the third quarter of fiscal 2027, up 103% from a year earlier, with adjusted earnings of $2.68 a share against a $2.47 consensus, and fourth-quarter guidance of $130 billion. The company also announced a further $150 billion in share buybacks. But the market's verdict was the opposite of the numbers. The stock is now about 38% below its October 6 record close of $243.37, down roughly 30% over the past month, and the average analyst price target fell to $251.74 on Wednesday from $265.70 on Monday, according to Reuters-cited data. The S&P 500 closed down 0.2% and the Nasdaq rose 0.4% on the day, so Nvidia's fall was its own story, not the market's.

What each side says: the bull case is the fundamentals — record revenue, record guidance, the Vera Rubin platform described by the company as its fastest ramp ever, and a $150 billion buyback. The bear case, which is winning for now, is everything around the numbers: succession uncertainty after Jensen Huang's ousting (the board has said it does not intend to reinstate him, and Huang is due before congressional committees on December 1), tighter US chip-export rules for data centres in Southeast Asia, and broader valuation anxiety about AI infrastructure spending — the same anxiety hanging over Anthropic's IPO, priced the same evening. The Motley Fool notes investors are weighing the CEO transition and China-related regulatory scrutiny; the Associated Press reports the stock initially rose 2.7% after the results before erasing its gains and ending lower.

For ordinary people, the direct effect is mostly through retirement accounts and index funds — Nvidia is one of the largest weights in the S&P 500 — but the wider signal matters more: the market is no longer rewarding AI companies for growth alone. It wants to see who pays for the data centres, who runs the companies, and whether the spending ever turns into durable profit. Anthropic's debut on Thursday will be the next test of that question.

In short: Nvidia beat on revenue, earnings and guidance — and fell 2.9% anyway, its fifth straight down day and roughly 38% off its October peak. The market is pricing succession, China rules and AI-spending doubts, not the quarter. Watch whether Anthropic's trading debut steadies the AI trade or deepens the slide.

"Google Releases New Gemini 4 Neon: What to Know" — Chiang Rai Times

Java4Shell: exploit attempts are now being seen, but no confirmed successes yet

"Java4Shell (CVE-2026-114938): OpenJDK RCE Vulnerability FAQ" — Tenable

The Java4Shell picture moved a step closer to real-world attacks on Wednesday. Tenable's updated FAQ reports that GreyNoise has seen exploitation attempts against CVE-2026-114938 but has not confirmed any successful exploitation against its sensors, while VulnCheck says a full exploit is available to its customers and Immersive's Kevin Breen reports building one. Talos and CERT-EU report no in-the-wild exploitation yet, though Talos anticipates probing soon, and SANS Internet Storm Center discussed the flaw — alongside a new NGINX flaw, below — without raising its alert level. The flaw is still not in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The fix remains the same: install your vendor's November Java update, or restart affected JVMs with the -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics flag. Cloudflare has also published WAF protection for the flaw.

NGINX has its own unauthenticated RCE flaw — "NGINX2Shell"

"10-Year-Old NGINX HTTP/2 Flaw Lets Unauthenticated Attackers Execute Code on Servers" — The Hacker News

A decade-old flaw in NGINX's HTTP/2 support, CVE-2026-123007, can let an attacker run code on servers with HTTP/2 enabled without logging in, The Hacker News reported on Wednesday. NGINX released version 1.31.9 today to fix it, and the flaw was found in part by an automated harness — Xint's tooling found four of the five NGINX flaws disclosed this week, including CVE-2026-123007. Talos anticipates probing soon, though no in-the-wild exploitation has been reported. If you run NGINX with HTTP/2, update.

"Critical Apache Roller RCE Flaw Under Active Exploitation, No Authentication Required" — The Hacker News

Apache Roller has an unauthenticated deserialization flaw, CVE-2026-129448 (CVSS 9.8), that is under active exploitation, The Hacker News reported on Wednesday — patch or take it offline if you run it. Old Windows bug let a threat actor steal data from over a million payment cards

"Old Windows bug enables threat actor to steal data from over 1 million credit, debit cards" — Neowin

Cybersecurity researchers at Cybernews found an exposed server belonging to a threat actor attacking point-of-sale systems, Neowin reported on Wednesday. The campaign compromised more than 700 POS terminals across more than 20 countries and exposed at least one million credit and debit card records. The attacker exploited a privilege-escalation flaw in the Windows Common Log File System (CLFS) driver that Microsoft patched almost two years ago — but many POS terminals were never updated, because retailers worried about downtime and compatibility or simply did not prioritise patching. A reminder that the patch you skip is the one that gets used.

Source: Neowin

France is blaming Iran for the supermarket fridge cyberattack

"France Is Blaming Iran for the Fridge Cyberattack" — Schneier on Security

France is blaming Iran for the large-scale cyberattack on French supermarket refrigeration systems reported last month, according to Schneier on Security. The attack forced Danfoss controllers into defrost, reportedly spoiling food across around 150 Intermarché stores and dozens more, and French investigators had been hunting for who was behind it with no claim of responsibility. If the attribution holds, it would tie the incident directly to the Iran conflict rather than to a lone hacker or criminal group, as earlier reporting had suggested. "Russell Brand Faces Ban on Speaking to His Wife From Prison" — Parade
