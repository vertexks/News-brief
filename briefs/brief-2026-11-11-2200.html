Nvidia's board met late Wednesday to decide Jensen Huang's future — still no decision announced

Nvidia's board met late on Wednesday night to decide whether to oust founder and chief executive Jensen Huang, Reuters reported, citing a source familiar with the matter. CBS News, citing two sources, also reported the board is preparing to remove him soon. As of late Wednesday night there was still no decision announced, no interim chief named, and no company statement — Huang remains CEO. Bloomberg reported the board has been working with an outside firm to identify potential replacements, and is weighing whether to name an interim chief while it searches.

The meeting marks a sharp turn from Sunday, when the board said it had confidence in Huang and his leadership team. Under Nvidia's bylaws Huang can be removed "with or without cause" by a majority vote of the directors — there are 11, and all but Huang are independent. The Wall Street Journal first reported on Wednesday that the board was preparing to oust him, hours after The New York Times reported a second affair, with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of health care, who reports to Huang. The first affair, with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom US officials believe was recruited by Chinese intelligence, was admitted by Huang in a staff memo on Sunday.

What each side is saying has not changed much, but the market has made up its mind about the direction of travel. Nvidia's shares fell 8.6% in after-hours trading to $158.84, and Reuters reports that if those losses hold, the company will have lost about $600 billion in market value since the board announced its investigation. "The market is starting to price Nvidia without its founder as the likely outcome, not a tail risk," portfolio manager Armina Rosenberg of Minotaur Capital told Reuters. "Losing Jensen would be a real blow." Prediction market Polymarket moved to an 88% chance Huang resigns or is removed by the end of the year, with odds for a successor favouring Ian Buck, who runs the data-centre business. Rosenberg said chief financial officer Colette Kress, in the role for more than a decade, would be the natural interim choice — but the harder question is who leads the technology roadmap longer term, since Huang, 63, has no obvious successor, owns about 3.6% of the company, and has dozens of direct reports. The fallout spread wider: Nasdaq futures sank 1%, Micron fell nearly 2% and CoreWeave dropped 6% after hours, while rivals AMD and Broadcom rose 4% and 3%.

One new strand landed on Wednesday: Axios reported, citing high-level sources, that US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick stalled President Trump's crackdown on companies suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China. The White House and a spokesperson for Lutnick did not immediately respond. Trump has said he has not discussed the allegations with Huang but has voiced support for him. The congressional clock is still running too — the House chairs' 5 p.m. ET deadline to agree to a closed-door briefing on November 18 passed with a subpoena threat on the table, as covered in this evening's brief.

For ordinary people the impact is still mostly market, not product: no interruption to GeForce drivers, product shipments or developer programs has been announced, and demand for Nvidia's chips is not expected to drop in the near term. But analysts warned the wider universe of companies Nvidia finances — largely customers building data centres that will use its chips — could suffer if the turmoil drags on. "In these types of booms or bubbles, sometimes all it takes is a slowdown to shift it into the bust phase," Nick Ferres of Vantage Point Asset Management told Reuters. The next hard dates are earnings on November 17 and the requested Capitol Hill briefing on November 18.

In short: the board met late Wednesday to decide Huang's future, with CBS confirming the ouster preparation and Bloomberg reporting a successor search is under way — but nothing has been announced yet, and Huang is still CEO. The market is now pricing in his departure.

Iran struck navigation systems on Oman's Didamar Island — the first attack on Omani soil in the Hormuz campaign

Iran has attacked ship navigation systems on Didamar, a small Omani island also known as Little Quoin that sits beside the southern traffic corridor of the Strait of Hormuz, three sources with knowledge of the matter confirmed to Reuters on Wednesday. Iranian state media said on Tuesday that the Revolutionary Guards' navy carried out strikes destroying "enemy navigation systems" on the island. The Institute for the Study of War identified the attack as having happened on November 8, before Iranian state media claimed responsibility on November 10.

The attack itself is confirmed; the damage is not. The three sources gave Reuters no damage assessment, and Reuters said it could not determine independently whether any navigation systems were destroyed. Reuters did verify video showing smoke rising from the island, and imagery from the EU's Copernicus Sentinel-2 satellite showed scorch marks and thermal activity near the radar and radio equipment. The Institute for the Study of War likewise questioned the extent of the reported damage, noting that commercial vessels kept using the southern route after the attack.

What each side says: Iranian state television said the personnel and equipment on the island were "helping the flow of oil smuggling by the enemy" — meaning the tankers that have kept using the southern route with American support. A Guards navy official added that no vessel Iran considers unauthorised had passed through the route overnight, and a separate Iranian statement blamed the destruction of what it called US coastal radar and navigation aids for raising the risk to ships using illegal passages. Shipping and maritime-security sources told Reuters the strike would likely have only limited impact on commercial traffic, because other navigation systems are in place elsewhere. Neither US Central Command nor Oman's media ministry immediately responded to requests for comment.

The human impact runs through oil and shipping costs rather than the island itself — Didamar is uninhabited rock with a lighthouse dating to 1914 and shore radar and radio gear used to track vessels and coordinate traffic at the entrance to the Gulf. The island belongs to Oman, which makes the strike politically sensitive: Oman's foreign minister discussed joint mechanisms for the strait with Iran's foreign minister as recently as November 5, and Oman has been a mediator in the conflict. The attack followed US strikes on Iran's Qeshm Island on November 2. It also comes as Gulf oil exports have recovered to more than 80% of pre-war levels in September and October, helped by US military-assisted flows through the strait and higher Saudi exports via the Red Sea port of Yanbu — the recovery Iran's campaign is trying to break.

In short: Iran hit navigation gear on an Omani island in the strait on November 8, the first strike on Omani soil in this campaign. The attack is confirmed by three sources to Reuters, but whether the systems were actually destroyed is still unclear, and shipping sources expect limited disruption.

Russell Brand jailed for ten and a half years — catch-up from Monday that earlier briefs missed

Russell Brand was sentenced on Monday, November 9, at Southwark Crown Court in London to ten years and six months in prison, after a jury convicted him last Thursday of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault against four women, for offences between 2004 and 2009. He was acquitted of two further counts of rape and one count of attempted indecent assault. He must serve two-thirds of the sentence — around seven years — before he can be released on licence, and will be on the sex offenders' register for life.

The sentence breaks down as nine years for the rape of a 21-year-old fan in 2009, after meeting her at a stand-up show at the Hammersmith Apollo, plus 18 months, nine months and eight months for the three sexual assaults, running concurrently with each other but consecutively to the rape sentence. The judge, Mr Justice Bennathan, told Brand he had become "a sexual predator" as he was becoming famous, and that he had displayed "staggering arrogance" and "an arrogant disregard for a woman's right to say no". The judge said Brand's fame had protected him until 2023, when a joint investigation by The Times, The Sunday Times and Channel 4's Dispatches prompted women to come forward. The Metropolitan Police investigation, Operation Actinolite, involved more than 100 witnesses and over 1,400 documents.

Brand, 51, has denied all the charges throughout and maintains he never had sex without consent. His wife Laura Gallacher, with whom he has three children, was in court for the sentencing and, according to his defence barrister Ian Winter KC, has no intention of leaving him; Brand mouthed "I love you" to her as he left the dock, the BBC reported. Outside the courtroom, the verdict has split opinion: commentators Megyn Kelly and Elon Musk have publicly questioned the trial's integrity, calling it a "political witch hunt", while the judge's sentencing remarks stressed the significant and lasting harm suffered by the victims.

The case is not fully closed. The Metropolitan Police have confirmed Brand remains under investigation over two further rape allegations, and detectives are assessing additional reports received since the conviction. Page Six reported on Tuesday that a new accuser in the United States has come forward and is in contact with attorney Gloria Allred — that report is single-source and unconfirmed. Brand is reported to be considering an appeal, though legal observers cited by the Daily Mail suggested the jury's mixed verdicts could make a successful challenge difficult.

In short: Brand was jailed for ten and a half years on Monday for one rape and three sexual assaults, with the judge calling him a "sexual predator" whose fame shielded him — and police are still investigating two further rape allegations against him.

Cyber

Maersk confirms data breach as WhiteArmor ransomware gang demands $5 million

Shipping giant Maersk confirmed on Wednesday that an attacker gained unauthorised access to a local legacy system inherited through an earlier acquisition and extracted data. The company said the incident is isolated, contained, and that it expects no disruption to operations, with no indication of impact on other Maersk systems or on customer and partner systems connected via APIs and EDI. It did not name the system, the type of data taken, or when the intrusion happened. The WhiteArmor ransomware gang listed Maersk on its leak site over the weekend and is demanding $5 million in bitcoin within 30 days, claiming about 100GB stolen from the OCF/LF Logistics platform — the system of LF Logistics, which Maersk acquired in 2022 — including nearly 30,000 driver records. The gang also claimed plaintext passwords were taken; Maersk told Cybernews that passwords are stored in protected form and that what the attackers stole were single-use codes that have since expired. Maersk confirmed to Cybernews that its breach is the same incident WhiteArmor claims, but has not confirmed the gang's data claims. (BleepingComputer · Cybernews · Neowin)

N0n ransomware group claims DraftKings and LiveRamp data exposures (unverified)

The N0n ransomware group listed two big names on November 11, both so far only tracker reports with no company confirmation. DraftKings: a leak marked "NOT PAID — EXPOSED" containing 6.3 million mobile device advertising identifiers from 2020 and 7.4 million hashed consumer email addresses from 2020, plus campaign demographic analytics from November 2019. LiveRamp: 39,055 files totalling 2.2 terabytes, including identity graph feeds and 19.2 million hashed email identities the group says are resolved to named individuals, dated October 2026. Both are claims from ransomware trackers, not confirmed breaches — the companies have not commented. (QPulse — DraftKings · DeXpose — LiveRamp)
