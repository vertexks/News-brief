Morning brief — Sunday, November 8, 2026

Russia hits Ukraine's grid for a second straight night as talks near

Russia kept hammering Ukraine's energy system overnight into Sunday, launching three guided missiles and 164 drones — 112 of them jet-powered — at targets across the country. Ukraine's air force said it shot down or suppressed 137 of the drones by 8 a.m., with hits recorded at 23 locations and debris falling at six more.

The damage is piling up on top of Saturday's much heavier barrage of 36 ballistic missiles and 172 drones. DTEK, Ukraine's largest private energy company, said the overnight attack left more than 600,000 people without power in the eastern Dnipropetrovsk region, including the districts of Pavlohrad, Samar and Kamianske and the city of Dnipro itself. The company said the strikes damaged one of its facilities in the Kyiv region and substations run by the national grid operator Ukrenergo — the substations that carry electricity from nuclear power plants to Kyiv, the surrounding region and central Ukraine. Emergency power cuts are in place in Kyiv, Kharkiv, Sumy and Poltava, with scheduled outages across most other regions. A 75-year-old man injured in a drone strike on Brovary, near Kyiv, died in hospital overnight.

Russia's defence ministry said on Sunday its forces had also hit a power substation in the Zhytomyr region and a container ship south-west of Odesa, claiming both supported Ukrainian military operations. A Russian regional governor said one person was killed in a Ukrainian drone attack on the Kursk region, after a drone hit a tractor in the village of Pervomayskoe.

Zelenskyy said in his nightly address that only four of Saturday's 36 ballistic missiles were intercepted, and blamed a shortage of US-made Patriot interceptors. Six ballistic missiles were fired at Kyiv's Northern Bridge, he said, and four struck it; the bridge is closed for repairs. Over the past week, he said, Russia launched 115 missiles, nearly 1,900 guided bombs and more than 3,000 attack drones at Ukrainian cities. He added that some of Saturday's missiles were North Korean-made KN-23s.

Kyiv's argument is that it cannot stop the ballistic campaign without more interceptors, and that Washington is best placed to help — the US approved a $2.1 billion Patriot sale last month, but those missiles are not yet manufactured and are not expected to arrive until well into the 2030s, so Kyiv is pressing the Pentagon to hand over missiles from its own stocks now. Moscow's line is that it is striking military-industrial, energy and transport targets, not civilians. Washington, meanwhile, is still talking up a deal: Trump met Putin's envoy Kirill Dmitriev at the White House on Thursday and said a "big agreement" was coming, and negotiators from Russia, Ukraine and the United States are due to meet in Abu Dhabi on Tuesday, November 10–11.

For ordinary Ukrainians the timing is the worst part. Temperatures in Kyiv are near freezing, and the strikes are landing on heating, water and power just as winter sets in. Kyiv's water supply was only just restored after its worst outage of the war, and repair crews are now working around the clock while the grid is hit again night after night.

In short: Russia's energy campaign is escalating into a second consecutive night of heavy strikes, with more than 600,000 people cut off in one region alone and Kyiv's defences running short of the interceptors that stop ballistic missiles — three days before the next round of talks in Abu Dhabi.

Mystery blasts on Qeshm Island as both sides report ships hit in the Gulf

Explosions were heard on Saturday evening on Qeshm Island and in Jask, Sirik and Bandar Abbas in southern Iran, and nobody has clearly said what caused them. Iranian state media reported the blasts, and the semi-official Tasnim news agency said two missiles had struck Qeshm, with no official information on possible damage. Iranian state television, though, suggested the explosions might be related to Iranian military activity in the waterway rather than US strikes, and said officials had not confirmed any missile strikes on Iranian territory. Neither Iran nor the United States has commented on the Tasnim report.

The blasts came a day after President Trump confirmed a limited US strike on Friday on an Iranian air-defence system on Qeshm, which he called "minor" and said took about 15 minutes. Washington said on November 2 that it had struck the island in a limited defensive measure after Iranians used surface-to-air missiles against US assets.

Both sides also reported hitting ships over the weekend. Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said two oil tankers had caught fire after hitting mines while trying to pass through an "unauthorized route" in the Strait of Hormuz, and the semi-official Fars news agency later reported a third tanker had suffered an explosion and caught fire. The US military said on Saturday evening that its air force had fired at the stern of a commercial vessel in the Gulf of Oman, disabling it as it tried to get through the blockade. UK monitors reported a separate incident involving a bulk carrier and military forces about 18 nautical miles east of Khor Fakkan in the UAE, with damage unknown. Reuters said it was not able to confirm any of the reported strikes.

Tehran's position, repeated by its officials this week, is that the strait stays closed until Washington meets all seven of its conditions — sanctions relief, access to frozen assets and war reparations among them — while Washington insists nuclear issues must feature in the first talks. Iran has submitted a nuclear proposal to Washington through Qatar, a senior Iranian official told Reuters on Thursday, saying the US was reviewing it. Qatar, which is mediating, denied reports on Saturday that the US and Iran had failed to reach a deal; its prime minister said both sides were continuing to work toward an agreement and that "significant progress" had been made. On Saturday the US military also announced a new task force to crack down on weapons and sanctioned material reaching Iran by land and across the Caspian Sea; the Revolutionary Guards dismissed it as "wordplay".

For the world economy the shipping war is the part that bites. UK maritime monitors said at least 11 ships had been attacked in and around the strait over the preceding week, with three sinking. Brent crude settled around $98 on Friday after a roughly 12% weekly drop, and flows through the strait are running at under 60% of capacity, with US diesel reserves below 100 million barrels for the first time in more than 20 years.

In short: a day after the US confirmed a strike on Qeshm, new explosions there have no clear cause and no one claiming them, both sides say they hit ships in the Gulf, and Qatar insists mediation is still alive — with Monday's new moon, a dark night favourable for military operations, now the next watch point.

Houthis hit Riyadh again; Aramco building damaged, Yemen toll rises to 33

Houthi forces claimed six missile and drone operations against Saudi Arabia on Saturday, saying they targeted King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, Aramco oil facilities in Riyadh and Khurais, a National Guard camp in Najran, and military positions in Marib and al-Jawf. The group said the attacks were retaliation for Saudi airstrikes in Yemen. Saudi Arabia did not confirm the Houthi claims of successful hits.

What Saudi Arabia did confirm is damage in its own capital. The energy ministry said on Sunday that falling debris caused limited damage to an administrative building belonging to state oil company Saudi Aramco in Riyadh's King Abdullah Financial District, with no injuries or fatalities. A Reuters witness reported shattered windows across several floors of the Aramco Tower. Coalition spokesman Major General Turki al-Malki said air defences intercepted two ballistic missiles launched toward Riyadh, along with two cruise missiles targeting the capital and the southern region, plus a ballistic missile heading toward the Eastern Province and a drone targeting the south.

Meanwhile the death toll from Saturday's strikes on al-Shariyah village, east of Sanaa, has risen to 33 killed — including 13 children and eight women — and 39 wounded, according to the Houthi-run health ministry, which called the toll near-final. The Houthis blame Saudi airstrikes that they say hit homes and rescue teams; Saudi Arabia's Center for International Communication said it was investigating the report and had no immediate public response. Reuters could not independently verify the account. It is one of the deadliest reported attacks since fighting between Saudi Arabia and the Iran-aligned group resumed in July, shattering a 2022 UN-brokered truce.

The blame game is just as unresolved over Friday's attack on al-Hadara market in Taiz, which Houthi health officials say killed 30 people and wounded eight. The Houthis accuse Saudi aircraft of three airstrikes on the crowded market; the Yemeni government and local Taiz authorities say a Houthi ballistic missile hit the market and deny any airstrikes took place in the district that day. Neither account has been independently verified. Sources told South24 that more than ten coalition airstrikes hit Houthi positions along the Taiz–Lahj fronts on Saturday night.

The Houthis say they are hitting Saudi airports, oil facilities and military sites until the airstrikes and blockade end, and they have again warned airlines to avoid Saudi airspace. Riyadh says its defences are intercepting the attacks and that it is striking Houthi launchers and infrastructure in Yemen. The UN says more than 1,600 people have been killed and more than 300,000 displaced since major fighting resumed.

In short: the Houthis struck at Riyadh and Aramco facilities again on Saturday, debris damaged an Aramco building in the capital, and the reported civilian toll in Yemen climbed to 33 in one village — with both sides still blaming each other for the market attack in Taiz and no independent verification of either claim.

Krakow: the three bodies at the gunman's home were his parents and sister

Polish police confirmed on Sunday morning that the three people found dead at the Krakow gunman's family home are his parents and his sister. The bodies — a 53-year-old father, a 52-year-old mother and a 20-year-old sister, all with apparent gunshot wounds — were found on Saturday evening at the family apartment in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski, about 150 kilometres north-east of Krakow. Police have not confirmed whether the suspect killed them before travelling to Krakow, and post-mortems are under way to establish how and when they died.

The suspect has been named by prosecutors as Michał O., a 24-year-old Polish national from Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski with no criminal record and no previous contact with law enforcement. On Saturday at about 3 p.m. he drove into Krakow's Main Market Square and opened fire with an AR-15 rifle from his car, killing six people — four women and two men, including a British citizen — and wounding 15 others. Police confronted him within about three minutes of the first report and arrested him a few streets away after a shootout lasting around 20 minutes; two officers were wounded, one critically. The suspect was seriously wounded in the exchange of gunfire, underwent several operations overnight and remains in critical condition, so prosecutors have not yet been able to question him or formally present charges. Police recovered seven firearms from the family apartment and are investigating their ownership; Polish media report the weapons belonged to his father, who was registered at a sport shooting club.

Prosecutors announced on Sunday that they are investigating the shooting as an act of a "terrorist nature", working with the Internal Security Agency. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński said preliminary findings pointed to a young man "fascinated by far-right ideologies" with radical ultranationalist views, and the Central Bureau for Combating Cybercrime has joined the investigation to examine possible links to radical groups online. No motive has been established, and no accomplices or organisational links have been confirmed. Disinformation spread quickly overnight: social media accounts tried to link the gunman to Ukraine, the United States or the Polish left, claims the authorities and the press have refuted. The defence ministry confirmed he was not a soldier, despite the military-style clothing he wore.

Prime Minister Donald Tusk, who travelled to Krakow on Saturday evening, called it terrorism and said: "Until now, Poland had never experienced a terrorist incident this serious." Former president Bronisław Komorowski called it the first time in Poland that an attack had been carried out with a long automatic weapon with such dramatic consequences. The shooting follows a rise in violence in Poland — three attacks on schools between late September and early October, and an October 11 plot against Tusk and his defence minister that police foiled — and it lands in the middle of a bitter political standoff over the Constitutional Tribunal, with the nationalist right accusing the government of repression and the government accusing its critics of exploiting the tragedy. Tusk appealed to political leaders "not to use this tragedy for your own ends."

Poland is in national mourning on Sunday and Monday, with Krakow's mourning running through November 11. The first test of whether the calls for unity hold comes on Wednesday, Poland's national holiday, when the annual independence march traditionally brings the radical right onto the streets.

In short: the Krakow attack is now a nine-dead case — six people shot in the square and the gunman's parents and sister found dead at home — with the suspect in critical condition, no motive established, and prosecutors treating it as a suspected terrorist offence as Poland begins two days of national mourning.

Ai2 ToyBlocks Launches Open Robot-Learning Simulation With 85,336 Demonstrations

The Allen Institute for AI released ToyBlocks on November 6, a controlled tabletop robotics framework with 85,336 demonstrations and about 1,600 hours of simulated data, built for reproducible evaluation of robot policies. It matters because robot learning has had a reproducibility problem — everyone trains on different setups — and a shared, open benchmark makes results comparable. (SXF Brief)

Cybersecurity

GPT-6 Cyber: OpenAI's Frontier of "Aligned" Security Agents

OpenAI launched GPT-6 Cyber on November 5, a version of its GPT-6 Astra model trained specifically for security research: finding flaws, building working exploits and writing patches, all inside authorised scope. It scores 48.5% on the ExploitGym benchmark, up from 32.1% for its predecessor, and OpenAI says it produced working exploits for 948 Linux privilege-escalation flaws and found 81 previously unknown high-severity bugs in Firefox — Mozilla has already shipped fixes for many of them. The model is rated "Critical" for cyber capability under OpenAI's own framework, and it is gated to vetted researchers in the Daybreak Red programme, not available to ordinary users. Why it matters: this is the model behind the Brotli RCE (CVE-2026-111497) finding, and it shows AI-written exploit research is now operating at industrial scale — defenders get the bugs found faster, but the same capability in the wrong hands is the threat. (Cyber Magazine)

Fake GitHub tools backdoor security teams

A campaign pushing fake security utilities on GitHub has compromised more than 2,000 Windows systems with backdoors and information stealers, according to Cyware's November 7 threat briefing. The malicious packages were published as version 1.0.1 on November 1, flagged as malware 70 seconds later and removed by 09:13 UTC — but the window was enough. Why it matters: the people being targeted are security professionals themselves, and the lesson is the same one as the LiteLLM and OVHcloud hijacks — verify what you install, even when it looks like a security tool. (Cyware)

Israel: Eisenkot gets the mandate today

President Herzog selected Gadi Eisenkot on Saturday evening to try to form a government, and the formal mandate ceremony is today, Sunday, at 5 p.m. Eisenkot has 28 days, plus a possible 14-day extension. He has 67 recommendations against Netanyahu's 46, but his core bloc is 48 seats and he needs 61 — with both Arab parties' 19 recommendations not translating into coalition support, and Lieberman opposed to any government dependent on them. The Knesset is sworn in on Tuesday, November 10. (Objective Daily News overnight watch)
